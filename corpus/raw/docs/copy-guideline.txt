About This Document
These guidelines synthesize the teaching frameworks of two of the most rigorous schools of
DTC copy thinking: Nik Sharma's Limited Supply podcast and Sharma Brands writing on landing
pages and PDPs, and Common Thread Collective's Ecommerce Playbook on creative strategy,
persona development, and performance copy. They have been applied specifically to
Threaditate's brand voice, three core purchase motivations, and its cold DTC buyer challenge.
This is not a style guide for fonts. It is an operating system for every word on your site.
Part 1: Brand Voice & Copy Principles
1.1 The Threaditate Voice
Threaditate's copy voice is calm confidence. Not hype. Not clinical. Not hushed wellness-brand
whisper. It knows something the reader hasn't figured out yet — that there is a mode of making
that slows your mind down rather than filling it up — and it shares that knowledge with quiet
authority.
Four words that define the voice:
• Assured — We don't hedge or soften. We state.
• Textured — The copy has specificity. Real words, not category words.
• Unhurried — Sentences breathe. We never chase.
• Grounded — The experience is physical, tactile, real. Copy should feel that way.
1.2 The Three Tests (Nik Sharma Frameworks)
Every headline, subhead, and product description must pass these three filters before it goes
live:
The Grandma Test

The One-Sentence Why Test
Rule
Brain-dump everything you want to say about Threaditate. Then distill it to one sentence that
captures the "why" someone should care. Every page should have this sentence as its north star.
Right now, that sentence is: "The only craft kit designed to still your mind while your hands make
something real."
1.3 The CTC Emotional Truth → Rational Justification Sequence
Common Thread Collective's core creative doctrine, drawn from Taylor Holiday's study of Ogilvy
and Bernbach: great copy sells on emotion first, then justifies the decision with reason. The
emotional truth is what makes someone lean forward. The rational justification is what lets them
click buy without feeling foolish.
For Threaditate, the sequence is:
EMOTIONAL TRUTH: "I want to feel like myself again — slower, more present, making something
that matters." RATIONAL JUSTIFICATION: "The guided app does the hard part. I just string. Even
my first attempt looks good."
Every section of your landing page and PDP should deliver one or the other — and they should
appear in this order. Emotional truth opens. Rational justification closes.

Part 2: Landing Page Copy Guidelines
2.1 The Module-First Approach
Nik Sharma teaches landing pages as a system of modular sections — each one answerable to:
"Does this module earn the visitor's next scroll?" Think of it not as a page but as a sequence of
decisions. Each module must win its moment or the visitor drops.
The landing page should be built mobile-first, every time. Most of Threaditate's paid traffic will
arrive via mobile. Design every word for a 390px screen first, then expand to desktop. Every
section title must work as a standalone sentence at 16px.
2.2 Section-by-Section Copy Directives
Module 1: Trust Bar (Pre-Hero)
A slim bar above the hero — one line, three trust signals. Fast, factual, no fluff.

• Keep under 8 words per element

• Match ad source: if ad is gifting-focused, lead with the guarantee; if mindfulness-

Module 2: Hero Section
This is the most important copy on the page. Nik Sharma's rule: the hero must answer What's
being sold, Why care, and How to get it — without scrolling. On mobile, all three must be legible
without pinching.
Element Direction
Headline One line. Leads with emotional truth. Max 7 words. No punctuation except a period if
needed for rhythm. Tests to run: "String art, guided by your phone." / "Something
beautiful. Made by you." / "45 minutes. No noise. Something real."
Subhead One to two sentences. Introduces the rational justification. Answers: what it is, how it
works, what you get. Example direction: "Threaditate converts any photo into real-time,
voice-guided string art instructions. Follow the app. Finish something that matters."
CTA Button "Get My Kit" outperforms "Shop Now" in gift and hobby categories. Test "Start Making"
for the mindfulness segment. Avoid generic e-commerce language ("Add to Cart" can
wait for the PDP).
Hero Visual Lifestyle photo of a completed piece being held, not the kit box. The person's face
should not be the focus — the work is the focus. Secondary: hands mid-session with
thread. Product shot lives in the PDP.
Module 3: Social Proof / UGC Strip
Nik Sharma's most repeated landing page note: social proof almost never goes where brands
put it. Most brands bury it. It should appear immediately below the hero — not at the bottom, not
in a tab, not after the feature list.
Copy Direction
Pull the three most specific, emotional backer quotes. Specificity is social proof. "I cried when I
finished" beats "This is amazing." "I couldn't stop until I was done" beats "Great product." Find the
quotes that describe the experience, not the quality. Stack three horizontally on desktop, one at a
time as a carousel on mobile. Star rating + backer count lives above the quotes.
• Never use your own words as social proof — only verbatim quotes with attribution
• Pull through backer quote language into your own copy where authentic (if 40 backers
said "meditative" — that word should appear in your copy)
• Test: a single hero quote above the fold vs. a star-rating strip. Both approaches work;
the question is which segment your traffic is in.

Module 4: How It Works
This module is purely rational justification. Three steps. No more. This is where cold DTC
buyers who almost converted but didn't lose their nerve. Make the process feel inevitable and
approachable.
Copy Direction
Three numbered steps. Each has a short headline + one sentence. Do not use "simply" or "just" —
they patronize. Example framework: 1. PHOTOGRAPH ANYTHING — Upload any photo in the app.
A face, a skyline, a pet. The app converts it instantly. 2. FOLLOW THE GUIDE — Voice instructions
and on-screen markers walk you through every string, one step at a time. No guessing. No wrong
moves. 3. FINISH SOMETHING REAL — Your completed piece is displayable. Frame it, gift it, or
keep it. It will look like you made it. Because you did.
• Present tense, active voice — always
• Second-person ("you") throughout — never third-person or "users"
• The word "easy" is banned — show ease through specificity, don't claim it

Module 6: The Positioning Statement (Comparison)
Threaditate's core strategic position must appear somewhere on the landing page — ideally just
below the feature block. This is the competitive moat, stated plainly. Nik Sharma calls this the
comparison module; CTC calls it the "honest differentiation" section. It should feel factual, not
defensive.
Copy Direction
Use a comparison table or a single bold statement. Avoid naming competitors directly on the main
LP. Lead with the category truth instead: "Every other string art kit gives you a pattern. Threaditate
gives you a guide. The difference is whether you finish — and whether it looks like yours." Or as a

Module 7: The Offer & CTA Block
This is the rational close. What does the customer get? At what price? What removes their risk?
Nik Sharma's rule: minimize clicks to conversion. This module should give visitors everything
they need to decide — without making them hunt for it.
• Lead with what's in the box (physical kit + app access) — bundle clarity reduces cart
abandonment
• Price should appear clearly — no tricks, no "starting at" language
• Guarantee copy: "If it's not what you expected, return it" is stronger than "30-day money-
back guarantee." Specificity reassures.
• Shipping language: "Ships within X days" outperforms "free shipping" when conversion
is the goal — certainty > savings for gift and first-purchase buyers
Module 8: FAQ
Every unanswered question is a lost sale. FAQs on the landing page serve cold DTC buyers —
people who don't trust yet. CTC's research doctrine: run customer interviews or pull Kickstarter
comment questions and answer those exactly, in the customer's language.
• Do I need the app to use the kit? → Honest answer. Yes and no — frame it as "the app
is what makes it guided. Without it, it's string art. With it, it's Threaditate."
• What skill level do I need? → None. Emphasize specifically.
• What photos work best? → Specific examples build confidence: faces, pets, skylines,
silhouettes.
• How long does it take? → Real range, not "it depends." 45 minutes to 3 hours for most
designs.
• Is this a good gift? → Yes, with specificity about who it's for.
Part 3: Product Detail Page (PDP) Copy Guidelines
3.1 The 85% Rule
Nik Sharma's most important PDP statistic: 85% of visitors will not scroll below the fold. This is
not a reason to cram everything above the fold — it is a mandate to be ruthlessly selective
about what earns prime position. The above-fold zone must answer three and only three things:
What you're selling, what the customer gets from it, and how they can have it.
Design Consequence
On mobile, the PDP above-the-fold zone is approximately 600px tall. After the product title, price, and
primary image carousel, you have room for approximately two lines of benefit copy and one CTA.

3.3 The Image Carousel — Copy Is Visual
Nik Sharma is explicit: lifestyle photography sells more than product shots alone. The image
carousel is copy. Each image answers a customer question:
• Image 1: Completed piece — beauty, the end state. This is the desire.
• Image 2: Kit contents laid flat — answers "what do I get?"
• Image 3: Hands in session — answers "can I actually do this?"
• Image 4: App screen in use — answers "how does the app work?"
• Image 5 (optional): Close-up of completed string detail — reinforces quality
Every image should have alt text that serves SEO. Alt text format: "[descriptive phrase] —
Threaditate guided string art kit."
3.4 Below the Fold — Educate to Convert
Below the fold is for the browser-not-yet-buyer. These are people who liked what they saw but
aren't convinced yet. Nik Sharma's framing: this section closes the deal. Use it to answer the
objections that stopped them from clicking Add to Cart the first time.

Sticky Navigation Bar
Non-negotiable. A bar that follows the visitor down the page with the product name, price, and
Add to Cart button. This should appear as soon as the original CTA scrolls out of view. Without

In-Depth Description Block
This is the full narrative. Write it in second person. Answer three questions: 1. What is this
product specifically? 2. Why is it different from every other string art option? 3. How do you use
it, and what happens if you've never done this before? Target: 100–180 words. Specific
language over category language. The word "unique" is banned. The word "innovative" is
banned. Show what's different — don't claim it.
Example Direction (not final copy)
Threaditate is a string art kit with a guided companion app. You upload a photo — a face, a skyline,
your dog, anything — and the app converts it into a real-time instruction sequence. Each step tells
you exactly where to place the next string. You confirm it's done. Then you move to the next one. No
diagrams. No guessing. No looking at a reference and back at your loom until you lose your place.
Just you, the thread, and what the app tells you next. Most first-time makers finish in under two
hours. The kit ships with everything: the circular peg loom, a full thread set, and access to the app.
You bring the photo.
Iconography Bar
Three to five icons that communicate key attributes instantly — for scrollers who won't read.
Each icon must have a label of two to four words. No sentences.

Full Reviews Section
All reviews, not just five-star. Nik Sharma is explicit on this: showing the full review distribution
builds more trust than hiding it. Customers can smell curation. The standard of "this product is
good" is now met by having real reviews that include the occasional 3-star with a thoughtful
response. Pull the most emotional, experiential reviews to the top. Filter to show by default:
"Most Helpful" not "Most Recent."

4.2 Copy Anti-Patterns
These are structural mistakes that appear at the page level — not word-level errors, but copy
architecture problems:
• Starting with "We" — always start with "You." The customer is the subject of every
sentence.

• Opening with company history — no one who arrived from a Meta ad cares. Lead with
the product, the experience, or the hook.
• Passive voice — "The string is placed..." becomes "You place the string."
• Unanswered questions — if a visitor could reasonably wonder it, your copy should have
answered it already. Unanswered questions become abandoned carts.
• Competing calls-to-action — one CTA per section. Multiple CTAs create decision
paralysis.
• Social proof at the bottom — this is the most common DTC mistake. Proof goes near the
top. It earns the rest of the scroll.
• Features without benefits — listing what the kit contains without explaining what those
contents do for the person buying them.
• Copy that's longer than it needs to be — every sentence that doesn't earn its place
steals attention from one that does.
Part 5: Testing Protocol
5.1 What to Test First
Nik Sharma's guidance across multiple Limited Supply episodes: test your headline on Meta
ads before you commit it to the landing page. The ad creative is the fastest, cheapest way to
validate whether your hook lands. A headline that gets clicks in an ad is a headline that works.
Don't guess — run it.
CTC's doctrine: creative ideas should be testable, tailored, and transferable. A copy hook that
works organically on social can become the foundation of your best-performing ad — and vice
versa. Treat the site, the ads, and the social as a single copy ecosystem, not three separate
systems.
5.2 Priority Test Order
1. Hero headline — test 2–3 variants on Meta ads first. Winner becomes the LP headline.
2. CTA button copy — "Get My Kit" vs. "Start Making" vs. "Add to Cart." Small change,
meaningful conversion delta.
3. Social proof placement — strip immediately below hero vs. integrated within hero
section.
4. Above-fold benefit line — emotional truth variant vs. rational justification variant.
5. PDP description length — 80-word punchy vs. 160-word full narrative.
5.3 What Good Looks Like
A landing page that is working: visitors scroll past the hero at a rate above 50%. Visitors reach
the CTA module at a rate above 20%. Add-to-cart rate from the LP exceeds 3%.
