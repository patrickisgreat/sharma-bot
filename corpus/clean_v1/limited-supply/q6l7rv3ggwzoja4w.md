# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Nik Sharma welcomes Aaron Paul to discuss affiliate marketing, PR, and building consumer brands. Aaron has spent six years in the affiliate space, generating over $500 million in affiliate revenue, and founded Paul Street, an agency specializing in PR affiliate, mass media, performance PR, and influencer partnerships for brands like HexClad, Todd Snyder, and Liquid IV.

The conversation begins with an exploration of how PR has evolved. Nik notes that PR was once a make-or-break channel for brand launches six to seven years ago, with major outlets like Fortune and Bloomberg providing significant traffic spikes. However, the landscape has shifted considerably.

Aaron explains that PR is now primarily an exercise in credibility building rather than audience discovery. He describes PR as quickly dying in its traditional form, with the real value emerging at the intersection of PR and affiliate marketing. Rather than viewing PR as an ongoing initiative focused on monthly placements, Aaron recommends a sprint-based approach tied to story-worthy moments like capital raises, new product launches, or celebrity partnerships.

The key shift is understanding that PR today serves the middle of the funnel, providing social proof rather than discovering new audiences. When consumers have decided they want to purchase from a brand, PR assets give them validation that they won't be ripped off and that the product actually works. Nik points out that traditional media articles often have surprisingly low viewership and click-through rates, making the direct traffic value minimal.

Aaron emphasizes that affiliates serve as a mirror of brand perception. If a brand isn't well-known or lacks credibility, affiliates won't be interested in promoting it. This creates a symbiotic relationship where PR initiatives that build credibility make the brand more attractive to affiliate partners. Any big-picture initiative, whether sports team partnerships, Formula One driver deals, or PR inclusion programs, increases comfort and credibility that makes people want to become affiliates.

Creators are positioned as the next evolution of PR and publishers. Rather than just sending products to magazine editors, modern brands should target creators across podcasts, Substack, TikTok, and Instagram. There are multiple ways to work with creators, from organic seeding to CPA and CPC deals to flat-fee engagement arrangements.

The conversation shifts to timing for affiliate program adoption. Aaron stresses a critical principle: brands typically get only one shot with each affiliate. If an affiliate invests in content about a brand and it doesn't convert or creates a negative experience with their audience, they won't return. Affiliates wake up to hundreds of brand partnership requests daily, so first impressions matter.

Affiliates are described as simultaneously lazy and greedy. Brands must provide everything affiliates need to effectively promote the brand while minimizing their work. This means offering multiple angles on how to talk about the product. For a greens gummy brand, this could include angles around GLP-1, vitamin D, busy moms, and other positioning options that allow affiliates to use their creative freedom while promoting the brand.

Aaron introduces the concept of EPCs, or earnings per click. Successful offers typically generate two to three to four dollars per click. The math is straightforward: affiliates look at click-based revenue and calculate conversion rates based on their audience's behavior and the offer's attractiveness.

Affiliates prioritize three factors above all. First, the brand's offer and products must convert. A high CPA means nothing if traffic lands on a poor-converting page. Second, brands must pay on time. An unpaid affiliate quickly becomes an inactive affiliate. Third, CPA structures should be negotiated rather than fixed across the board. Instead of standard ten to fifteen percent payouts, successful brands offer more to attract affiliates initially, then leverage that relationship for better deals as it matures.

Aaron explains that roughly eighty percent of affiliate revenue typically comes from just fifteen to twenty percent of affiliates. These top performers merit special attention and custom deal structures. Options include paid initiatives like newsletter placements, whitelisting deals where affiliates run traffic on their own Facebook pages with percentage-of-spend models, access to articles run on the brand's Facebook account, CPA deals aligned with what the brand pays Facebook, and CPC test deals to explore new content types.

The concept of media buyer affiliates represents a more sophisticated affiliate category. These are people who specialize in buying traffic across channels like Reddit, Tapjoy, Facebook, YouTube, and app networks. They arbitrage the difference between what they pay for traffic and what brands pay them on a CPA basis. A media buyer might acquire customers for eighty dollars while the brand pays them one hundred fifty, allowing them to scale to thousands of sales per day while maintaining a healthy margin.

Media buyer affiliates evolved from the early days of affiliate marketing, when the space operated in black hat territory with minimal compliance oversight. Those early practitioners developed exceptional creative skills and landing page optimization abilities simply to compete in an overcrowded market. Today, these same skills are applied to legitimate consumer brands.

Aaron shares the story of Jumbleberry and its efforts to bring media buyer expertise to legitimate brands. They initially struggled but eventually found success with brands like Goli Nutrition, now the largest TikTok Shop partner, which was essentially built by affiliate marketers. The Onnit example demonstrates the playbook: understanding what works from a brand's paid media perspective, then applying direct response principles to create advertorials and optimized landing pages that drive affiliate traffic.

For Onnit's Alpha Brain, they created an article on article.onit.com functioning as a direct response advertorial about Joe Rogan's productivity habits. The narrative led readers through the benefits of Alpha Brain with call-to-actions and offer-based elements before directing them to a gated landing page rather than the main website. When dialed in, individual affiliates drove three to four hundred to five hundred sales per day, with ten to fifteen affiliates collectively driving nearly two thousand sales daily on a CPA model.

Aaron notes that CPA-based affiliate marketing is a sleeper tactic that has scaled many of today's household brands, including Pet Labs Co., Athletic Greens, and others. Media buyer affiliates pioneered tactics like advertorials and landing page optimization four to five years before direct-to-consumer brands mainstream adopted them. Whitelisting deals, now trendy, were standard practice among these affiliates years ago.

The fundamental distinction between affiliate marketing and algorithmic channels like Facebook is that affiliate is relationship-based. Brands deal with people, not machines. Success requires conversation, negotiation, and customizing the environment for mutual success. Every deal will differ in payout structures, exclusivity terms, and creative styles. Viewing affiliate partnerships through a relationship-building lens rather than as a mechanical system drives scaling.

Aaron encourages brands to understand that they should test affiliate thoughtfully and commit to it. Brands that hesitate and test with limited budgets often fail and subsequently claim affiliate doesn't work, when the channel actually requires meaningful investment and strategic approach to succeed. The brands that crush it are those understanding the investment and time horizon required.
