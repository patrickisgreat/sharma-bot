# The Boring Things Work

The fundamentals matter more than flashy trends. Everyone at e-commerce fuel was running something very simple—usually one category or one product—and they knew it inside out. They knew their entire industry and everybody in it. They just executed the basics really well.

A lot of people overthink site design, animations, and other details. The reality is you need to focus on good landing pages, good copy, trusted payment badges like MasterCard and PayPal verification checks, and selling a good product. That's it.

One surprising thing learned was about navigation menus. Sean McGinnis, formerly CEO of Kuru Footwear, had about 150 options in his navigation menu—every single one a reason to buy their shoe. You might sit in a room and think there are seven reasons people buy, then choose one to test. Instead, he recognized there are seventy-five reasons people buy the shoe and put them all on the nav bar. As soon as somebody sees what they're looking for, they click.

There should be better footwear options for specific professions. Consider nurses—they need shoes that can withstand being washed, that are comfortable for standing all day, and that can handle human fluids. Figs created excellent medical scrubs but never really nailed healthcare footwear the way they could have.

He found a manufacturer, made the shoe, did ninety iterations, tested it, and launched it successfully. The site gets fifty-four thousand uniques a month. He was a single player owning the category.

Birchbox was purchased for forty-five million in 2021 but has completely fallen apart. In November 2022, they posted on Instagram saying they knew members were frustrated and would have answers in a couple of weeks. Five months later, they never delivered those answers. # Silicon Valley Bank Collapse

Multiple companies were affected. Camp, a kid's retailer, banked there. Omsom, the sauce company, was there too. EMI got out on Thursday or Friday, which was quick thinking.

Interestingly, most people I talked to weren't banking with Silicon Valley Bank. They were using Mercury, Brex, or Chase instead. Many moved to Brex for their banking services after they launched their full bank offering with financial incentives.

One company received a text Thursday asking if they should move money. Someone said they'd be fine. By Friday morning, they initiated a wire but the bank was already closed. They worried about money over the weekend.

Many portfolio companies emailed saying either they had exposure or they didn't. The federal government stepping in meant everyone was ultimately fine.

# How SVB Failed

Banks make money by taking deposits at one interest rate and loaning out those deposits at a higher rate. Silicon Valley Bank grew explosively—from fifty billion in deposits in December 2018 to one hundred billion in December 2020 to one hundred seventy-five billion in December 2022. They were doubling every two years while the industry grew at thirty-seven percent.

About a year ago, SVB made a terrible decision. They took eighty billion in deposits and bought long-term debt maturing in 2030-2033, paying only two percent interest. When interest rates go up, bond prices go down. Securities you bought at two percent suddenly look worthless when you can get four or five percent elsewhere.

The math is simple: if a bond paying five percent is worth a dollar, but you can get ten percent elsewhere, nobody wants the five percent bond. The price has to drop. SVB had to mark down eighty billion in securities materially. They announced a two billion dollar write-down and said they'd raise two billion dollars. That money never came.

The deposits themselves were unusual. Silicon Valley Bank focused on tech startups and companies, not regular people. The average account was more than four million dollars. One hundred fifty-two billion of one hundred seventy-three billion in deposits was uninsured—above the two hundred fifty thousand limit. They had few depositors with massive accounts.

When interest rates rose, depositors realized they could get much higher rates elsewhere. They started withdrawing money. For the first time, deposits declined—from one hundred ninety-eight billion in March 2022 to one hundred seventy-three billion in December 2022 to one hundred sixty-five billion in February 2023.

On Thursday or Friday, they announced a two billion dollar capital raise. Everyone panicked. In one day, forty-two billion in deposits was lost. That's almost a third of their total deposits. Everyone withdrew money first because they feared insolvency.

# The Government Bailout

The federal government stepped in and guaranteed all deposits, even those above the two hundred fifty thousand dollar FDIC limit. This was probably the right decision because without trust in banks, the entire economy falls apart. If people can't access their money, it collapses the whole system.

But this created unfair outcomes. The government essentially said if your bank fails and you're a tech company with four million dollars at Silicon Valley Bank, you're protected. But most regular people don't get that protection because they're not tech companies.

Another question: what happens to companies that borrowed from SVB? If they had a million dollar loan and SVB goes under, do they still have to repay? What about contractual terms requiring them to bank only at SVB?

# Treasury Account Rates and Banking Options

Mercury now offers four point seven percent on deposits sitting in treasury accounts. Mercury's FDIC insurance covers one million, compared to Silicon Valley Bank's only two hundred fifty thousand. Brex covers one and a half to two million. Mercury just released a product for three million in coverage.

Most people moved to Mercury, Brex, or Chase after the SVB collapse. There's an opportunity for insurance products to insure accounts above two hundred fifty thousand for a fee—companies might pay to avoid this stress.

# Federal Reserve Actions

The Federal Reserve set up a Bank Term Funding Program where they essentially buy securities at par value even though they're worth less. If you bought Bitcoin at sixty thousand and it's now worth twenty-five thousand, the government loans you sixty thousand against it as collateral. The American taxpayer is subsidizing the difference.

The FDIC insured banks will pay for the SVB bailout through increased insurance premiums—it's effectively a tax on banks. But the Bank Term Funding Program comes from the American taxpayer directly. This is the first time feeling compelled to run for federal office because the system is so unfair.

# Interest Rate Forecasting

A year ago, it wasn't obvious that interest rates would rise above five percent. Wealth managers were saying the Federal Reserve would achieve a soft landing without major rate increases. Today it's clear that was wrong. Interest rates are above five percent and possibly heading higher.

A year ago when SVB bought two percent securities with ten-year maturities, it wasn't as obviously stupid as it is today. Today you can get four to five percent. The risk was there but not as apparent. Many smart people got this wrong, including professional wealth managers.

Regulators could have caught this through audits looking at security maturity and credit profiles. Long-term securities are risky because interest rates can change. But if everyone complains about too much regulation killing capitalism, regulators can't do those audits.

# Executive Stock Sales at SVB

The CEO of SVB sold 3.6 million shares in late February. The counsel sold almost twenty percent in early February. The CFO sold thirty-two percent at the end of February. The CMO sold a quarter at the beginning of February. These were significant portions of their personal stakes.

Executives can legally sell shares under pre-announced 10B51 plans that set a schedule for sales in advance. Recently, CEOs have been announcing plans to sell all shares the next week, claiming it still falls under the safe harbor. The SVB executives' timing is extremely suspicious.

# The OnTrack Case

There's a case involving OnTrack, a pharmaceutical company. The CEO texted coworkers saying their relationship with a major client Signia was a nightmare and cancellation was coming soon. Signia was their biggest client. He tried to sell shares, was told to wait thirty days, went to another brokerage instead.

He created a 10B51 plan on August 13th, 2021, and started selling four days later on August 17th. On August 19th, the company announced they were losing Signia and the stock crashed fifty percent. He sold at twenty-two dollars; shares ended up at eleven dollars then under one dollar.

He claimed the plan protected him from insider trading charges. The U.S. government says that's actually insider trading. He sold twenty-four million in shares that would be worth roughly six hundred thousand to seven hundred thousand today. He may face jail time.

# What Happens to the Money

It's unclear what happens to money gained through illegal insider trading. The assumption is it gets seized but the enforcement is inconsistent. Michael Milken made billions in the 1980s through insider trading, went to jail for two years, came out still a billionaire, and later received a Trump pardon.

# The Fintech Question

How does this change perception of fintech companies and neobanks? Average people now worry about whether they're insured at two hundred fifty thousand or more. Are there risks using newer financial companies?

Mercury backs its accounts on Evolve Bank and Trust and Choice Financial Group. If Evolve fell, would the government step in? Are new banks owed the same guarantee as Silicon Valley Bank? That's a question the Federal Reserve needs to answer directly.
