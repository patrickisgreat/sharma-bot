# Limited Supply: Offshore Hiring and Subscription Laws

As a brand, you're spending hard-earned dollars driving traffic to your site, but the problem is that 98% of that traffic is anonymous. Once these visitors leave, it's difficult to find them again and convert them into customers.

## Offshore Hiring Playbook

Offshore hiring often gets a negative connotation in the industry, but that's largely because many people don't know how to properly onboard talent outside the United States. I initially had unsuccessful experiences with offshore hires—I'd brought on developers and assistants from the Philippines, and while some worked out, others didn't. This left me skeptical about the entire approach.

However, my perspective changed when my friend Austin, who founded Morning Brew, recommended a company called Oceans. He'd hired a CFO from them and suggested I give offshore hiring another serious try. I created what I thought was an impossible job description: someone who knew Shopify, Facebook ads, and Figma design, who had an iPhone for FaceTime communication, who could work late Friday nights or Saturday mornings if needed, and who was genuinely obsessed with the role.

To my surprise, I found Ramiz, who was the former head of e-commerce at Ogilvy in Sri Lanka. He's now my right-hand assistant and checks every box I outlined. This success led me to hire another four or five people from Oceans in Sri Lanka, and we've built out a strong team there. We're now planning to hire ten to fifteen people outside the US this year across South America and Sri Lanka.

The hiring process I've developed works quickly—we've made first hires in two weeks and subsequent hires in four weeks. Here's how it works:

**Start with a recruiter.** If you're based in the US, you understand American culture and how things work locally. But if you're hiring in countries you've never visited, you don't know their local culture, and you risk being taken advantage of or scammed. A recruiter handles this for you. They vet talent, they do headhunting, and they find some of the best people—not just those actively looking for work.

**Create a specific job description.** This isn't your typical LinkedIn job posting. Think of it as a brief with lots of color and context. Include background on your company, how many people work there, why someone should be excited about the role, and what daily output you expect. If you're hiring a designer, maybe it's producing X assets daily. Quantify the output on the upper end so expectations align. Also include hours, time zones, and how you'll communicate—whether that's Slack, iMessage, FaceTime, or WhatsApp.

**Request candidate video submissions.** Have candidates record a quick video introducing themselves, explaining why they're interested in the role, where they're from, their background, and relevant hobbies. This keeps the process asynchronous and efficient. You watch videos only for candidates you're genuinely interested in.

**Create a test project.** Design a project that candidates can complete within one to two days. For a designer role, I created a brief for a new skincare brand with value props, benefits, asset guidelines, and font files. I asked for a wireframe, UI design, and a prototyped Figma file with animations. They should also submit a Loom video explaining the project, how long it took, what tools they used, any roadblocks, and interesting findings.

**Consider a secondary stress test project.** This can help you understand if someone can handle tight deadlines. For the designer role, I added a compressed-timeframe project where they had two to three hours to complete something of high quality. This lets you test whether they can handle your worst-case scenario early on.

**Conduct an interview.** Once you're satisfied with the work, do a proper Zoom or FaceTime call. At this point, you've already seen their work and gotten to know them asynchronously. The interview is mainly to ensure they're a good culture fit and a normal human being. Be prepared to answer questions about your team environment, PTO, days off, and hours—they'll likely have worked with American companies before.

**Prepare offer letter and onboarding.** Work with your attorneys on employment law specific to the relevant country to protect yourself and your employees. Use a payroll company like Thera, which came out of Y Combinator and handles international payroll seamlessly, living in your Slack channel and managing all the heavy lifting to ensure timely payments.

**Focus on ongoing management and incentives.** Don't treat offshore talent differently from US-based remote employees. The best advice is to not treat them as "offshore" at all—just treat them like normal employees. Do the same promotions, incentives, company parties, and culture-building activities. If you hire genuinely good people who are good human beings, everything works out perfectly.

## Subscription Business Best Practices

A good subscription business has several key characteristics. First, it should serve a recurring need or habit—something customers use up regularly like coffee, supplements, skincare, pet food, or personal care. If people love a product and run through it, you have a subscription opportunity.

Second, create a seamless customer experience. Most brands are good at signup, but only about 35% excel at making it easy to manage a subscription, and only 25% make cancellation easy. This matters more than you might think. Contrary to what many believe, making it easy to cancel actually creates happier, longer-term customers. Customers who struggle to manage or cancel their subscriptions will become dissatisfied customers.

**Subscription Cadence:** Most apps offer 14-day, 30-day, 60-day, and 90-day options. In nine out of ten cases, 30 days is all you need. There are exceptions—HelloFresh and pet food deliveries might need weekly options, and wine clubs often use quarterly billing to avoid the appearance of encouraging excessive drinking. But for most brands, 30 days is perfect.

**Membership Models:** Many brands are exploring membership instead of traditional subscriptions. I looked at three strong examples, all powered by Inveterate. Built, a men's apparel brand, charges $50 annually for 20% off purchases, free shipping and returns, exclusive discounts, and priority customer service. Liquid IV offers a free membership with 2% cash back on purchases redeemable for Liquid IV products and early access to new products. Dr. Brand charges $59 annually and clearly states on their landing page that "the benefits are so good, the membership pays for itself." Members receive 20% off one-time orders, 25% off subscriptions, a free $30 gift at signup, $20 cash back every three months plus 10% cash back on each order, and priority service.

Dr. Brand's example is particularly genius because the member sees the free gift value immediately, gets a discount on their first order, and then receives ongoing cash back. The margins in skincare are high—often around 90%—so a $30 gift that costs them $3 to make creates a compelling arbitrage.

**Churn Reduction:** Most subscription apps have built-in churn reduction tools. When someone goes to cancel, offer them options to skip the current order, swap products, or add a free gift to incentivize staying. Additionally, implement payment token recovery. When a customer's payment method fails, continue attempting to run the card until it goes through rather than immediately canceling. While this exists in a gray area legally, many successful subscription brands use this strategy.

**Content for Subscribers:** Content you send to subscribers should differ from content for one-time purchasers. Early access, "promotions," emails, and SMS should all feel different. Make subscribers feel like VIPs. You can even run video view campaigns specifically thanking subscribers, since CPMs are low and you can target exact audiences.

## New FTC Subscription Laws (April 2025)

New FTC rules are taking effect in April 2025 for any business falling into these categories: free trials converting to subscriptions, subscriptions with automatic renewal, pre-notification plans (post-purchase upsells made after an initial purchase with payment token on file), and continuity plans (regular billing at intervals with no set end date—standard DTC subscriptions).

**Explicit Consent:** Customers must actively check a box confirming they're opting into a subscription. You're already seeing this on Shopify checkout with messaging confirming the recurring payment.

**Easy Cancellation:** Subscriptions must be as easy to cancel as they are to sign up for. The days of making customers call to cancel or navigate complex forms are over. If you cannot offer cancellation as easily as signup, you're creating legal liability.

**Transparent Disclosures:** Make subscription information obvious. How often does it charge? What's the renewal amount? If it's a trial-to-subscription, what does it renew at and when? The customer portal must be easy to understand and navigate, with clear billing and subscription information.

**Reminders and Notifications:** Send notifications in advance of charges with clear information. Include direct links to easily cancel if desired. When someone cancels, send a clear confirmation email outlining the cancellation and effective end date.

These requirements aren't especially burdensome for brands already doing things right, but they will eliminate predatory subscription practices and create liability for those who don't comply.
