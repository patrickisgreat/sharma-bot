# Limited Supply: Season 8, Episode 12 - Listener Mailbox

Getting customers to your own site instead of Amazon requires strategic thinking. If you're selling on Amazon primarily, you need to give people compelling reasons to purchase from your Shopify store instead. One effective approach is offering incentives tied to email capture. For example, offer a free toothbrush when someone provides their email address. This accomplishes two things: you build an email list for ongoing marketing, and you create a reason for customers to choose your site over Amazon's convenience.

The discount or incentive doesn't need to be strictly monetary. Instead of offering a lower price, you could structure it as a bundle deal—buy four cases and get a free case, for instance. The key is making your direct-to-consumer experience special and differentiated from Amazon, where everything becomes commoditized. You need to increase your average order value so you have room to advertise and acquire customers profitably. Without that AOV expansion, the economics won't work for sustainable growth.

When launching as a completely new bootstrap brand, the easiest path to your first customer comes through social media platforms where you can reach millions of people with minimal investment. Product Hunt is another effective channel, particularly for launching native products. Reddit communities and niche forums where your product solves real problems are valuable sources. You can also reach out to creators individually with product samples, use existing marketplaces like Amazon or TikTok Shop where traffic already exists, or tap into community platforms where potential customers are already discussing problems your product solves.

However, getting that first customer is genuinely easy. The real challenge is acquiring customer number one thousand. The costs scale dramatically as you grow. This is fundamentally different from pharmaceutical development where R&D costs are front-loaded. With e-commerce, it's the reverse—your first customer is cheap, but your last one is exponentially more expensive.

The biggest mistake brand new direct-to-consumer founders make is failing to develop repeatable marketing channels. Many early-stage founders believe that word-of-mouth and customer referrals will drive growth because people love their product. This approach almost never works as a primary growth strategy. You need to identify and master a repeatable acquisition channel, whether that's paid social, YouTube, TikTok, or another platform.

There are two particularly dangerous myths early founders fall into. First is the referral myth—the belief that satisfied customers will naturally refer others at scale. Second is the willingness to spend more than gross margin or contribution margin on customer acquisition in hopes that lifetime value will justify it. This rarely makes sense for direct-to-consumer businesses, though it might work for subscription services like cell phone companies where long-term contracts justify high acquisition costs.

Other early-stage mistakes include spending too much money on branding agencies, design, and website development before validating the business. Choosing the wrong partners—whether agencies, fulfillment providers, or contractors—can be costly. Getting legal paperwork wrong from the start creates expensive problems later. Reading contracts carefully is essential; never sign any contract without an out clause. You always have leverage in negotiations.

When scaling beyond ten million dollars in revenue, many brands make critical errors in how they respond to changing market conditions. When advertising costs rise or product competitiveness declines, some founders respond by spending more rather than taking necessary medicine. It's perfectly acceptable for a business to contract from twenty million to ten million in revenue if it means regrouping and rebuilding on solid ground. You're not a public company with shareholders watching your quarterly results. Many founders panic and spend recklessly, which can lead to bankruptcy or severe financial stress.

Another mistake at this scale is hiring too many agencies. Most agencies are actually mistakes. Operating with one or two makes sense, but four or five indicates you've gone too far. Fractional CFOs and finance firms sometimes build unrealistic models projecting growth that has no real foundation. You don't need to double month-over-month or maintain extreme growth year after year. Running a sustainable business is legitimate, even if it means more modest growth.

Regarding maximum ad spend allocation, it depends heavily on your business model. For durable goods with high margins and one-time purchases—think mattresses or furniture—you can justify spending fifty to seventy percent of gross margin on customer acquisition. If you're selling a thousand-dollar mattress with two hundred dollars in COGS and eighty percent gross margin, spending six hundred dollars on marketing still leaves you with a solid profit.

For repeat-purchase products, more conservative spending of twenty-five to forty percent of revenue is appropriate, with forty percent being aggressive growth-focused spending. These percentages change across the business lifecycle. As you approach billion-dollar scale, spending percentages typically decrease, though many large public companies obscure their true marketing spend by hiding it in other line items.

Regarding AI's impact on e-commerce marketing, most applications aren't yet ready for prime time. Roughly ninety-five percent of new AI marketing tools will become commodities within six months. Some genuinely interesting applications exist though. Flockshop uses AI to render cosmetic shades across different skin tones and ethnicities, rapidly creating product imagery. AI-generated videos showing brand stories and history can work well if you're producing content at scale. Facebook's AI headline and description suggestions for ads are decent but rarely better than what you can create yourself.

For customer service, AI is more proven—using it to handle "where's my order" queries makes sense since these are commoditized responses. For core marketing functions like copy and creative, you're probably two to four years away from AI making a real difference, not six to twelve months as some claim. The fundamental problem is that every successful brand built its momentum on originality. AI, by definition, creates based on what already exists. It doesn't generate imagination or true differentiation. When everyone has access to the same AI tools, you lose competitive advantage.

Getting customers excited about leaving reviews is simpler than getting them to post on social media. The approach that works best is just asking. Send plain-text emails from the founder thanking customers for their purchase and requesting a review. These emails should ideally go to the inbox, not be promotional templates loaded with images. Explain that you're a small business trying to improve and that reviews help you immensely.

The psychology works because people don't want to publicly trash a small business they're rooting for, but they're happy to leave positive reviews to support brands they like. If a customer had a bad experience, offer them a chance to reply directly rather than publicly post negative reviews. This approach—combined with strategic use of coupon codes, gift-with-purchase incentives, or PostScript for cashback rewards—can significantly increase review volume.

Getting people to organically post about your brand on social media is much harder. Most people post about very few purchases they make. The brands that succeed at this have created either an exceptional unboxing experience or massive cultural cachet. Jolie has become somewhat of a status symbol around showerheads. Last Crumb has built unboxing experiences so exceptional that people want to photograph and share them. They spend significantly more on packaging and shipping than typical brands, but the social amplification justifies it.

For most brands, trying to incentivize posts with gift cards or small rewards produces disappointing results. Instead, focus on building authentic brand love through great content, storytelling, and a strong brand aura. You can amplify customer content by reposting customer stories to your own channels, which encourages future posts. But expecting posting to be a reliable customer acquisition metric will likely frustrate you.

For brands with a thirty-dollar AOV trying to transition from Amazon-only to direct-to-consumer, the math is challenging but not impossible. First, if your product genuinely needs replacement every three to four months, lead with this information and consider putting customers on subscription automatically. This creates predictable revenue and simplifies the repurchase decision.

Second, focus aggressively on increasing AOV. Stack purchases by offering discounts for buying three or four units at once. Source complementary products—if you're selling toothbrushes, add a dop kit. This wasn't purchased consumable wear items, so it could cost you a dollar fifty to source and sell for twelve to fifteen dollars. Pre-purchase and post-purchase upsells expand AOV significantly. When Native started with a thirteen-dollar AOV and grew it to twenty-six or twenty-seven dollars through three-packs and upsells, this created the advertising room needed for profitable customer acquisition.

Third, recognize that transitioning from Amazon-primary to direct-to-consumer is exceptionally difficult. Customers already know you on Amazon, your reviews live there, they've saved their payment information there. The best-case scenario for most brands is shifting twenty to thirty percent of sales to Shopify while maintaining Amazon. Nobody successfully grows to Amazon-only then flips to seventy percent Shopify.

Finally, play the email game aggressively. Use incentives to capture emails—free products for email sign-ups. Once you have email addresses, market continuously. Offer reasons to buy from your site instead of Amazon that go beyond just price. Consider exclusive products available only on your site. If you stock your five products on both channels but develop exclusive products available only direct-to-consumer, you create compelling reasons for Amazon customers to visit your site. The same strategy works with retailers—target and Walmart all expect exclusives.
