# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Jared runs all things marketing for Resident Home, which was acquired for a billion dollars by Ashley Furniture. Resident is one of the most successful mattress direct-to-consumer companies and has been known for being at the cutting edge of performance marketing. After the acquisition, Jared built out an internal agency to service all of Ashley's media needs while managing the marketing of a massive catalog of furniture pieces.

Jared's background spans several decades in direct marketing. He started in a traditional direct marketing agency where he aggregated data through free products and sweepstakes, then marketed that data to customers. He worked on credit card applications, ringtones, and other products that people probably weren't actively seeking. After about ten to eleven years there, he moved into the brand side of marketing, working in skincare, hair care, and pet space. He also had entrepreneurial stints trying to invent products like a special cup for toddlers and a vegan organic dehydrated coconut chip company. He spent two years in ad tech, which he views as a transformative experience that fundamentally changed how he thinks about marketing. That experience led him to Resident six years ago.

Before the acquisition, Jared was one hundred percent focused on Resident's customer acquisition. After the acquisition, his duties split between Resident and Ashley. At Resident, he manages acquisition, CRM, creative optimizations, and operations. At Ashley, he built an internal team to take over the marketing spend that had been handled by external agencies. This presented significant challenges because Resident is a very lean company without the resources to simply add new work on top of existing responsibilities. Jared had to build the Ashley team while simultaneously executing on it.

The transition from Resident to Ashley required different skills. At Resident, the focus is entirely on performance marketing and optimization toward conversion. Ashley's situation was more complex. Initially, Jared's approach was to understand what was happening with their agencies, take over execution without changing much, and make smarter operational changes. After about two months of getting their footing, they started making significant changes to the media mix based on performance data. These changes paid off almost immediately and have continued to improve over time.

When Jared first looked at Ashley's ad accounts, he was surprised not just at the CPAs but at the overall efficiency of the business. The Ashley brand provides significant benefits in terms of organic and direct traffic. Within the platforms themselves, there were many changes needed. However, Ashley's situation is fundamentally different from Resident's because Ashley has physical retail stores with associated overhead that Resident doesn't have.

One of the key learnings Jared ported from Resident to Ashley was ensuring subject matter experts in house for core platforms. At Resident, the skill level of platform specialists was very high, and Jared wanted to uplevel that at Ashley. However, they also had to lean on existing Ashley teams to understand aspects of the business that were entirely new to the Resident team.

The internal agency structure at Ashley required different skills than managing marketing for Resident. While Resident has one clear stakeholder, the internal agency at Ashley has to manage relationships and expectations with another marketing team within the same company. This different dynamic required people with skills the Resident team had never needed before.

Taking learnings from Resident to apply to Ashley proved more difficult than expected. Ashley's business is far more complex with tens of thousands of SKUs, true omnichannel retail operations, and wholly owned stores. Resident operates primarily online with a much smaller catalog. While there are paths to connecting online spend to retail results at Ashley, something that was impossible at Resident, the complexity makes this much harder to manage.

On the measurement side, both companies employ a buy versus build mentality. At Resident, they've built their own ID graph, MMM models, and multi-touch attribution models. They're in the process of building similar tools for Ashley, though this is a longer and harder journey because connecting online spend to in-store conversion requires understanding a path to conversion that extends into physical retail.

The approach to catalog management at Ashley is completely different from Resident. Ashley has significant complications with feed management including different pricing zones across regions. At one point they had twelve different pricing zones, now working down to four. The same product has different prices in different geographic regions, which creates diagnostic issues when Google thinks something is out of stock when it's not based on the region the crawler is coming from. The catalog is constantly changing with third-party products, wholly owned products, and exclusive products all in the mix. This is a massive undertaking compared to Resident's much smaller catalog.

Regarding lifecycle marketing and email, the Resident team hasn't taken over that function for Ashley yet. At Resident, selling a mattress and then following up to drive lifetime value through sheets, bed frames, and bedroom furniture is helpful but not critical. At Ashley, lifetime value is much more important because there are many more rooms in the house and hundreds of thousands of SKUs where there are real relationships between products.

Resident uses AI extensively on the creative side with a very robust naming protocol for ads. Because mattress sales don't generate enough signal for traditional A-B testing, they focus on understanding the elements of ads that are working. They parse data by room color, beats per minute of music, and even the angle of the mattress. This naming convention is detailed, and they use AI to help modify and work on naming as well as creative generation.

Jared recently used AI to dig deeper into why a major holiday promotion performed well. He wanted to understand not just what performed, but why. By analyzing past holidays, he discovered that last year's terrible weather was a factor in why this year's Memorial Day weekend saw better shopping. This wasn't something he would have thought of on his own, but it helped explain the results.

The team is starting to use AI computer vision to identify creative elements and tag them automatically. They currently do this manually with people dedicated to naming ads, but they see potential in tools that can identify what elements are in ads, whether people and pets are featured, whether it's lifestyle content, and other visual attributes. The goal isn't to replace people but to make their jobs easier by using tools to automate the tagging process.

When exploring creative tools more broadly, Jared notes that many have limitations when pulling results from platforms unless you hook up your source of truth or MTA and feed that back to the platform. Tools that develop briefs based on creative analysis and do competitive analysis are interesting. They're also experimenting with VO3 to recreate successful ads. Currently limited to six to eight seconds for hooks, they want to test whether their growth team and creative operations people without expert video skills can recreate winning hooks that they've spent money on in studio. If they can, this could mean one person could produce hundreds of hooks per week instead of a handful, fundamentally changing their creative production capacity.

For Ashley with its massive catalog, they've made huge strides using AI-generated images or improved images via tools rather than just photo shoots. The implications are significant because there are tens of thousands of products in their feeds, and improvements to those images could drive enormous performance gains across the entire business.

Black Friday is the biggest tent pole of the year for both brands. For Resident, the process is very regimented because they do this at scale, spending hundreds of millions of dollars annually. They focus on specific holidays when Americans are conditioned to buy furniture and mattresses: President's Day, Memorial Day, Labor Day, Black Friday, and Cyber Monday. The process is almost like rinse and repeat. They know they'll be uncomfortable with efficiency metrics in the lead-up to the holiday but that everything will normalize on the holiday itself and beyond. This requires the intestinal fortitude to trust the process even when the numbers look bad.

For Ashley, the approach is different. They take their cues from the brand team and promo team regarding what will be in market, the promotional ideas, and offers. They execute much of the strategy but are really following Ashley's lead rather than dictating their own offers. Both teams need to work together because otherwise what Resident's marketing does on its own and what Ashley is doing on their own won't work together in the marketplace.

During tent pole holidays at Resident, they activate all traditional digital channels plus connected TV. They don't typically use connected TV outside of these holiday periods because no one browses a mattress site casually. But during holidays from Friday to Monday, if someone has been on the site the previous week without purchasing, serving them a connected TV ad on their living room television can trigger recognition and drive them back to purchase. Jared considers this marketing hygiene, the right thing to do, even though it's very difficult to measure. When you're spending millions per day over four days, it's nearly impossible to isolate TV's contribution. You have to trust that it makes sense and include it in the mix.

AppLovin is one of their channels, and Jared was initially extremely skeptical about ads in mobile games. His CEO asked him to check out the platform, and Jared essentially bet his job that they couldn't sell mattresses through ads in games like Candy Crush. He was wrong. The platform worked surprisingly well. Initially there was significant hand-holding and no exclusions or self-service UI, but the AppLovin team was excellent, providing great service and recommendations. Understanding the platform's performance relative to their internal tools and other channels showed it continued to grow as a part of their mix.

The creative approach on AppLovin differs from other platforms. They focus heavily on end cards because the ads are thirty seconds and non-skippable. If they can keep someone engaged for twenty-eight or twenty-nine seconds, the end card can present the offer in a compelling way. This is very different from other platforms where you're not guaranteed someone will see your full ad. Additionally, porting nine by sixteen creatives from Meta or TikTok doesn't necessarily work on AppLovin. The platform rewards creative diversity and picks up on what's working faster than some other platforms.

Regarding incrementality, Jared notes that in a perfect world everyone would do incrementality tests at all times, but it's actually impossible. Instead, they use their internal tools to analyze prior periods and platform performance versus other platforms. If they increase spend and their predictive tools indicate what they should see in the future, they monitor whether that actually happens. Their goal is to have roughly eighty percent of spending within ten percent of average ROAS. If they increase a platform and it falls fifteen percent below average, something went wrong. AppLovin has consistently stayed within this range as they've increased spend, validating their analysis.

For advertisers just starting on AppLovin with the October first self-service launch, Jared's recommendations are to focus on end cards as the closer and think about them as the opposite of a hook. They should use creative diversity and not assume that what works on Meta or TikTok will work on AppLovin. Expanding into new creative types and allowing the platform to pick up on what's working is important.

Regarding team dynamics and motivation, Jared emphasizes that culture and mindset are paramount at Resident. Everyone wants to perform and kill it. They work every holiday including President's Day, Memorial Day, Labor Day, and of course Black Friday and Cyber Monday. If someone doesn't have that performance mindset, they won't succeed. The company is so lean that you can't hide. If you're not performing or your work product isn't up to speed, it shows quickly.

What keeps people motivated is access to results and data. Everyone can check results and see the impact of their work every day, especially over tent poles and holidays. During those events, people are checking results every hour. The company is distributed globally with team members from California to Tel Aviv, which allows them to work almost twenty-four seven. People take the baton from their colleagues when their shift ends, creating a relay where everyone is working toward the same goal.

Resident's largest office in the world is in Tel Aviv. Their data and analytics team, product development team, and website development team are all based there. The growth sphere in Tel Aviv isn't as developed as in the US with respect to performance marketers, so finding people takes time, but when you find them, they're phenomenal. What really sets them apart is their data and analytics team spanning from data engineering to data scientists to analysts.

Jared believes in being a bigger fish in a smaller pond rather than a medium-sized fish in a really big pond. Working with Meta, Google, and TikTok teams in Tel Aviv allows them to have better relationships and more attention than they might get as a larger account in a bigger market.
