# Limited Supply: Landing Page Tips for E-Commerce

Welcome back to another episode of Limited Supply. Today is going to be a quicker episode focused entirely on landing page tips drawn from looking back at numerous landing page studies, customer acquisition funnels, and performance marketing for brands without strong brand awareness.

The inspiration for these tips comes from a particular philosophy about hiring and learning. When looking to hire a growth marketer, the best candidates often come from companies without strong brand awareness. These people have had to work much harder to hit their conversion goals and ROAS targets because fewer things work when your brand is less recognized. They've had to try more things and test more aggressively. With that same mindset, these landing page tips are designed to work whether you have a well-established brand or not.

It's important to note that these tips impact your conversion rate once someone lands on your site. If you're having issues with CPMs or click-through rates on your ads, that's a separate issue about ad creative that warrants its own discussion. For today, let's focus on what happens on the landing page itself.

## The Power of Social Proof

The number one thing most people don't do is leverage enough social proof, and often this is because they're drinking their own Kool-Aid. When you're the one selling a product, you wear it, you see it every day, you touch it constantly. Everything seems obvious to you. But you already believe in the product, which is why you're not the best person to audit your own landing pages.

Landing pages exist to accelerate the conversion funnel—to speed up the process of someone learning what you're selling, why you're selling it, what it does for them, how quickly it gets there, and how it compares to other options. But many people forget to include social proof.

Show people proof that thousands of others have bought this without issues. Show them proof that your benefits actually work for real people. This can take many forms: customer reviews with names and photos, simple statistics like "40,000 bottles sold on TikTok Shop," badges from publications like Allure or Cosmopolitan, videos of influencers, or screenshots of comments from Facebook and TikTok. Real customer reviews, comments, screenshots, and videos of customers opening boxes and using products constitute the most powerful social proof.

Interestingly, publications and paid celebrities are among the least conversion-impacting forms of social proof you can use. Instead, focus on things that feel authentic and real to people—customer comments, user-generated content, and genuine testimonials.

## The Critical Role of Copy

Copy is eighty percent of what you sell, and this is true for two reasons. First, if your copy looks complicated at a glance—if it's chunky, paragraph-heavy, filled with jargon and buzzwords—people will immediately be turned off. However, if your copy is easy to understand and displayed with clear hierarchy, you'll see immediate improvement.

By hierarchy, I mean you should be able to clearly distinguish the headline from the sub-headline, from the bullet points, from body copy, from copy beneath icons, from the price point, and from the returns guarantee beneath the CTA. Visual clarity is the first step.

The second step is ensuring your copy is actually good. It needs to be punchy, savvy, focused on benefits rather than value propositions. Your copy is your positioning—it's how you bring someone in.

Your copy should throw something out there that people can hook onto and lock in with. If you're selling rice cakes to moms, your copy might be "Mom's favorite afterschool snack." That gives a mom reading your page something to connect with immediately. She thinks, "That's me. I'm interested."

There's something to learn here from YouTubers and vloggers who are excellent at speaking to their camera in a way that feels like they're talking directly to you. They create something relatable, attainable, or aspirational that viewers can connect with. That's what builds loyalty. Your landing page copy should do the same thing.

## Designing for Mobile First

Designing for desktop is like having a surround sound system without a subwoofer. It still works, but it's not optimal. The vast majority of your traffic—likely eighty-plus percent—comes from mobile. There are rare exceptions where brands have more desktop traffic or where discovery happens on mobile but conversion on desktop, but these are typically higher AOV items with lots of customization options.

Write your copy for mobile. Design for mobile. When you design for mobile, it's more efficient, and you can then throw it into desktop and rearrange things or add elements to fill space. But if you design for desktop first and then try to fit everything into mobile, your mobile experience won't be optimized.

The biggest tell-tale sign of desktop-first design is when the CTA isn't even above the fold on mobile. Same issue happens in shop sections of landing pages—you want the title at the top and CTA at the bottom without requiring scrolling. But if it's designed on desktop and squeezed into mobile, it becomes too long and requires excessive scrolling.

When you design mobile-first, you also think about mobile-specific elements like optimized cards, improved navigation, a better add-to-cart experience, and a more optimized variant selector. Challenge yourself to design the harder way first into mobile, then expand to desktop.

## Making Pricing and Offers Stand Out

Make your pricing, discounts, and offers much more noticeable and bigger. The bigger your price displays, the less it feels like you're hiding something, and conversion rates actually go up. Use different colors, bold fonts, highlights, stickers, or colored CTAs with contrasting outlines—orange CTA with black outline, red CTA with black outline. Do this within your brand colors, but prioritize conversion.

If you spend thirty minutes every couple of days looking at ads manager and seeing where winning brands are driving traffic, you'll notice that successful performance marketing brands care about their brand aesthetic but care much more about profit. You should too.

Beyond just making prices big, use supportive copy like "a dollar a day with this offer" or "less than five dollars a day to use X" or "less than two dollars a day to achieve X." This helps people do the math in their heads and justify the purchase, which helps drive conversion.

## The Power of Imagery

Pictures do tremendous work that shouldn't be discounted. Many brands do beautiful photoshoots where their products look stunning, but that's appealing to someone already drinking the Kool-Aid, not to someone discovering the brand for the first time.

Look at how insurance companies, alcohol brands, telecom companies, and cable TV companies approach imagery. Their pictures always focus on joyous times, events, or showing the good life—what life will be like with their product. Your imagery should do the same.

If you're selling a greens powder, don't just show the greens powder. Everyone knows what greens powder is. Show what your morning looks like when you wake up and have the greens powder. Show what your afternoon looks like because you had it. Show how life looks with the greens powder in it. Maybe you're in a workout fit on a SoulCycle bike. The product contributes to a lifestyle that's aspirational, something people are trying to achieve.

This principle also applies to illustrations, icons, graphs, and charts. If you have educational or scientific content, supplements, or anything similar, these visual elements are great places to showcase illustrations and icons. The more you can use numbers, the better. Numbers make information feel more trustworthy and real. Think about all those viral Buzzfeed articles and quizzes that always had numbers in the title—that's the same principle.

## Pop-ups and Email Capture

When people land on your site, have a pop-up subscribe form ready, but don't show it to people coming from your email service provider like Klaviyo. However, for people coming from Facebook, organic, Instagram, or other referral sources, make it a very value-added experience.

Everything on your site comes down to answering one question in the customer's mind: what's in it for me? With pop-ups, offer something that excites them. It could be a discount, a chance to enter a raffle, a free product if they hit a certain AOV threshold, or anything else that makes them want to enter their email.

Once you have their email and phone number, ask for more zero-party data—birthdays, location, product preferences, whether they're shopping for men's, women's, activewear, loungewear, etc. The more adjectives and traits you can add to people's profiles, the better. As AI becomes more integrated into marketing workflows, this zero-party data becomes increasingly valuable. It's inevitable that AI will become more ingrained in everyone's processes, and having rich customer profiles will make everything work better in the future.

## ADA Compliance

Make sure your landing page is fully ADA compliant. Many attorneys specifically look at Facebook ads and hunt for non-compliant landing pages to sue. While ADA compliance apps exist, they don't protect you from lawsuits. Anyone can still sue you.

The only real protection is working with a law firm that specializes in ADA cases. They can run the same reports that litigious attorneys use and provide you with evidence—timestamps, date stamps across different devices and screen sizes—proving your compliance. When frivolous cases come, you have documented evidence of compliance rather than just settling for ten to twenty thousand dollars.

## Simplifying Checkout

Don't make the checkout experience more complicated than necessary. Remember, you use landing pages to make the sales process much faster. A customer should never need more than two clicks from the landing page to reach checkout.

The checkout UI should reiterate the benefits that made someone click the ad and come to your page in the first place. If you're running multiple targeted or segmented campaigns with different audiences, focus on your highest-performing evergreen benefits. Include maybe one or two reviews in your checkout process and don't overcomplicate it.

Remind people during checkout that they're not the first to make this purchase, highlight your returns policy, showcase that many people are happy with their purchase, and reiterate why people love and buy from your brand.

## Offer Optimization

When you have multiple offers on a landing page—whether they're different discounts or different bundles—put the best offer at the top. If you have fifteen percent, twenty percent, and twenty-five percent discounts, put the twenty-five percent at the top, not the fifteen percent.

This isn't about deception since the option is still visible at the bottom. You're just buying yourself ten seconds of attention so someone sees "twenty-five percent off" and decides to scroll down to see more. You're not being deceitful, just strategically purchasing attention.

## Comparison Charts

Include a comparison chart. There's literally never been a comparison chart that didn't increase landing page conversion rates. In one test with a subscription-focused brand, the subscription LTV was significantly higher with the comparison chart.

This makes perfect sense. In competitive spaces like meal kits, supplements, probiotics, and beauty products, people know they'll get aggressive customer acquisition pitches from competitors. Comparison charts address this head-on. They say, "Yes, all these guys will come after you, but here's why we're better." This clarity likely contributes to higher subscriber LTV because it reduces uncertainty.

## UI Design Investment

Invest in and elevate your UI design. You don't need branding agency-level design, but you absolutely cannot have an overseas Fiverr-level landing page. People don't necessarily look for beautiful design, but they look for an aesthetic that says, "I'm not going to scam you once you get my credit card."

Too many landing pages feel like potential scams, which is another reason social proof and benefit restatement are so important. Invest in UI design without going overboard. Focus on conversion and UX. Get nice icons from flat icon libraries and customize them in Figma if needed—change colors, weights, whatever makes sense. The more elements that are specific to you—whether that's photography, copy, illustrations, icons, or a nicer-looking comparison chart—the more the brand feels accepted and well-liked.

## Addressing Buyer's Remorse

Add buyer's remorse-friendly language to your page. Answer key questions like: If I buy today, does it ship today? When will the product arrive? What's the return and refund policy?

The North Star question to solve for is something like: "If I order this and my girlfriend or boyfriend thinks I'm stupid when they see what I bought, can I return it?" As long as you cover all aspects of that question, you'll address everything you need to. Make it crystal clear that there's a path to return the product if they're not satisfied.

## Using Quotes Strategically

Use customer quotes to call out benefits, not value propositions. Don't have quotes saying, "I love the teal color bottle and it's machine washable." That's a feature. Instead, use something like, "Upgrading to the 64-ounce tumbler changed my life. I drink more water today than ever before and feel much healthier with more energy."

You're not focusing on teal and machine washable—you're focusing on the lifestyle transformation. It's changed my life, I drink more water, I have more energy, I feel healthier, maybe I'm sleeping better because of proper hydration. Sell the lifestyle someone could live, not just the features someone who already knows the brand would appreciate.

## Merchandising Your Landing Page

Organize your product page like you would organize a physical storefront. Look at how brands like Parachute, Buck Mason, Little Words Project, and Brooklinen organize their physical spaces. The key principle is to merchandise better.

In physical stores, things near the door sell faster because stores know door placement drives higher take rates. It's like how Target puts doorbuster and dollar products at the front. Organize your landing page to focus on things you want to push.

Get clear about what drives new customers, what drives repeat customers, and what drives lapsed customers. Then organize your landing pages and website accordingly. Everything can be on your site, but be strategic about what's most prominent.

## Reducing Text

Whenever possible, reduce text. Say the same thing with fewer words. Fewer is always better.

## Being Truthful About Limited-Time Offers

If you have something that's actually a limited-time offer, highlight it. But if it's an evergreen offer, do not say it's limited-time. You'll get in trouble for that.

## Including FAQs

Always include an FAQ section. Like comparison charts, FAQs are an easy way for someone to quickly understand differences or answer questions in their mind. Look at common questions left on ads in comment sections, Instagram DMs, customer service interactions, and what you hear in real conversations. Answer those questions.

Some brands have product-specific FAQs, some have brand-wide FAQs, and some have both. For example, The Skinny Confidential has FAQs specific to individual products and broader FAQs across their entire range.

## Page Load Speed

Make sure your page loads extremely fast. Run what might be called a "grandma test"—imagine your grandma loading this landing page on a tiny iPhone that's four or five years old, with a subpar screen and an older 4G or LTE connection rather than 5G. If it loads slowly, you've got a problem.

Everything needs to be locked: easy to read, nice visual hierarchy, copy that's easy to understand and skim, illustrations and icons to break up text, fast loading speeds, and a straightforward path to checkout once someone decides what they want.

## Testing Variations

Now that you have all the pieces you need, duplicate your page and test a different headline. Move sections around. Try putting the founder story at the top instead of the bottom. Try putting the shop section at the very top instead of the third or fourth section.

There's a lot of conflicting data about what's "best," so don't get caught up in universal rules. Instead, test your specific variations. It's so easy to test, especially with Shopify where you can duplicate with one click and quickly edit headlines with your keyboard.

You might find that for your specific product, price point, and customer segment, the optimal layout is different than for another brand. The only way to know is to test.
