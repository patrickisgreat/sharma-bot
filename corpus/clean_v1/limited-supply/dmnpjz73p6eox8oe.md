# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Golshan Kumar, CEO and co-founder of Swishables, sat down to discuss his approach to building consumer brands with a focus on distribution, unit economics, and emotional brand building. Swishables is a liquid mouthwash pre-filled in a recyclable sachet designed for on-the-go consumption—those moments between coffee and meetings when you need a quick breath refresher.

Golshan brought significant experience from his previous venture selling bottled water for nearly a decade, which fundamentally shaped his philosophy on what makes a good business idea. When evaluating new opportunities, his first question is always about scalability: how many distribution points can you realistically build for this product? This distribution-first mindset led him naturally into the Swishables space, where the unit economics made immediate sense. Transitioning from heavy aluminum bottles to lightweight sachets solved a critical shipping and manufacturing problem that had haunted his previous business.

A core principle Golshan emphasizes is that having a good business idea requires having actual good unit economics. Too many founders focus on growth without understanding the financial validity of their model. The water business taught him that when you're dealing with shipping constraints and expensive materials like aluminum—especially with tariffs—you need to have a magnifying glass on the fundamental economics before committing to a product direction.

When it comes to identifying white space, Golshan thinks differently than many founders. Rather than competing on existing retail shelves, the question becomes: how do you create entirely new shelf space? This extends beyond traditional D2C versus retail thinking. Most brands default to one of two channels, but Golshan sees this as a red ocean mentality. Blue ocean strategies involve creating entirely new channels where competition is minimal. For Swishables, this means thinking about golf courses, restaurants, airlines, clubs, and other venues where a portable mouthwash makes sense as an alternative to traditional mints. The beauty of these alternative channels is that they don't operate on a pay-to-play model the way beverage distribution does, which opens up opportunities that wouldn't exist in water.

On the question of copycats, Golshan acknowledges that imitators are inevitable and ultimately a compliment to good product design. The real focus should be on building brand moat, not fighting copycats. What separates winners from followers is team quality, distribution diversity, speed of execution, and the intangible culture you build around your brand. Two identical products with different teams will produce dramatically different results.

Regarding the retail versus D2C question, Golshan believes there's no universal right answer, but he leans toward D2C first in most cases. The advantage is collecting data to understand where you're winning, what works, and what needs improvement before approaching retailers. Retailers accept new brands more readily when you have traction data. Additionally, there's a critical strategic consideration many founders miss: once you set your price floor at a major retailer like Walmart, every other retailer will benchmark against that. Setting your pricing incorrectly at the outset can constrain your entire business for years.

Retail itself operates as a completely different beast with different unit economics, different resource requirements, and different timelines. Getting into retail involves working with brokers who facilitate introductions to retail buyers. The broker's job is securing the meeting and preparing the pitch. Buyers care about one thing: whether your product will move off their shelf. They have targets to hit, and if a brand underperforms, it's typically off the shelf within twelve months. Some brokers work on retainer, others on retainer plus commission. The key is having the right broker who understands your category and has relationships with the specific buyers you need to reach.

When it comes to marketing for retail, the most successful approach treats discovery channels as marketing opportunities that create demand before consumers even hit the store. If someone tries your product at a hotel or airport, they're far more likely to purchase it at retail later. This creates a flywheel effect. Additionally, retailers are essentially real estate firms—every square foot has dollar value. Getting off the shelf, not just being on it, is the real game. This means creative placement strategies, promotional support, and retail media investment that goes beyond the standard shelf space.

Golshan's philosophy on brand building centers on emotional outcomes rather than features and benefits. While good brands sell features, great brands sell emotional outcomes. Swishables isn't selling a mouthwash; it's selling confidence. The confidence that comes from knowing your breath is fresh before an important meeting. He uses Corona, Nike, and Apple as examples of brands that have perfected this approach. Corona doesn't sell beer; it sells the sensory experience of the beach. Nike doesn't sell running shoes; it sells the feeling of being faster. This emotional dimension is what creates true brand moat and drives infinite lifetime value, because the customer isn't just returning for a product—they're returning for how the product makes them feel.

On team building, Golshan structures his company around experts in different domains rather than hiring traditional employees for every function. His cap table includes no passive investors; everyone contributes specific value in their area of expertise. This approach allows for lean scaling without excessive overhead. His immediate hiring priorities are operations to handle backend scaling constraints, and someone on the e-commerce side to serve as a liaison. The team itself is a product that requires as much attention to detail as the physical product.

When asked about the most overrated metric for early-stage CPG brands, Golshan points to a widespread obsession with growth at the expense of profitability. Many founders adopt a "we'll make money later" mentality, raising capital and prioritizing growth metrics. But when circumstances shift—tariffs spike, credit becomes expensive, market conditions change—that mentality collapses. Profitability and contribution margin should always be a focus, even for growth-oriented companies.

More specifically, he challenges the standard use of ROAS (return on ad spend) as a metric. ROAS is too broad, too easy to manipulate, and doesn't reflect actual business health. Instead, brands should focus on POAS—profit on ad spend—which measures actual profit generated from advertising rather than just gross return. These are two fundamentally different KPIs, and the distinction matters enormously. A business can have a 3:1 ROAS while losing money on advertising if margins are poor. POAS forces alignment between marketing performance and actual business profitability, which is the only metric that truly matters long-term.
