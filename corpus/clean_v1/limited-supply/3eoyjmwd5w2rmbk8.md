# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

OpenStorm was founded to serve entrepreneurs and fill a market void. Over two million businesses are built on Shopify, but most are too small for private equity and not growing fast enough for venture capital. These entrepreneurs spend sixty hours a week running supply chain, customer experience, business operations, and marketing across all facets of eCommerce, yet they lack good exit options when they're ready to move on. OpenStorm's mission is to inspire entrepreneurs and shoppers to find something new—giving entrepreneurs certainty of an offer and an exit when ready, while increasing product discoverability for consumers.

The fundamental difference in OpenStorm's approach is that they don't view a business as just a spreadsheet. Rather than offering simple EBITDA multiples and hoping to cash out at higher multiples later, OpenStorm aims to become eCommerce operations experts for these brands so they can reach their full potential. This philosophy shapes everything from how they price businesses to how they integrate and operate them afterward.

OpenStorm operates two core products. Acquisition involves buying a business outright, giving merchants the freedom to move on to their next venture. Drive is for merchants not quite ready to leave their business but needing help running it. With Drive, OpenStorm runs end-to-end operations and pays back guaranteed income to the merchant over a twelve-month term. Many Drive cohort businesses have eventually turned into acquisition offers as merchants became more comfortable with the model.

The company focuses on Shopify businesses doing between two hundred fifty thousand and ten million dollars in annual sales. They look for growing, healthy businesses with positive cash flow and good margins. They specifically target products that are differentiated and loved by customers, evidenced through reviews, customer support interactions, repeat purchase rates, and customer behavior. The healthier the unit economics, the better, since that's harder to change than other operational levers.

When acquiring a business, OpenStorm conducts thorough diligence. The company has learned valuable lessons from early acquisitions—some businesses came in without barcoded products, requiring team members to fly out and label everything. These learnings have shaped better processes. They look beyond just reviewing data to talking with founders and staff about actual operations.

OpenStorm's operational approach differs from traditional aggregators in several key ways. Approximately forty percent of the company is technical—engineers, data scientists, and data analysts. This technical focus allows specialized operators to have ten times the leverage they would otherwise. The alternative leads to scenarios like some Amazon aggregators acquiring two hundred fifty businesses but needing twelve hundred employees, economics that don't work at scale.

One example of their technology is the promo engine. One person or team can load an entire year's promotional calendar, discount codes, email templates, and other elements for a brand, and the system automatically updates hero banners and offer activations across the portfolio without manual intervention. They monitor performance and only make tweaks when needed.

Another significant technology investment is in customer support AI. OpenStorm automates between sixty to seventy percent of all customer interactions while maintaining high satisfaction scores. They've built tools for customer service agents that provide heads-up displays and AI assistance, allowing agents to ask questions to an AI database rather than escalate routine issues. This enables them to avoid expanding headcount while actually improving customer satisfaction and response times.

OpenStorm operates nearly fifty brands today and has served almost two million customers. Rather than assigning pods to specific brands, they prioritize work based on each store's needs. If conversion rates need improvement at certain stores or are benchmarked as underperforming, the storefront team gets prioritized. Similarly, some brands are meta-heavy while others rely more on Google. Some need work in retention and promotional calendars while others need offer or pricing optimization.

The channel mix has evolved significantly. While meta once represented eighty to eighty-five percent of customer acquisition, OpenStorm has diversified substantially. One of their most popular brands, Jack Archer, was seventy to eighty percent meta when acquired but now sees Google generating thirty-five to forty percent of revenue. CRM and repeat purchase revenue have also grown as a share of total sales.

Cross-selling between brands happens but isn't forced. At one point, five percent of revenue came from cross-selling, and it occurs naturally when new products launch and customers occasionally discover adjacent products they want to buy. However, they're thoughtful about this—they won't show customers products that don't fit their interests just for the sake of generating cross-sell revenue.

The vast majority of OpenStorm's business is pure D2C through Shopify. While some brands maintain small Amazon presences, most sales come through Shopify. That's where OpenStorm feels they have the most leverage and opportunity.

Regarding vendor relationships, OpenStorm negotiates bulk discounts with many vendors. When they can tell vendors they're effectively fifty accounts, that carries significant negotiating power. They work with partners like Klaviyo, Shopify, and others to secure better pricing that makes sense for operating so many brands.

The process of selling to OpenStorm begins at open.store, where founders fill out basic information about their business and connect their Shopify and ad account data. An automated pricing process runs in the background, analyzing margins, scalability in various ad platforms, performance trends, and customer loyalty metrics. An offer is presented within roughly twenty-four hours.

When constructing offers, OpenStorm considers what they think they can generate in EBITDA from the business in the next twelve months under their ownership. They give offers to a wide variety of merchants—hyper-growth companies command higher offers, while slower-growth or stagnant companies still get liquidity offers. The margin profile, repeat customer behavior, macro trends, and where OpenStorm can apply their operational strengths all factor into the equation.

It's not a simple EBITDA multiple. OpenStorm is very open to conversations with entrepreneurs about opportunities they see in their own business. If a founder hasn't tried certain channels, hasn't expanded product range, or underinvested in landing page optimization, OpenStorm considers those factors. The goal is to understand how well OpenStorm can run the business given their advantages in capital, technology, and team size.

All offers are non-binding. Some entrepreneurs have come back multiple times over years, essentially using the process as a health check on their business. OpenStorm wants to build these relationships early and provide direct, opinionated feedback that helps entrepreneurs either run more successful businesses or receive higher offers in the future if they're not ready to sell immediately.

Across their portfolio, OpenStorm sees interesting patterns about consumer spending and market sentiment. CPMs on Facebook continue rising even with creative refresh and wins. Because of this, OpenStorm has heavily invested in building channel expertise beyond Facebook. Successful merchants today need far more tools in their toolkit than just Facebook ads and landing page tweaks. They need to excel at CRM, offer optimization, pricing strategies, bundle strategies, Google, affiliates, and organic social to generate cheaper impressions.

The macro data suggests consumers aren't tapping out yet, even with high credit card debt. OpenStorm isn't seeing an overall pullback in purchasing, so their focus remains on maintaining strong spend efficiencies, diverse channel mix, and continuous improvement across every channel to combat inflationary pressures.

OpenStorm has developed several interesting technology products beyond their internal tools. Gumdrop is an influencer platform where creators sign up weekly to see drops of products from OpenStorm brands. Creators can select products they like, receive them, post content with CTAs, and earn commissions. High-performing creators get approached for more collaborations. This allows OpenStorm to scalably unlock influencer partnerships for many brands.

They also have consumer apps for their bigger brands like Jack Archer, where repeat purchase rates are high. These apps help customers discover latest offers and the full catalog through push notifications.

On the horizon, generative AI in creative is a major focus. Creating content and assets for nearly fifty stores represents a huge opportunity for AI assistance.

OpenStorm works with an incredible network of vendors in the Shopify ecosystem. They use tools like Drip for price testing and intelligence, and Fermat for performance marketers to quickly mock up landing pages for testing new concepts.

Some OpenStorm brands are on TikTok Shop, though the creative requirements and technical constraints have prevented wholesale adoption. New channels like TikTok Shop occasionally have limitations like allowing only five businesses per single EIN, which creates friction for operators with OpenStorm's use case.

The company tests new technologies constantly. With so many stores, they can try things simultaneously without interrupting performance too much. Functional experts make recommendations based on conference learnings, industry buzz, and peer feedback. If a tool shows significant performance improvements on certain stores, they roll it out to others.

For entrepreneurs building consumer brands between five and fifty million in revenue, the key insight from OpenStorm's experience is to never forget about your most loyal customers. Channel mix, customer support, and customer experience management should always be strong. Retention is always a strength, never a weakness. Taking care of customers while building proper metrics to track as you scale makes the journey easier and sets you up for the next level.

Scaling from one hundred thousand to one million in annual sales is different from going from one million to five million, which differs from five to fifty million. The constant through all these stages is ensuring you're maintaining excellent customer experience while focusing on other growth areas. You can never cheat customer experience.

When building teams to scale a business, bringing in incredible operators is essential. Everyone should understand how their work ladders up to individual store performance. Educating folks from customer support to marketing to supply chain about how they directly impact the P&L and growth creates better alignment and motivation.

OpenStorm distinguishes itself by being a combination of aggregator, agency, and software company. They're not just buying companies or consulting, and they're not just building copycat software. They build legitimate, useful technology—like the ability to change fifty different website heroes for promotions simultaneously. The company's strength comes from this unique positioning and genuine technology innovation that solves real operational challenges in scaling eCommerce businesses.
