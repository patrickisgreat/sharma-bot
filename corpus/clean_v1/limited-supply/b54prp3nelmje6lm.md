# Limited Supply - Season 8, Episode 5

Nik and Moiz discuss emerging trends in direct-to-consumer brands, particularly how Gen Z-focused companies are taking different approaches to growth compared to traditional DTC pioneers.

## The Rise of HALARA and TikTok-Native Brands

HALARA is a Singapore-based athleisure brand that emerged during COVID, capitalizing on the boom in comfortable, fast-fashion clothing. The founder identified a gap in the market where existing options like Lululemon and Aloo were expensive, and built a business around making similar products at accessible prices using a Shein-like fast fashion model.

What makes HALARA's growth particularly interesting is their approach to social media. Rather than running traditional evergreen ad campaigns, they identify influencers and customers who are organically posting about their products with good engagement, then pay to amplify those posts to millions more people. This strategy has generated over ten billion views on TikTok and fifty million views on YouTube, far exceeding established brands like Aritzia.

On their e-commerce site, HALARA employs several conversion optimization tactics. When customers arrive, they're presented with a spin-the-wheel popup guaranteeing a win, which significantly increases email capture rates. The cart experience includes add-on deals with steep discounts, but crucially, the discount is only available once the customer adds sixty dollars more in products. This creates an additional incentive to browse and add items beyond the initial purchase. Throughout the checkout experience, the brand emphasizes savings by calling out how much money customers are saving compared to strikethrough prices.

HALARA also uses product discovery strategically, showing "you may also like" sections with bundled offers. On individual product pages, they don't repeat the cart discounts but instead offer different incentives, like free gifts at certain spending thresholds. These thresholds appear to be set slightly above common average order values to encourage incremental purchases.

The site drives approximately four and a half million monthly visitors through a combination of TikTok shop performance, organic social content, and their main website. Their TikTok shop maintains exceptional performance metrics, with eighty-three percent four to five star ratings, ninety-two percent of orders shipped within forty-eight hours, and ninety-nine percent customer service response time within twenty-four hours.

## The Shift in How Gen Z Brands Grow

Traditional DTC brands like Everlane, Bonobos, Native, and Warby Parker built their businesses through large-scale marketing campaigns, slowly taking market share one impression at a time. The new generation of Gen Z-focused brands is taking a fundamentally different approach.

Rather than launching with big campaigns, brands like Crown Affair and Strawberry Milk Mob are building niche audiences first, validating their products within those niches, and only then expanding more broadly. Crown Affair built an incredible brand presence within its niche before suddenly appearing nationwide at Sephora.

Strawberry Milk Mob represents an even more interesting case study. Founded in 2018 with a mission to make cute bikinis at accessible prices, the brand initially struggled with sales. After attempting traditional content strategies on TikTok without success, the founder pivoted to a lifestyle-focused content approach. She created a series called "reason number forty-two why I love men" that used humor to generate engagement. These posts would include observations like noting when a partner fills up the gas tank overnight, or jokes about toilet seats left up. This approach generated genuine engagement and value for followers, drawing them to discover what the brand was actually selling.

The strategy worked dramatically. Strawberry Milk Mob now generates between seventy thousand and one hundred thousand dollars in revenue on drop days without running any paid advertising, relying entirely on organic social media reach. The brand's annual revenue is estimated at eight to ten million dollars, despite having a bare-bones website with minimal optimization and no obvious call-to-action buttons on the homepage.

The shift represents a fundamental change in leverage dynamics. Where it used to be true that whoever had the largest audience held the most power, it's now whoever is best at creating engaging content that will win.

## The Danny Duncan and Influencer Merch Model

A parallel trend exists with influencer merchandise stores. YouTuber Danny Duncan's merch site exemplifies this approach. These stores often feature minimal design optimization and unrefined Shopify templates, yet generate enormous revenue. Many of these influencer-run stores do between ten and twenty million dollars in annual revenue despite seemingly sloppy presentation and customer service.

What's notable is that these influencers don't appear to care much about design quality or polished customer experience. They simply put their pictures on a basic template and let their existing audience drive sales. This works because the influencer audience already has loyalty and trust. The merchandise is almost secondary to the influencer's primary content and appeal.

## Private Label and Retail Strategy

Major retailers including Walmart, Target, Costco, Trader Joe's, and Aldi have built significant revenue streams through private label brands. Trader Joe's, which produces no products itself, derives approximately eighty-five percent of store sales from private label goods. Costco sells private label vodka that is actually Grey Goose. These retailers benefit from both margin expansion and customer loyalty.

Walmart recently launched Better Goods, its biggest new private label brand in twenty years, with three hundred items mostly priced around five dollars. The company also operates Great Value, Members Mark, Market Side, Freshness Guaranteed, All Roy, and Parents Choice brands.

The opportunity for expansion into additional categories appears significant. Sports equipment like basketballs and tennis balls, camping and outdoor gear, generic supplements, major appliances, and drinkware like Stanley cups seem like natural candidates for private label expansion. These categories currently rely on established brand names like Wilson, Penn, or the original Stanley, yet private label versions could capture price-sensitive customers while maintaining quality standards.

The key to brand survival against private label competition is creating genuine differentiation that customers value enough to pay a premium for. Charmin succeeds by owning "softness" in the toilet paper category. Dove owns moisturization in soap. Oreos maintain loyalty because customers specifically want the genuine product. Brands must create clear, defensible positioning that justifies a price premium over generic alternatives.

## The Consignment Question

An interesting strategic question emerges around why major retailers don't use consignment models more aggressively with newer brands. Walmart and Target typically pay suppliers for inventory within thirty to sixty days. These retailers could instead only pay for products once they sell, keeping cash on their balance sheets and potentially earning interest on the float.

This approach wouldn't work with established powerhouses like Procter and Gamble, which has negotiating leverage. However, emerging brands seeking retail distribution might accept consignment terms to gain access to store shelves. Retailers could even establish internal financing departments to help suppliers finance purchase orders, taking a percentage of sales in return. The amount of capital tied up across thousands of stores waiting for slower-moving inventory represents billions of dollars that retailers could put to better use. The question of why major retailers haven't implemented this strategy more broadly remains unanswered, suggesting there may be structural or competitive reasons for the current payment model.
