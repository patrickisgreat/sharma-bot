## Increasing AOV

The first question comes from Deb, who asks about the best way to increase average order value. Increasing AOV starts with the messaging that leads somebody into the brand and carries its way through the entire commerce experience. You need to message accordingly as you're driving people to the brand. For example, supplement brands do a really good job of this by encouraging somebody to subscribe or wait to see results until their third order or maybe they're nine weeks in. But it really starts with the messaging, focusing on things like the routine, consistency, and the effect of compounding products mixed together or used together.

As you click and get to whether it's a landing page, listicle, product page, or homepage, you want to focus on bundles. You want to create a customer offer that brings somebody in and allows them to sample enough products or try enough variants so they can figure out what is right for them to subscribe to. Bundles are a great lever.

When AB testing your product page, be careful that you're optimizing for the right thing. There was a test earlier where they thought they achieved a higher conversion rate by adding the Apple Pay with Shop Pay button on their product page. But what they didn't realize is their revenue per session was actually down by about 60%, even though conversion rate was up. That's not the right thing to look for.

You have to put yourself in the mindset of a consumer. What is actually useful to them? What is actually helpful to them? Think through your own product offering. Is that actually going to benefit them in the long run? Because if it's not, then that's not something they're going to continue to come back and purchase.

You want to make sure that the value exchange is even, meaning what they're paying for feels even in terms of the value they're getting in return. After that, you can figure out whether you're upselling, cross selling, testing out bundles, or testing out kits. If you go to the Salud website and try to buy a single stick pack, you're going to see a button that says you can upgrade your order into a bundle because you're going to get a discount. Those types of things are great and are great things to AB test on your product pages. But again, be very careful that you are optimizing toward a certain metric, whether it's AOV, revenue per session, or UPT (units per transaction) or number of items in the cart. Stay consistent with what you're measuring.

Jackson, the men's jewelry brand, does a really good job with this. If you add something to the cart, you see a very nice pop-up module to add something else. Plunge, the ice cold plunge, if you add a plunge to the cart without adding the filters, they do a nice job showing you a filters upsell. Their messaging there is great—"yes, I want clean water or no, I'm fine with dirty water." It taps into the psychology a little bit.

Another example is thinking about how Rebuy sells a lot of their stuff, which is in-cart upsells. A lot of people don't play with in-checkout upsells enough. If you're customizing your checkout on Shopify Plus, you've got social proof, but also the ability to add complimentary products right in the checkout to your order.

Post-purchase checkout is another opportunity. Once somebody's made a purchase, you've already got their payment token from the order. That payment token has an expiration of about 10, 30, or 60 minutes. The post-purchase upsell plays on that. You give the opportunity to get the same product or a different product at a discounted price and get somebody to add it on. They don't have to re-enter their credit card info because you've already got that from the purchase that just happened. What that does from an architecture standpoint is delay the order from being fulfilled. It lets the customer choose if they want the upsell. If they say no, the order gets pushed to fulfillment. If they say yes, the order gets modified, the card gets charged, and then it gets pushed to fulfillment.

Outside of tactical marketing stuff, like awareness, content, or seeding, really focus on those bundles and focus on multiple products. Focus on the messaging around why you should have multiple products. A lot of people focus on using Rebuy or upselling into a bundle or creating a build-your-own bundle, but they don't focus on the messaging coming in. They also don't necessarily focus on how those orders or bundles are being pushed out to the fulfillment center, which causes a lot of issues depending on your setup and in fulfillment. Make sure that the way it's feeding in is also being read properly so there's no issues when those orders are being fed into your 3PL.

## Testing New Product Concepts

Chris asks what's the best way to test new product concepts or value propositions before launching the product. There are a couple ways to do it. There's a quick and easy way that requires maybe a few thousand dollars. If your brand already exists, use your brand page and create a landing page and drive traffic and see what happens. You can test email opt-in, engagement of ad to cart, and the actual conversion rate of those products.

If you don't have that set up, you can test one step higher. You can just have different concepts as ads that drive to a page and basically understand click-through rate. That's the easiest way.

To test value props before launching a product, run ads to a landing page or product page and understand what copy works. Make static ads in Figma—you can either get a template online, design them yourself, or find some really good ones and copy them. The copy that takes up most of the static ad is what you're testing. The value props you're testing go in an ad set, and you start running them and see which one gets the highest click-through rate. If you're selling the product already, see which one gets the highest conversion rate.

You want to understand whether it works better as a lifestyle shot, studio shot, UGC shot, black and white versus color, animation versus video. These are things you want to test. The best way to test this is by looking at click-through rate and then, of course, if you have the down-funnel events, you can look at those analytics as well.

If you don't have any dollars to spend but you happen to be good at social media, you can test a lot of this messaging through polls or through understanding click-through rate on swipe ups or link stickers in stories. You can test this with email subject lines and look at the open rate. You can test inside emails, looking at the click rate of who's clicking based on the messaging. All you're looking for is a conversion event and to understand what impact your test has on the conversion event.

## Local Delivery Services for Food and Beverage

Tyrell asks for advice on a food or beverage brand using local delivery services like Uber Eats, DoorDash, or GoPuff. If you're selling as a store on Uber Eats or DoorDash or Postmates, look at what some ghost kitchens are doing. Jeff Bezos or the guy behind Walmart and Jet.com started a food court concept called Wonder. They've got great inspiration for how to do deals, how to leverage offers inside Uber Eats and DoorDash, how to do photography and messaging, garner reviews, decide which ones to focus on and highlight. Even the inserts you get when your order is delivered are worth looking at. There are a number of ghost kitchens that do this much better than most restaurants do.

GoPuff is different because you have a couple of options. If you're in GoPuff's select beta program, you have the ability to actually fulfill an order through GoPuff and collect the order on your Shopify.com website. So you go to your site, you purchase a drink, but then you get it delivered within two hours via GoPuff. There used to be another fulfillment company that did two-hour delivery. It's a very interesting concept and phenomenal for driving sampling, which is huge in beverage because you're trying to sips to lips or cans and hands—you're trying to sample as much as you can.

If you're on places like GoPuff, Instacart, and Amazon Fresh, make sure your listings look really good. Think of your product carousel as a landing page. Think of your descriptions as your supporting copy for that landing page. Start testing ads on GoPuff, Instacart, and Amazon Fresh. They have advertising options, and colleagues have found that they work extremely well. You can pretty much optimize entirely for net new customers, which is amazing as a feature. They do really well for driving sampling and store awareness.

GoPuff has a ton of different inventory options, whether it's throwing a product in as a free insert. For example, last week if you ordered on GoPuff in New York, you got a free half days iced tea. They also have the ability to buy sponsored placements in the app. As soon as you open the app or open into a category, you'll see it there. With Instacart ads, you don't even pay unless somebody clicks. If you're a tequila brand advertising for the keyword gluten-free tequila or flavored tequila, if somebody sees your ad and doesn't click it, you don't even get charged, but you still show up, which is amazing from a brand standpoint.

## Transitioning from DTC to Retail

Chris asks for suggestions on how a brand can navigate the transition from primarily direct consumer to rapidly expanding in retail through paid and owned channels. My recommendation about going into retail is that you shouldn't go into retail unless you feel like you can truly drive sell-through in there. There are instances where brands go into retail and they start clicking. However, that's not normal for most brands. Most brands that go into a Target or Walmart or any sort of retailer, it's really on them to either be able to sell the product like crazy or end up buying a lot of sponsored placements inside the store.

You need to figure out how you're driving traffic. Fundamentally, the entire game or answer to suggestions on how you navigate the transition is that you just need to make sure you can drive traffic. There are a couple different ways with different brands.

One brand started running ads driving to a landing page where you put your phone number in and you're basically told if you go buy this product, we're going to Venmo you five bucks back. You take a picture of the receipt and text it to the company. The company also gets a good understanding of which Target you're buying from and what other products you're getting in this order, and then they'll reimburse you that way.

Another approach is the "Mr. Beast play"—how do you just flood awareness at the top and make sure you have distribution at the bottom to support it?

An alcohol brand we worked with when they launched in Target, Walmart, and Costco started turning their email list into a CRM to drive retail sales. E-commerce didn't become as big of a focus, obviously in alcohol, and in most categories you have the bigger opportunity in retail, not always the case, but in a lot of cases for food and beverage. They started chopping up the email list down to zip codes and understanding exactly where they could drive traffic and how they could do it and what messaging they could push and how they could tie the promotions in retail back to what they're sending online.

Movement Watches, when they launched on Nordstrom, took their two or three most popular styles online, marked them as sold out, and said go buy them here instead. That drove a ton of traffic there and it was great.

You've got to figure out what is that sustainable way to basically drive retail sales. That's driving really good sampling and then driving repeat purchases through people in those different geos. Some of the stuff mentioned is kind of like one shot or very limited time strategic stuff, but may not fit that overall bill.

## Optimal Team Setup for DTC Brands

Jan wants to know what is the most optimal team setup for a brand running DTC in 2025 and what functions should be internalized versus outsourced. It's quite different for each brand, but there are a couple examples of brands that are interesting and staying very lean and efficient.

One brand, which is doing quite well and approaching the nine-figure revenue mark, has four people internally as full-time employees: co-founders, a marketing lead, and a retail sales lead. They recently hired a growth marketer, so now they've got five people and everything else is outsourced. Media buying is outsourced. Creative is outsourced—actually a lot of their creative just comes internally from customers and creators. Measurement is outsourced. Any sort of displays needed, whether for retail or for events and activations, is all outsourced.

Another supplement business approaching the 50 million mark this year, bootstrapped and started about two years ago, has two co-founders. One is entirely focused on marketing and sales, and one's entirely focused on product. They have three or four internal people on the marketing side, two or three people shared with other brands on supply chain and ops, and then agencies for the rest.

Another brand about to launch is a big celebrity beauty brand with three people internally and everybody else's agencies.

The way to think about it is you want the founders obviously internal, and you want your key quarterbacks internal. Everything else, execution-wise, can either be offshore talent, agencies, freelancers, contractors, or fractional roles. You don't necessarily need to have everybody internally. It also depends on how big your revenue is.

If you're doing four million dollars a year in revenue, you probably don't need a full-time UX UI designer. Maybe you just need a contractor or a small agency. If you're doing ten million dollars a year, you're probably not wanting to have an internal function for that. You probably just want to work with an agency to take care of design, dev, QA, and all that.

If you're a brand doing a hundred million dollars and you're constantly making changes to your site because your model is more focused around a drop model, then maybe it makes sense to hire somebody internally to handle that because now you're not paying an agency fee. You're getting the skill level you'd get with a good, well-talented agency.

Agencies are great for testing new stuff. If you're just launching on TV, TikTok, Shopify, or wherever, agencies are great. They bring all the learnings and know exactly what to do and what not to do. There are so many times when talking to brands about what they're doing because you already know the patterns they should follow or what they should do or not do, or who to work with and who not to work with.

## Top of Funnel Strategies and Meta Performance

Brad asks for thoughts on top-of-funnel strategies and its effects on Meta performance. When TV was first started seven years ago, the effects were seen directly. As working with more celebrity brands, they come with their own sort of top-of-funnel awareness, and that clearly showed the power of top-of-funnel and good top-of-funnel strategy.

The way to think about it is like the movie Cars where Lightning McQueen is rebuilding the road when it was smooth and not when it was super bumpy, and then there are cars behind it. The paving of the road is how to think about top-of-funnel and awareness, and the cars behind it is how to think about running Meta ads. You can't run Meta ads in a clear, cost-effective, wallet-friendly way unless you've got good awareness before that or preexisting.

Whether it's TV ads, billboards, large celebrity campaigns with Sydney Sweeney—that's what most people think of when they think of top-of-funnel. But if you want to get a little bit more precise, anything from whitelisting with creators, product seating on TikTok, advertorials—these are all also what's called lower funnel, upper funnel stuff that have similar effects to what you can do on Meta.

When TikTok went away, the cost of CPMs went up on Facebook. Although it's more like correlation or causation, if you're running TikTok well, TikTok is very equivalent to a TV. TikTok is Gen Z's TV in terms of how it drives interest and excitement and influence in the Gen Z mind or brain.

Definitely focus on top-of-funnel strategies. It can be something as simple as dinners and influencer events and activations and advertorials and whitelisting creators, or as crazy as citywide takeover campaigns and billboards and all that stuff. All of that has a direct effect on Meta's performance. You're basically paying for eyeballs that you can't get on your own. If you can get eyeballs on your own, whether that's through word of mouth, organic content, or people talking about you, your Meta ads are just going to convert better.

If you look at old-school retailer brands you see in a mall and look at their Meta accounts, you'll see that their acquisition cost is significantly different. One clothing brand that's direct-to-consumer and has been around for five, six, seven years might have a CPA of 120, while a fortune 500 mall retailer has a CPA of 20. The only difference is that there is significantly more top-of-funnel for the mall brands. They're a recognized name. You don't have to do the work of asking if this is a legit brand, if I'm going to like it, what about returns, what about customer satisfaction. You don't necessarily have to think about any of that.

The same thing with influencer brands. The reason that Feastables or Brian Johnson or Lola V or Love Wellness or any of these brands have lower CPAs than their competitors is simply because the top-of-funnel is basically being done as a byproduct of that celebrity co-founder or operator. You can't obviously recreate that, though you kind of can if you have a good content engine like Kanpai Foods.

## Whitelisting with Creators

Lars asks how to negotiate access for whitelisting with creators and whether there are performance differences between partnership ads and creator-run ads. The legal best route here is running paid partnership ads. Whether you post or you just whitelist a piece of content that the influencer posted and tagged you as a paid partner, that's one way to do it. Obviously there you get all the engagement that comes with that original post.

The second way is you basically import the account into your business manager. There's a tool called Leadz that helps do that with creators and do it quickly. Once you get the account in, you basically work with a creator and pay them a couple thousand bucks. This isn't a famous, famous influencer but a content creator who makes good video ads. Pay them a thousand or a couple thousand bucks. You write out specific hooks, specific talking points, specific outros, specific calls to action, and of course the different B-roll. That allows you to take one creator and make 15 to 20 videos versus just paying for one video and being stuck with that.

Because you like to make 15, 20 videos out of that, the paid partnership stuff is great. But what to do is import their Instagram and or Facebook account and then run whitelisted ads, obviously still tagging paid partner as the brand and all the ads because you have to either do that or mention it in the ad text or the headline in order to avoid being flagged. Then you just run it.

When doing this eight years ago with Sarah Dietschy, she created the content, it was chopped up, and we launched with 96 different variations of ads with different ad copy, different headlines, different hooks. Eventually three or four of them ended up scaling to probably twenty to thirty thousand dollars a day in spend.

You want to make sure that if you've got especially a famous influencer or celebrity, you have context as to why this person is interacting with this random brand that is on an Instagram ad. If you don't, then you're giving somebody the reason to think that something is not real or it's facetious. Give somebody the most context and then let them decide whether or not your brand and product are the right fit for them.

You can also take it one step further and make the coupon code, if you have one, named the creator's name with a discount. Sarah 20, Sarah Peachy 20, whatever it may be. You could also have a landing page where it's got Sarah's favorites as a bundle or Huberman's favorites as a bundle or just speaks to why this person actually likes this product and why they use it. Just give it more context.

## Building Campaign Landing Pages

Lisa asks when and why it's worthwhile building a campaign landing page and the pros and cons of doing this in-house versus with an agency. In personal opinion, the more you can drive traffic to a landing page, the better.

A landing page tends to be different from a homepage or collections page or product page. Those tend to be very templated. Most brands will find a template on Shopify and then sometimes add to it, but most of the time just kind of swap things out for what they want to include on those pages. These pages tend to not include things like comparison charts, "when's my order shipping," a bunch of social proof in the form of UGC videos, publisher reviews, customer written reviews, or screenshots from ad comments.

The reason to like landing pages, especially for paid traffic, is because if you're spending money on that click, bringing somebody to the site, you want to make sure that you include everything this person could possibly need in order to make an educated decision of whether or not they should buy and try your product. You should use a landing page basically anytime you're driving paid traffic, whether it's with influencers, PR ads, billboards, TV commercials. Even if you're doing a promo at a coffee shop and you're giving out cards, you should have a landing page that people are going to so that the experience is continuous versus just randomly landing on a homepage, which has no context as to what the ad said and probably speaks differently in terms of how the ad was speaking about the problem and solution.

The recommendation is to work with an agency to build a number of page templates in Shopify. These should be Shopify page templates that you can easily duplicate and reuse and change the copy, change the imagery, change the offer, the merchandising, and then make all those changes in-house. Whether you have somebody who's offshore, an intern, or a junior person who's helping you whip up new pages based off the five templates that you've made, you could have a build-your-own bundle template, a listicle template, a comparison template, a hero product bundle template, a single SKU product template, and probably more.

If you had those built and put into Shopify, you could very easily click the duplicate button and you've got a whole new page, a whole new listicle that you can fill out and AB test driving traffic to. If you've got that offer bundle landing page, duplicate it, test the new bundle, and AB test and see which one drives a better conversion rate or better overall customer result.

That approach works well because you never want to charge a founder based on usage or a monthly fee to just host their pages. A brand would come and you'd build ten pages and then they would never need you again, which is great for them but not as great for the agency. It's not right to charge people on a usage basis for something that should be living in their own Shopify or in an environment where they can go in and make changes at no cost.
