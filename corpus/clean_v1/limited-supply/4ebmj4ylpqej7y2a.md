# Limited Supply: Episode 6, Season 8

TikTok's algorithm creates highly personalized experiences that extend beyond just video feeds to comments themselves. The platform shows different comments to different users based on their engagement patterns and preferences. For instance, on a coffee video, users who drink coffee might see comments about coffee preferences, while those who don't drink coffee or dislike it would see comments criticizing coffee. This personalization strategy is designed to keep users engaged and deeply invested in the platform.

TikTok Live shopping has emerged as a significant trend, fundamentally shifting how products are being sold on the platform. Rather than traditional QVC-style live streams with hosts presenting products, the new format features cameras pointed directly at products with voiceover narration. These streams lack any on-camera personalities and focus entirely on the product itself. This approach has proven surprisingly effective, with some products selling tens of thousands of units through TikTok Shop.

The products succeeding in this format share common characteristics: they're typically priced between fifteen and thirty-five dollars, have strong brand awareness, and address specific problems that are easy to demonstrate visually. Notable brands leveraging this approach include Halara, Liquid IV, Mary Ruth Organics, and Guru Nanda. These livestreams often feature the same product continuously with someone in the background answering questions via voiceover.

Most of these livestreams are actually run by affiliates rather than the brands themselves. Brands like Liquid IV offer commissions around fifteen percent per sale, while competitors like Mary Ruth Organics offer twenty percent commissions and send free samples to creators. This affiliate-driven model allows anyone with minimal production capability to participate in selling established brands.

There's debate about whether this sales method impacts brand perception negatively. While it moves significant volume, the dropshipper aesthetic of product-focused livestreams may damage brand reputation, particularly for companies positioning themselves as premium or natural products sold through traditional retail channels. The format feels more aligned with budget retailers than premium lifestyle brands.

The rise of Chinese companies like Sheen, Temu, and TikTok in the American market has prompted increased government scrutiny and policy discussions. Sheen faces particular criticism regarding cotton sourcing from regions where labor practices violate human rights standards. However, testing revealed only about two percent of Sheen's cotton comes from problematic sources, compared to a fifteen percent industry standard across major brands. The selective focus on Chinese companies while overlooking similar practices by American companies like Nike or half of Amazon's Chinese sellers raises questions about whether regulatory pressure is driven by legitimate concerns or competitive protectionism.

Most successful American brands fail to adequately pursue international expansion despite significant untapped opportunities. Canada represents approximately ten percent of the American market opportunity and should be easier to enter, yet many brands focus exclusively on saturating the U.S. market. Shopify's data illustrates the international opportunity: Europe represents fifty-four percent of Amazon's traffic, Japan represents twenty-four percent, and Canada represents seven percent. These markets are largely underserved by American DTC brands.

Logistics present the primary barrier to international expansion. Lighter products like supplements or personal care items ship easily internationally, but heavier products require establishing separate supply chains or leveraging existing manufacturing facilities to serve international markets. A significant gap exists for service providers who could quarterback international expansion efforts, helping brands navigate legal requirements, tariff structures, logistics, and market-specific regulations without requiring massive upfront investments.

The widespread adoption of GLP-1 medications like Ozempic, Trulicity, and Monjaro creates a new consumer segment worth serving with specialized products. People using these medications are typically wealthy, predominantly female, and willing to spend significant amounts monthly on their health and appearance. This population experiences reduced appetite and consequent nutritional deficiencies, constipation, and other side effects that could be addressed by specialized supplement formulations. An opportunity exists to create products specifically formulated for Ozempic users, similar to how AG-1 serves specific health-conscious demographics. Products addressing nutritional gaps and specific side effects like constipation could capture significant market share within this expanding user base.
