# Limited Supply: Season 4, Episode 8

When subscriptions became big in the e-commerce space, roughly three or four years ago, the focus shifted to hacking recurring revenue and tricking people into subscribing without providing genuine reasons to do so beyond ten to fifteen percent discounts. The real problem with this approach was a fundamental lack of focus on convenience or solving actual customer problems. At SubSummit in Dallas, many speakers emphasized bringing convenience back to subscriptions, whether through simple solutions like never running out of essential products or avoiding the hassle of lugging cases of water up a driveway into a house. Convenience became the dominant theme, rather than just discounting mechanics.

One critical issue with subscriptions is the difficulty of unsubscribing. While companies may make it technically easy to cancel online, the overall friction of remembering passwords, navigating password managers, and finding the subscription settings on a mobile site creates enough friction that people become frustrated with subscription models entirely. This inconvenience overwhelms customers even when companies are genuinely trying to sell them convenience. At Native, there were significant learnings about subscription frequency. Some customers used one stick per month due to heavy exercise and surface area under their arms, while others went eight months before needing a replacement. This variation made it extremely difficult to get frequency right, and the business ultimately pitched a two-month frequency more for company convenience than customer convenience.

The cost of acquiring subscription customers was extraordinarily high. An early A/B test at Native in 2015 showed that a subscription-only landing page had ten times higher customer acquisition cost than a page where people could choose between one-time purchase or subscription. People fundamentally hated subscribing, making this dynamic even more challenging. The ideal funnel for subscriptions involves one to two purchases before asking someone to commit to subscribing, which also results in longer lifetime value. Customers acquired directly into a subscription funnel, particularly those incentivized with thirty percent off initially and then ten percent off going forward, churn the fastest and become the worst customers.

A major emerging problem with subscriptions is chargebacks. Banks like JP Morgan have created policies where if someone cancels a subscription and then attempts a chargeback on a previous order, the bank doesn't require proof of when the cancellation occurred. For example, someone could cancel a subscription on June seventh and then do a chargeback on a January order. When the company provides proof of cancellation on June seventh, the bank doesn't care because they don't require proof that the cancellation happened before the original charge date. This effectively allows customers to get free products by calling chargebacks, and there's virtually no defense against it.

The chargeback problem extends to situations where companies have proof of delivery through FedEx photos showing the package in the customer's hands, but customers still win chargebacks by claiming non-delivery. This costs the company twice: they lose the product that's already shipped and landed, and they lose the money. The entire system is stacked against direct-to-consumer businesses, with a twenty-five dollar fee on top of the stolen product value. No direct-to-consumer lobbyist exists to fight these banking policies, despite this being a major pain point for the industry.

Two major e-commerce conferences provided significant insights. Commerce Summit in New York was particularly valuable because it brought together operators running businesses at scale, many doing fifty million to five hundred million in annual revenue. The conversation was remarkably blunt and candid compared to typical industry discussions, likely because these operators were discussing in confidence rather than broadcasting on social media.

One surprising discovery from Commerce Summit was the under-eight-hundred-dollar tariff exemption. There are no tariffs on imports under eight hundred dollars, which several brands exploit by shipping through three-party logistics in Canada or Mexico and then individually shipping items into the country to avoid tariffs. Some founders have even built their own manufacturing facilities, saving thirty to fifty percent of costs compared to standard contract manufacturers, with startup costs around one and a half million dollars. This only makes sense at significant scale, typically fifty million dollars in revenue or higher.

When selling a company to a strategic buyer, cost savings alignment can be just as important as customer alignment. If a strategic buyer already produces similar products, they may be able to reduce your costs through consolidated manufacturing, procurement, or distribution, creating value beyond customer acquisition synergies. This is particularly relevant for companies doing several hundred million in revenue.

A critical insight about business metrics is that gross revenue is largely irrelevant. What matters is understanding the cost per order across all operational expenses, including picking and packing, customer service tickets, returns, and other fulfillment costs. Every dollar of revenue has associated costs that need to be understood. If you're going to double advertising spend expecting eighty percent revenue growth, you also need to increase customer service staffing, inventory, fulfillment capacity, and other infrastructure accordingly. The real key performance indicator isn't revenue itself but the complete operational impact of each dollar earned.

Conducting regular audits of business finances is essential. Many CEOs delegate financial oversight to CFOs and never actually look at credit card statements. A better practice is reviewing company credit card statements monthly, questioning every charge, and immediately slack-messaging employees about unexplained expenses. This simple practice catches unnecessary recurring subscriptions and prevents slow-motion budget erosion.

The cost-cutting mindset that prevails among successful founders at scale is striking. Many are extremely aggressive about finding savings opportunities and questioning why things are done the way they've always been done. Despite this, many businesses doing hundreds of millions in revenue prefer sixty-day payment terms over two percent cash back on credit cards, which seems irrational. A two percent reduction in customer acquisition cost through cash back credit cards requires zero operational changes and immediate impact, yet many large companies reject this in favor of maintaining payment terms. This preference for terms despite massive profitability suggests cash flow concerns remain real even at tremendous scale.

Temu represents a fascinating case study in subsidized e-commerce. The company sells products at seemingly impossible prices: eight makeup brushes for one dollar, six lip balms for ninety-seven cents, with free shipping from China to the United States and free returns within ninety days. Temu's parent company is Pinduoduo, a Chinese e-commerce platform. The company spent approximately one point four billion dollars on advertising in the United States this year, with plans to spend four point three billion next year, making it competitive with Procter & Gamble's total advertising spend.

These prices are possible because Temu receives multiple subsidies. First, they negotiate special shipping rates with Chinese carriers like J&T Express. While normal shipping from Guangzhou, China to the United States costs approximately fourteen dollars, Temu's scale allows them to negotiate rates where the carrier subsidizes about five dollars of the shipping cost, reducing Temu's cost to nine to ten dollars. Second, they negotiate aggressively with manufacturers, promising massive volume in exchange for below-normal pricing. A manufacturer that might have sold one hundred million units to other buyers can suddenly sell one point one billion units to Temu, and while those additional units may be sold at a loss, the sheer volume keeps factories running. Third, and most significantly, they benefit from a critical US government policy: the eight-hundred-dollar tariff exemption. Any product shipped directly from China to a US consumer avoids tariffs that would apply if the product were imported into the United States and then shipped domestically. This is worth approximately twenty-five percent savings compared to traditional import-distribution models. The jobs, logistics, and tax benefits all accrue to China while American consumers subsidize Chinese manufacturing through lack of tariffs on small packages.

Temu's product pages employ sophisticated conversion optimization tactics. They display social proof extensively, showing thousands sold, limited inventory warnings, payment plan options, demand indicators stating fifteen hundred units sold in the last hour, and warnings that over nine hundred ninety-nine people have the item in their carts. Everything emphasizes scarcity, popularity, and affordability. Reviews are overwhelmingly positive, which is likely explained by differences in Chinese review standards versus American FTC regulations, though Temu technically must comply with American rules. Despite the obviously subsidized nature of these businesses, they operate with remarkably good user experience, honest delivery estimates using major US carriers, and transparent about China-based shipping while leveraging American logistics companies.

The awards and certification industry reveals widespread pay-to-play schemes. Forbes' Shook list charges thirty-five hundred dollars per year to use a "Forbes-ranked" designation in marketing materials, with higher fees for paid advertising. PARS International, which represents major publications including Insider, New York Times, Time, CNN, and Reuters, charges fifteen hundred dollars to place logos on websites and double that for perpetual licensing. Different rates apply for merchandise and television commercials. Allure magazine certification requires payment to use their logos in advertising. Made Safe, a relatively unknown certification organization for products made with safe ingredients, charges between six thousand and two hundred thousand dollars annually based on product revenue. Whole30 approved designation costs mid-five figures. The Beverage Tasting Institute charges to award high scores and then charges again to use those scores in advertising. Even the Susan G. Komen foundation charges one hundred thousand dollars minimum to use their pink ribbon branding, despite the ostensible charitable purpose. B Corp certification at one thousand dollars annually is actually reasonable given the rigorous testing required, but most others are purely pay-for-play schemes with minimal brand integrity.

The most insidious aspect of these certification schemes is that they accept payment from virtually anyone, meaning a cigarette company could theoretically pay for a health certification seal, creating the absurd situation where products with no legitimate claim to quality or safety can display official-looking badges. These certificates have become so compromised that their only real value is to companies willing to pay for them, not to consumers seeking genuine quality assurance. Trust in product recommendations ultimately comes down to personal relationships where people know recommendations weren't paid for, making friend referrals far more valuable than any industry certification or award.
