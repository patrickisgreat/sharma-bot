# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Welcome to Limited Supply. Over the last nine years, I've spent my time building, scaling, and investing in brands. Through this show and my weekly newsletter, I'm here to share everything I've learned—the wins, the losses, the experiments, the tactics, and the insights that can help you unlock your next hundred thousand in revenue.

Today's conversation features Hudson Leogrand, the founder of Comfort Clothing. Comfort is one of the biggest brands to exist on TikTok Shop, likely the top apparel brand on the platform. Hudson has done over five hundred million in TikTok Shop sales and expects to do between one hundred and two hundred million for the remainder of this year. We're diving into Hudson's Q4 playbook to understand what a brand operating at his caliber is doing, how they're planning and thinking about Q4, and how they approach merchandising, pricing, sales, TikTok Live, creators, and affiliates.

When Hudson started on TikTok Shop, he didn't have a track record or existing sales numbers. What he did have was an understanding of the platform's early potential. He reached out to micro-affiliates—very small creators who had never worked with a major brand before. These creators weren't accustomed to usage rights negotiations, rates discussions, or brand managers. Hudson pitched them on the vision of where the business was going, planting a seed that even if they earned enough to cover their rent or car payment, it would be worth their time.

The fundamental strategy was simple but demanding: quality and quantity. The algorithm doesn't discriminate between content you think will win and content that actually does. You need both dimensions working simultaneously. When asked if these tactics still apply today, Hudson confirmed they do. He doesn't want to work with celebrities or macro creators seeking A-list talent. Instead, he wants the most relatable people possible. His demographic is enormous—essentially everybody. He wants to evoke genuine emotion in content. When people watch ads, Hudson doesn't want them thinking a celebrity got paid; he wants them feeling the authenticity.

Comfort currently has six hundred thousand affiliates, with five hundred core people at the company who all serve as faces of the brand. Hudson wants to keep adding like-minded people who fit the community and wake up wanting to win. This approach works because they're growing alongside the brand rather than the brand trying to catch up with already-successful creators.

The influencer economy has always pushed celebrities as a solution, but the return on investment simply isn't there for most brands. The authenticity argument matters more. TikTok's halo effect proves especially powerful. Despite spending half a million dollars daily on Meta advertising, Hudson says nobody ever mentions seeing Comfort on Instagram. Everyone says they saw them on TikTok. TikTok is the brand's origin point, and all content gets repurposed across Meta, Snapchat, AppLovin, and Pinterest.

For creative formulas, Hudson thinks in layers. There's a hook, a call, the middle content showing the product experience, and then a call to action that creates FOMO and scarcity. He uses what he calls the information basis: people don't need time to make decisions; they need information. The hook evokes emotion through the middle content, and the CTA drives urgency.

Street interviews proved unexpectedly powerful. One street interview advertisement generated over seventy million dollars in sales. The format shocks people because they're so accustomed to scrolling and becoming desensitized. By the time viewers realize it's an ad, Hudson has already hooked them into watching and buying. Dense UGC videos work better in the middle and bottom of the funnel, bringing customers back. The key is doing something different than everyone else. Everyone follows the same playbook and expects different results.

When asked how he comes up with these unique ideas, Hudson admits he doesn't actually have TikTok and doesn't use it much. Instead, he relies on his five hundred internal team members who understand the company's direction and what's worked versus what hasn't. They surface trends, point out what competitors are doing, and identify product opportunities. When thinking about new products, Hudson combines market data with internal insights. He uses an app called Particle to see what other brands are doing, identify their top sellers, understand revenue generation, and review TikTok Shop sales and customer feedback.

Product expansion beyond hoodies comes from direct feedback. Comfort asks affiliates what they want to see next. On the website, they use Fairing, a Shopify app that asks customers during checkout if there's something they looked for that they couldn't find or something they'd like the company to make. This is how they developed blankets and waffle tees. Hudson's creative director also guides product decisions, helping balance his impulses to launch numerous products with maintaining vision alignment.

The brand positions itself as a one-stop shop for comfortable essentials. Blankets represented an opportunity because there's no clear leader in that category—no single dominant brand. Comfort also operates as a mental health brand, donating a portion of every order to mental health charities and partnering with the ADA. This shapes product decisions. Some items are designed with specific purposes: weighted hoodies for comfort, five-pound hoodies for children with autism who want the sensory input but don't want to wear a visible weighted vest to school. Product expansion aligns with these deeper brand values while remaining profitable.

Hudson emphasizes that fixed prices and constant discounts destroy margins. He's adopted a dynamic pricing model addressing a real problem: the business was selling too fast. Growing from sixteen million to one hundred seventy million in revenue while bootstrapped created severe scalability challenges. They had to create methods to keep items in stock while extracting revenue.

Pre-order is one key mechanism. Even when items are in stock, customers can purchase at a cheaper price if they're willing to wait two months. This lets Comfort function as the bank, taking cash upfront while deferring fulfillment. This funds the business and finances purchase orders. Last month alone, pre-order generated fourteen million dollars.

Dynamic pricing changes based on stock availability. The goal is maximizing profitability while ensuring customers can actually buy products. Previously, Comfort would constantly run out of stock, leading customers to assume the brand never had inventory and to shop elsewhere. When an airplane mode hoodie sold one hundred thousand units in forty-eight hours, even with pre-order, the price stayed at retail rather than being heavily discounted. People are willing to pay when there's genuine hype around a product.

Some months have hit fifty percent of total orders coming from pre-sales. They target staying under twenty percent, but higher percentages still work. Importantly, they continue running Meta and Snapchat ads for products that are out of stock, watching CPA and ROAS on customers willing to wait two months. If pre-order remains profitable, they keep the ads running.

For holiday pre-orders, Hudson structures dates around when purchase orders get placed. There's a deadline to pre-order and guarantee arrival before Christmas. Once they max out orders for a given date, the delivery date moves up. As Christmas approaches, they place huge orders so everyone can still receive better pricing by ordering in advance. Customers feel like they're hacking the system by ordering early for a discount, but fundamentally, Comfort is managing inventory and cash flow efficiently.

Clear communication is essential. Hudson learned early that being proactive versus reactive determines success. They have to be incredibly proactive about pre-order communication or customers will flood them with questions and leave negative reviews everywhere.

For holidays specifically, bundles drive the strategy. Every hoodie has a matching pair of sweats, and Comfort incentivizes bundle purchases to increase average order value. If your product's price is too low, you can't profitably acquire customers on Meta. By building value in bundles and offering substantial discounts, Comfort can spend more money acquiring customers while remaining profitable.

People like wearing sets. Hudson regularly sees customers in full Comfort outfits at airports and other public spaces. Bundling has been highly productive specifically because it matches how customers actually want to present themselves.

For Q4 this year versus last year, Hudson made one major change: he's starting earlier. Last Q4 was a nightmare. Seventy-five percent of their products sold out before Black Friday. They had to put everything out of stock on Black Friday and couldn't run any SMS or email marketing. It was devastating. His CEO worried about the company going under. This year, the approach is completely different.

The strategy is starting October fifteenth with a pre-Black Friday sale. Then they'll go all-in a week before Black Friday and maintain the push through the week after Cyber Monday, extending the promotional period across the entire month. The goal is not being sold out on Black Friday, then playing Cyber Monday and the full Cyber Week. Being first to market matters because when everyone else runs the same promotions simultaneously, CPMs rise and everyone spends significantly more money. Getting in early lets Comfort dominate auctions and spend their Q4 budget while ahead of the game.

Hudson doesn't worry about pushing the start date earlier every year because customers understand the business model. They're transparent about dynamic pricing, sales timing, when products restock, and how quickly items sell out. For a clothing brand, Q4 is the bread and butter anyway. It gets cold, everyone wants hoodies, and people are buying gifts. Even though Comfort isn't strictly seasonal, Q4 naturally sees more growth.

Building the affiliate community happened over time. Hudson started by managing everything himself, recruiting creators through iMessage, then Discord, then weekly calls. Management doesn't mean babysitting; it means incubating, training, and making creators really good. They need to understand every aspect of the business.

Now with six hundred thousand affiliates, not all are equally active. Roughly half post regularly. Many are simply customers. These ads can profitably acquire affiliates who then purchase products again using a thirty percent affiliate discount code.
