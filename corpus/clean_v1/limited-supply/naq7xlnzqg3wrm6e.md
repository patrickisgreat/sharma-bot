# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Michael, the founder and CEO of FabFitFun, shares his journey building one of the biggest subscription box companies in the world. The company started with FabFitFun as an editorial website in 2010 before launching subscription boxes in 2013. Today, they have over 650,000 square feet of fulfillment center space, roughly 300 plus employees, and have touched one in five or one in six women in America as subscribers.

Before FabFitFun, Michael and his original partner started an agency in 2007 building websites for politicians. During the 2008 election cycle, they were explaining basic internet concepts like Twitter and blogs to political campaigns scrambling to understand the digital landscape. Eventually, they pivoted toward working with celebrities and Hollywood figures in Los Angeles. Out of that celebrity work, the idea for FabFitFun emerged after attending gifting suites where they experienced curated products firsthand.

The genesis of FabFitFun came from recognizing a consumer need: people wanted help staying on trend and accessing new, cool products at great value. Unlike Birchbox and Ipsy which focused on samples, FabFitFun differentiated by offering full-size products with substantial retail value. Their boxes range from about $55 a quarter while delivering over $300 in retail value.

Michael's brother joined the business and they became co-CEOs and co-founders. The subscription box proved extremely differentiated and innovative, but the company didn't stop there. They started with simple add-ons to boxes, which evolved into thousands of SKUs. Now they describe FabFitFun increasingly as a full-fledged retailer and shopping club, with the subscription box serving as the most visible, highest-velocity products in a larger store ecosystem.

One core insight driving the business is understanding their customer's mindset: it's pressure to be cool, on trend, have nice things, and make spaces beautiful. By surfacing what's cool and making it convenient while delivering real value, FabFitFun solves a genuine problem. They've positioned themselves as the inverse of TJ Maxx by offering discounts at the front of the product lifecycle during brand launches rather than at the end during liquidation.

Brands love working with FabFitFun because the platform serves as a marketplace for brands to find new customers at scale. The company has one in five or one in six women in America as former or current subscribers. For brands, this represents incremental customers rather than cannibalization since FabFitFun doesn't rely on Google product listings and doesn't feel like a discount outlet.

The company's marketing evolution is notable. Early on, Facebook was a major channel. The right-hand rail programmatic ads were effective for direct response. One particularly successful channel was influencer and creator partnerships, especially with reality stars. A New Yorker article once stated that brands were "nothing until you've worked with FabFitFun." This native advantage came from their early partnership with Juliana Rancic and their existing celebrity network.

During the early era of organic reach on social media, influencers had genuine organic reach as people tuned in weekly to watch their shows. There was natural synergy between creators wanting to talk about new things every season and FabFitFun consistently bringing new products. The company even partnered with Facebook to help build their whitelist tool for creator content, sitting side-by-side with the product team.

As the social media landscape changed and organic reach declined, FabFitFun adapted. They now think of creators more as content creators than distribution channels. Distribution comes through paid media spend and owned channels. Whitelisting remains a significant part of customer acquisition but operates differently than the early influencer era.

The company balances brand building with direct response marketing through what they call "performance brand marketing." They maintain key value propositions across all creative, constantly iterating the same messages so they become ingrained in consumers' minds. This approach has largely built their brand, audience, and community.

Top-of-funnel efforts include working with creators, podcasts, and events. Earned media has changed significantly since the early days. Traditional press outlets no longer drive meaningful traffic, but buzz and organic sharing from consumers still matters. The company focuses on creating products and collaborations that cut through the noise, like their collabs with trending brands like Vacation. By positioning themselves as both a retailer and publisher, they can tell stories about the brands they work with and drive organic interest.

FabFitFun expanded beyond the core subscription box through several mechanisms. They created add-on shopping, which grew into thousands of SKUs. They developed house brands and a program called FabFitFun D for early-stage consumer product brands. This program offers forward-looking purchase order commitments, access to their 3PL operation, expertise in creative and performance marketing, and packaging solutions. They position themselves as "queen makers" for early-stage brands.

One successful house brand is Unhide, a faux fur blanket company launched after the company identified strong demand signals for faux fur blankets from their audience. A partnership fell through, so they launched the brand independently with a joint venture partner. Unhide is now an eight-digit revenue brand with presence beyond their ecosystem.

Another successful investment was in Our Place, a cookware company. When multiple entrepreneurs pitched cookware businesses in 2018, the company decided to support one with their FabFitFun D program. They partnered with founders Amir and Shiza, and Our Place's Always Pan became the top-selling single SKU home product on Shopify. Michael sits on their board today.

Now the company operates as a core retailer at the center with a constellation of brands they own and operate, have joint ventures in, or partner with directly. They're also productizing B2B services like their 3PL business and recently acquired a packaging company called Books, an eco-conscious packaging business that previously served brands like Goop and Rōd Beauty.

They acquired Pupbox, a subscription box for pets, from Petco about a year ago. Petco had acquired it from its original founding team but it didn't fit their thesis. FabFitFun was able to bring it into their ecosystem, lean out OPEX, and leverage their supply chain and merchant capabilities. Over half their customers have pets, creating natural cross-sell opportunities while remaining organic to the platform.

On metrics and growth, Michael highlights that retention is the north star but every metric must be contextualized. High turn programs can be healthy depending on the marketing program running. He's skeptical of tactical things that superficially reduce churn without making customers genuinely happier. The product and programming should reduce churn through delivering value, not through optimized cancel flows.

Subscription fatigue is real as everyone launches subscriptions. The winners are bundling more value and standing out through differentiation. FabFitFun focuses on natural extensions of their offering and understanding where they have permission to expand. The company is exploring a credit card but wants to ensure it delivers differentiated value rather than being a captive audience play.

For evaluating new opportunities, Michael emphasizes rigorous debate with his co-founder brother and leadership team. They have weekly strategic meetings and twice-yearly multi-day offsites. The best opportunities are often more strategic than they are obvious metric-plays, because obvious ideas become the domain of big companies. They're skeptical of things claimed to be easy and turnkey, knowing that anything truly great requires substantial work and resources to execute well.

On merchandising, they employ a large team pairing merchandisers with consumer insights. The process involves creating many options, understanding brand willingness to work with them favorably, and analyzing consumer data from surveys, sell-through rates, ratings, and reviews. They have millions of data points on product performance. Their editor-in-chief Katie oversees debates and decisions at the top of this process.

The company's relationship with brands has evolved significantly. Early on, they had to sell brands on participation. Now brands chase them. This shift happened gradually, likely around 2020, as evidence accumulated over years showing FabFitFun was genuinely good for brands. When brands appear in FabFitFun boxes, their retail sales fly. The company positions itself as free marketing for brands, getting new consumers in front of products without cannibalizing brand traffic or appearing as a discount outlet.

Michael describes the company's role as a curation platform finding brands their audience will have affinity for. Even if specific products don't fit box requirements, they can be featured in the broader store. They're intentionally broad across beauty, fashion, accessories, home decor, and wellness. While primarily women-focused between 20 to 60 years old, they remain flexible and creative about expansion opportunities.

On philanthropy, they've raised at least half a million dollars for nonprofits throughout their tenure, previously featuring a nonprofit each season with donation add-ons. They're reformulating toward deeper thematic focus in a specific vertical. They also rallied authentically during the LA fires, creating a build-a-box program for people who lost their homes. Authenticity matters in these efforts rather than feeling plugged in.

Regarding AI, Michael is intellectually curious about the technology and uses ChatGPT frequently. The lowest hanging fruit for e-commerce is customer service automation. The company is applying AI to meeting summaries and has a Slack channel encouraging team-wide exploration. On the merchandising team specifically, AI is being applied to handling massive amounts of consumer insights, outbound and inbound brand conversations, and surveying methodologies before purchasing decisions.

The company's value proposition combines convenience, discovery, and trust. Their audience trusts them to choose the right things and tell the right stories. They're neither purely a brand nor purely a retailer, but rather a discovery and curation platform that's evolved into a shopping club. The work is iterative, always questioning what needs to happen to innovate and stay relevant. Michael emphasizes celebrating wins while staying alert to pain points, understanding that founding is an absolute rollercoaster involving multiple moments where you feel you might go out of business. Finding joy in the work itself is essential to sustaining through the journey.
