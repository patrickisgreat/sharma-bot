# Limited Supply Podcast - Episode 6, Season 3

You know, I feel like culture is so overlooked. It's very well managed in jobs where there's physical labor. I think the companies that—whether it's a contract manufacturer, a moving company, installation company—the companies that manage culture really well grow really quickly.

Today on the show, we're talking with Mary Berry, the founder of Texas Beauty Labs and now Cosmos Labs, one of the most capable contract manufacturers in the beauty and personal care space. Mary is the kind of person who doesn't just say she's a partner—she acts like one. She'll fly across the country at a moment's notice to make sure her clients are taken care of.

I met Mary about eight years ago when I was looking for someone who could manufacture Native deodorant. I had called other contract manufacturers, and they told me they could have my first batch ready in six to twelve months. When I called Mary at Texas Beauty Labs, she said six weeks. That's when I knew we could do business together.

Let's start with what Texas Beauty Labs actually is. We make stuff for other people. We don't have our own line—we make other people's products. We buy raw materials, mix them, put them in packaging, and ship them out. We also do product development work and handle the chemistry behind formulas.

Mary started Texas Beauty Labs in 2006 or 2007, though it's a blur to her now. The first facility was next to a docking horse and a mobile home. By 2015, when we started working together, she was already in her third facility in Pflugerville, Texas. Back then, they were making products for Milk and Honey, a spa in Austin, and various other clients. Business wasn't great—she actually had to sell an old Mercedes she'd always wanted because she couldn't make payroll.

For about eight years, the business was negative. They were nearly foreclosed on their house. Mary's husband, Jason, worked as an electrical engineer at Dell making $75,000 a year, and that was basically their only income. They had nine employees who depended on them, and Mary made them her priority. She even pawned her wedding ring multiple times to make sure people got paid.

When I first started working with Native, we ran into a quality issue. I was in San Francisco, went to our fulfillment facility in Oakland, and noticed that some of our deodorant felt like lotion instead of being solid. I immediately texted Mary and asked if we could meet the next day. She came to our office, saw the problem within thirty seconds, and called her factory to stop production immediately.

We had completely changed the formula within 48 to 72 hours. That's when I walked out of that building and thought, I'm going to make a billion dollars. I didn't know why—I'm not even particularly motivated by money—but I felt it in my bones. This guy knows what he's doing. We've got it squared away.

Mary ended up selling Texas Beauty Labs, but there was a non-compete that prevented her from doing manufacturing for a period. She tried launching a vitamin brand called Cosmos Vita, but it wasn't working. I actually gave her some blunt advice about it. We were having lunch at Balthazar in New York, and I looked at her spreadsheet and said, "Shut this business down now." She listened, and that was one of the best things I did for her because that clarity was actually a kindness.

When her non-compete expired, she immediately started Cosmos Labs. The day her non-compete expired was the day I texted her about a container that was coming my way for Long Weekend. An hour later, she texted me to catch up, and I was like, perfect timing. Send it to me.

What makes a good client for Mary? There are two pathways. One is marketing guys who know what they're doing—people like us who understand how to sell products and what we want. Mary gets to do what she's good at, and then she gets to be controlling about it. Her second favorite type of client is someone who comes in with a really intense product brief, someone who knows exactly what they want, the price point, the components, and can get it done quickly.

The worst clients are people who have an idea for a product but don't really know how to sell it or haven't thought through the economics. Mary has a hard time saying no because she likes to help people, but she's working on that. She's developed a template response that basically says no in a nice way, but the message is clear.

As for emerging categories, Mary is getting a lot of inquiries about sexual wellness products, which makes her uncomfortable. She's 45 years old and married, but she still gets red in the face talking about it. She's also seeing interest in SPF products, which is a big deal right now. But the most important thing for anyone launching a beauty or skincare brand is margins. You can have a great product in a great category, but if the margins don't work, the business won't work.

When it comes to scaling, Mary thinks about supplier relationships. With Native, they actually disrupted the global supply of high melt shea butter because they were buying so much of it. Once you're buying truckloads of raw materials, your costs come down, and Mary believes in passing some of that savings back to her clients. It's about working with someone you trust who will be honest about pricing as volumes increase.

One of the things that made Native successful was Mary's willingness to take risks that other contract manufacturers won't take. We went from a facility the size of a small room to a 16,000 square foot facility in what felt like overnight. We moved in a single weekend, closing Thursday and Friday, and were back in operation on Monday. No other contract manufacturer would have had the appetite to do that.

At one point, we needed to increase production dramatically, and I remember her sales team saying it couldn't be done. Mary looked at them and said, "This is our biggest client. We're not saying no under any circumstance." That's partnership.

We also had to get creative with equipment. We found an old chicken pot pie filling machine because it could handle thick products poured at low temperatures. Nothing else on the market could do what we needed. Mary's approach was always, "We'll figure it out."

When it came to hiring, Mary quickly realized that paying minimum wage wasn't working. People were unreliable, and it was because they couldn't afford to be reliable. She read about living wage versus minimum wage and decided to pay everyone in the facility $12.75 an hour when the living wage in Austin was $12.75. That's what she's always done—pay people enough that they know they're being treated fairly.

Today, the minimum at Cosmos Labs is $16.25 an hour, which is the current living wage in Austin. Mary also does things like monthly birthday lunches for everyone, and she's learning Spanish because she wants to communicate with the Spanish speakers on her team. She's had people work for her for three years, which is unheard of in manufacturing.

She's also very protective of her team's dignity. If she finds out someone is being rude or disrespectful to her employees, she will fire that person or that client immediately. She had someone yell racial slurs at her team once, and she literally almost physically assaulted that person she was so angry.

What Mary is really good at is understanding the minutiae of manufacturing. She can tell you the price of organic glycerin shipped is twelve cents an ounce. She can look at a deodorant and tell you by weight that something's wrong. She can understand particle sizes of baking soda and how that affects the final product. She can look at fill weights and know instantly if they're off.

When we brought in a process engineer from Benefit Cosmetics to audit our operation, he showed us that the baking soda we were buying from Costco in bulk had inconsistent particle sizes. We were literally buying bags from Instacart—fifty bags at a time, thirteen and a half pounds each—because we needed to control costs. Her husband, Jason, did most of those runs, and apparently an Instacart driver eventually figured out who was ordering all the baking soda.

The process engineer showed us that if we sourced baking soda directly from suppliers, we could specify the exact particle size we wanted, which would give us a more consistent product. That's the kind of insight that comes from working with people who've been at major companies like PNG or Benefit.

One of Mary's favorite memories is when Texas Beauty Labs made the Inc 500 list of fastest growing companies. She made a video montage of photos from when she started the business in her house, and she sobbed the entire time making it. At the celebration party, she got so drunk that her shirt was covered in spilled drinks. She got up in front of everyone and said something to the effect of: the reason this company worked is the people that worked there. It has nothing to do with me. It's everyone that came together and made it work.

Her least favorite memory is selling the business. It was heartbreaking in ways she still can't quite articulate. She also learned something important: she's not fit to have a business partner. She's too wild, does crazy things on impulse, and that's not fair to someone else. But she's realized that what works for her is having a strong core team around her who can execute on her vision. Her operations person, Ashley, is now the CEO and business partner, and that structure works well.

Mary's advice for anyone trying to build a business? Don't just say you're a partner—be a fucking partner. Show up. Make it happen. Fly across the country if you need to. That's what makes relationships special and what makes businesses grow.

For entrepreneurs launching a skincare or beauty brand, Mary's biggest piece of advice is to pay attention to margins. There are a billion moisturizers and a billion of everything else. You can have a great product in a great category, but if the margins don't work out, the business won't survive. She also recommends thinking about working with a contract manufacturer when you're ready to do about 5,000 units. That's the sweet spot where it shows you have some experience, some backing, or you've self-funded successfully, and it means they can get you to market quickly.

Anyone interested in working with Mary can email her at mary at cosmos-labs.com, though she mentioned they need to work on the domain name.

What strikes me most about Mary is her genuine commitment to the people around her. She didn't have to pay above minimum wage. She didn't have to fly to New York at the last minute. She didn't have to split costs when there were quality issues. But she did those things because that's how she operates. She builds businesses on relationships and trust, and that's why people want to work with her and for her.
