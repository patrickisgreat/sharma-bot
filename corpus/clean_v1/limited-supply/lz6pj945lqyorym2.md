# Episode 8: Benchmark Metrics, P&Ls, Q4 Marketing, and Random Thoughts

One of the important parts about repeat purchase rate that people often neglect is how they measure it. Sometimes they measure it by looking at how much of a month's revenue came from returning customers. For example, they might say they had $100,000 in sales this month and $30,000 came from returning customers, so their repeat purchase rate is 30%. That is not the right way to measure it. The right way to measure it is to look back a year ago and ask what percentage of customers from that same month a year ago have bought again. That's the only way to measure it accurately.

When Native started the business, they had a repeat purchase rate of about 20 to 25%, which was not great for a consumable product like deodorant. By continuously iterating on the formula, they got it close to 50% by the time the business was sold, which is fantastic. When trying to sell the business, conversations about repeat purchase rate with buyers were interesting because they'd claim they'd never seen anything that high, yet when questioned about their own repeat purchase rates, many couldn't actually articulate what theirs was or how they measured it.

There's another metric that's worth understanding alongside repeat purchase rate: returning customer revenue. If 70% of your revenue this month is coming from returning customers, you're probably not growing very quickly because only 30% of your revenue is new customers. This is an interesting metric to keep in mind, as it tells you a lot about your growth trajectory. If the repeat purchase rate is really high, you have to wonder whether the company is even bringing in new customers effectively. At Native, the business started at 98% new customers and eventually moved to 40% new customers as the company matured and repeat customers compounded.

Another important benchmark metric is marketing costs as a percentage of revenue. Looking at large companies' marketing spend shows interesting variations. P&G spends 24% of revenue on marketing, Nestlé spends 32%, Unilever spends 19%, and L'Oreal spends 50 to 55%. Among direct-to-consumer brands, Lululemon spends 38%, Yeti spends 39%, and FIGS spends 62%. Generally, 40% is a reasonable maximum for companies trying to grow really fast that have better margins. Companies spending less than 25% probably aren't spending enough on marketing, as someone will eventually realize the business is phenomenal and start spending more. A sweet spot seems to be around 30 to 33%, where brands are focused on growth but not growth at all costs. With older, established brands like Unilever, the percentages are lower because brands like Ben & Jerry's don't need as much advertising as a new CPG company would.

Customer service tickets as a percentage of orders is another valuable metric to track. In a dropshipping business where third parties handle fulfillment, over 40% of orders resulted in customer service tickets because of inventory issues and slow shipping. At Native, the target was about 20%. On Black Friday when they did 25,000 orders, about 20% of those resulted in tickets, with even more on that day because products wouldn't ship until Monday or Tuesday while customers expected them on Sunday. Measuring this weekly is important, and if the percentage is more than 20%, something is probably wrong with fulfillment speed, FAQ completeness, or website functionality. If it's under 20%, the company might not be getting enough new orders. This metric helps identify operational issues and should be reviewed regularly.

Starting September 1st, creating a monthly P&L is essential for understanding business performance. A P&L is basically an Excel spreadsheet where the top line is revenue and the bottom line is net profit. After revenue, you subtract every expense: credit card processing fees, marketing, Facebook ads, postage, pick and pack, wages, and everything else to see how much money you made or lost that month. It's useful to look at all expenses as a percentage of revenue. If you're paying 40% of revenue in wages, that's significant and suggests you need a substantial sales team to justify that cost.

One nuance with e-commerce companies is how to handle inventory purchases. If you buy 10,000 units of deodorant in August but only sell 2,000 of them because you bought five months of inventory, you have a choice. Using the cash method, you'd expense all 10,000 units immediately since you wrote the check for them. Using the accrual method, you'd only expense the 2,000 you sold, keeping 8,000 as inventory. The accrual method is better because it matches revenue with expenses. When you sold $100,000 in revenue from 2,000 units, you expense 2,000 units, not 10,000. This shows how profitable you actually are.

The difference between these methods can be dramatic. A friend with a beauty company doing $2 million in monthly sales thought he wasn't very profitable. When his P&L was examined, he was using the cash method and expensing all inventory purchases every month while growing rapidly. By switching to the accrual method, it became clear he was making about $300,000 per month in profit. His bank account looked depleted from inventory purchases, but his actual profitability was excellent. The accrual method gives a clearer picture of business health because your bank account can fluctuate wildly from inventory purchases while the business remains highly profitable.

For Q4 marketing channels, brands need to think about channels where costs don't increase with demand the way Facebook CPMs do when major brands like P&G unleash their advertising budgets. One tactic is using Agile's out of home truck advertising. Since delivery trucks are already running routes, adding a billboard to the truck doesn't significantly increase costs. Agile collects mobile device IDs from people who were near the truck, then brands can retarget those users on Facebook. The cost of trucks doesn't surge like digital advertising does.

Email is another stable channel because email costs don't increase in Q4 the way Facebook CPMs do. A platform like Klaviyo could theoretically charge three cents per email on Black Friday if it wanted to surge pricing, but it doesn't. Direct mail is another stable prospecting channel because the cost of stamps and printing postcards doesn't increase in Q4. Affiliate is typically a standard percentage year-round. These are all channels where costs remain predictable while Facebook and other digital channels become increasingly expensive.

Another tactic is using gift with purchase as a loss leader instead of heavily discounting on top of ads. Travel sizes are a great example. At Native, travel sizes cost about 62 cents to produce but had a perceived value of $3 to $4. A free travel size is much better than 10% off, which would cost $1.20, because the customer likes the gift more and the cost is half. One brand has developed an interesting affiliate strategy for Black Friday. Instead of just offering discounts to customers, they also offer bonus commissions to affiliates and prize incentives like cash or gift cards to top performers. This way they maintain price integrity and don't constantly spam their customers with discount emails. The affiliates do the marketing for them.

Collaborations are a powerful marketing strategy. Truff's recent collaboration with Hidden Valley Ranch went extremely well. The hot sauce and ranch combo made sense because people frequently mix them together. The packaging was well-designed and didn't over-feature Hidden Valley. The products sold out quickly and are now being resold on eBay for $200 to $400 per bottle, with original retail around $25. What's impressive is that this collaboration worked well with a brand that isn't super beloved today. Hidden Valley isn't necessarily seen as a premium ingredient-focused brand that people seeking natural products would choose. Yet the collaboration felt right, and that demonstrates sophisticated marketing thinking.

There were other successful collaborations worth noting. Taco Bell and Doritos had one of the most profitable collaborations in history. Truff also had one with Taco Bell featuring mini hot sauce on fries and nachos. At Native, attempted collaborations with Supreme and LaCroix didn't work out, but they seemed like strong ideas. A Supreme-branded deodorant and LaCroix-flavored deodorant with similar packaging aesthetics seemed like they could have resonated.

When brands that historically never discounted start running sales, it signals they're reaching for growth. Allbirds, which never had sales previously, recently announced a sale. Tuft & Needle was famously anti-discount, emphasizing that customers would pay the same price regardless of the date. They even canceled a referral program that was driving 30 to 40% of new customers because they wanted price consistency. Then a private equity firm bought them and immediately started running frequent discounts like Labor Day sales with $600 off mattresses. The shift happened instantly once financial incentives changed. Similarly, Casper went from positioning itself as the anti-discount mattress to running heavy promotions and financing options. Running sales infrequently, like Apple with just Black Friday, doesn't train customers to wait for discounts. But if brands are running sales frequently, they condition customers to expect deals.

Conversion rate optimization is a critical but often overlooked lever for growth. Several tactical elements improve conversion rates. One is auto-applying coupon codes at checkout with landing page permalinks that include the coupon code in the URL itself. That way customers see the discount already applied when they reach the checkout page. Another powerful tactic is displaying that "Your information is secure" with a lock icon, especially for older customers who are more likely to worry about online security.

Putting coupon code entry boxes in the slide-out cart rather than just on the Shopify checkout page dramatically improves conversion rates because many customers don't realize they need to go to the full checkout page to apply codes. When switching from WooCommerce to Shopify, removing the obvious coupon code box from the cart caused material conversion rate drops because people would look for it on the cart page, not find it, and abandon without going to checkout.

Writing explicit assurances like "Your order will ship in 24 hours" increases conversion. Return policies on the checkout page help. Trust badges like trust pilot reviews with star ratings improve conversion. Harry's has a strong stamp communicating their guarantee, and Magic Spoon has a happiness guarantee. Even simple elements like displaying that information is secure increases conversion, particularly for older demographics.

GoPuff's recent financial situation is a cautionary tale. They raised $2 billion at a $15 billion valuation last year but reported losing $400 million in the first three months of this year, meaning they're burning over $100 million per month. They've since laid off significant portions of their staff and claim they're fixing the problem, but the losses were so severe that it made news in the Wall Street Journal. Creating a P&L revealed they're spending massively more than they're earning.

Italic is an interesting business model where the founder sources products from the same factories that make products for luxury brands, then sells them at much lower prices. Their site shows when a product is "made at the same factory as Louis Vuitton" or other premium brands. This allows customers to buy quality products without the luxury brand markup. The company has successfully built a business around this concept with products ranging from t-shirts to cookware to knives. The quality is reportedly excellent, though it's unclear how they legally advertise the factory connections without brand pushback.

Mad Happy is a clothing brand that's doing phenomenal work with collaborations and product releases. They navigate the fashion industry well, which is risky because brands either crush it or fail. Strikes is a men's makeup company selling through Target and their website. Their sunscreen and concealer products are well-designed, with concealer being particularly effective at covering blemishes and skin imperfections. While men's makeup might seem niche, it highlights how women have long had access to products that solve appearance issues, while men are just beginning to explore these options.
