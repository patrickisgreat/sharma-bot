# Limited Supply - Season 2, Episode 7

When you're working with an agency, you want to make sure you're actually a priority relative to their other clients. The whole point of working with an agency is working quickly, getting learnings, and accessing good talent who's seen and done cool things on other brands. If you're getting bottom-level talent, you're probably not getting the best of what that agency has to offer.

Looking at who their clients are is crucial. If you're a new beauty brand and you go to an agency whose portfolio is filled with L'Oreal, Cody, and Saks Fifth Avenue, that's a terrible fit. Great logos, but the mindset of how big agencies work is completely different from what a growing direct-to-consumer brand needs. You want clients in your ballpark. If you're doing between ten and fifty million in revenue, you need an agency that understands that scale and that mindset.

Always reach out to people who have actually worked with the agency before. There are instances of agencies listing logos they've never meaningfully worked with. Get people on the phone rather than email. Via email, nobody's going to give you the truth because they're afraid of writing something defamatory or having it get back to the agency. On the phone, they'll be honest about whether the agency was good or terrible.

If an agency says they do everything—site development, SEO, creative ads, landing pages, paid media—run. That's not an agency with passion in its DNA. That's either a PE-backed firm or someone building an agency with an Excel sheet. The quality of work will be deeply disappointing. It's like someone on TikTok saying they'll set up your whole store and make it cash flow. If they have a cash-flowing business, why are they running TikTok ads instead of Amazon ads? Because they want your money, that's why.

When an agency claims they're good at multiple things, ask the "wait, really?" question. Get samples of work. Make sure that from a design and coding standpoint, everything is legitimate. Don't just trust the word. Most new founders assume why would this person lie to me, and they get screwed over pretty badly as a result.

If someone says they can't tell you any of their clients because it's confidential, that's bullshit. Any legitimate agency will reach out to clients to ask permission to make an introduction. If they refuse to reveal any clients, especially in SEO where you can run a Google search to verify their work, you have no way to judge them. Run.

There was an experience at Hint where we hired an agency that looked great on paper. The CEO set up a meeting, they had two or three in-person meetings, and they presented a sick strategy deck. But we didn't understand that they weren't a performance marketing agency—they were a big brand, big campaign, billboard takeover type of agency. That was mistake number one.

Mistake number two was we were behind Nike, Amazon, Kindle, and a bunch of massive brands in their priority list. We were at the bottom. As a result, we got their bottom-level talent. To them, a hundred grand a month in agency fees wasn't worth that much when they were making ten times that from somebody else. Going somewhere where you're actually a priority is super important.

Make sure you talk to the people you'll actually be working with, not just the sales team. Sales and the actual execution team are completely different groups. The salespeople are charismatic and produce incredible decks. The people actually doing the work might not be able to produce that same level of output. You want to talk to the execution team specifically.

Try talking to former employees to see what they thought of the firm. You can learn a lot about company culture and whether talent is leaving. There was an experience with a development agency that got bought by a private equity firm, and within a week, the entire team on our account resigned. Within another week, five new people came in and five new people resigned. It was a complete disaster. Talking to former employees might have given us a heads-up.

Also make sure that when you work with an agency, they have direct relationships with you. Some agencies will take the contract and skim seventy percent off the top, then pay another agency, who pays another agency. That's shady. If there are subcontractors involved, that should be transparent with separate agreements.

When you're starting a business, don't immediately invest in customer service software. If you're expecting to do ten sales a day, you can live in Gmail for a while. It's cheaper, and you can figure out if the business will actually work before spending money on Gorgeous, Help Scout, or Zendesk. There's no need to pay forty dollars a month to handle one email a day.

Similarly, don't outsource customer service too quickly. Almost every founder wants to outsource it because they think it's beneath them, or they want to make higher-level decisions, or it's the cheapest thing to outsource. The problem is you lose the connection with what's wrong with your business. Don't hire anyone to handle customer service tickets until you're getting more than twenty tickets a day. An average customer service agent can do eighty to one hundred tickets a day, so twenty tickets is only two hours of work. You can do that faster as the owner because you know all the answers in your head.

If you're still having lots of product or business problems, don't hire someone. You need to be in it yourself to understand what's wrong. Only after you consistently have more than twenty tickets a day does it make sense to start thinking about hiring help or using software.

At Native, we didn't get a referral program until we were doing about a million dollars a month. We got Talkable, and when I asked them when most people start using it, they said around a hundred thousand a month. We were doing a million, so it made sense. But they're pricey, and implementation takes time. You can implement these things, but they'll suck if they don't feel personalized to the brand or the person going through the experience.

Anyone can set up a "give five dollars, get five dollars" program, and nobody will use it. You need something more unique to your business. You also need to think about whether it's worth the effort. If you're doing twenty-five thousand a month, setting up a referral program won't generate enough revenue. But if you're doing two hundred fifty thousand a month with four or five thousand customers, you might get five hundred people or a hundred people to refer someone, and that might make sense.

The same logic applies to a lot of other tools. Attribution platforms, for instance—you probably don't need one right at the beginning. Spending on multiple channels often doesn't make sense until you get to ten million in revenue, or at least one million. Staying simple and optimizing for customer experience and the communications they receive is underrated.

There are a few places where spending money makes sense. One is YouTube influencers, especially if you're selling beauty, cosmetics, or personal care products. People research these categories heavily on YouTube. You want to own that space with reviews from multiple creators. Rather than just existing on your website and in a Facebook ad, you want people to find reviews when they search your brand name. Hire ten YouTube influencers to create content about your product.

Another place is branded Google search. You want to own your own real estate. If someone searches your brand name and nothing comes up, get some initial social proof and visibility. If you're a really small brand just launching, pay for branded Google search terms so the top hit is your website, not some random result.

Harry's recently rolled out a membership program called Core. It costs fifteen dollars a year, positioned as one dollar and twenty-five cents a month. The benefits include free expedited shipping, free engraving on products, access to member-only products, and ten percent off future orders. The value proposition feels weak. If you're spending one hundred fifty dollars or more a year with Harry's anyway, the math might work, but fifteen dollars a year is so cheap that most people probably won't bother.

Another brand doing this is Fresh Clean Teens. They charge nineteen dollars a year and promise twenty percent off everything site-wide, free shipping, early access, and priority customer service. The benefits feel generic—the same things you'd see on any subscription page. Early access means nothing; it's just getting first dibs when something launches. Priority customer service is also probably bullshit for most brands at scale.

There are companies building infrastructure for this. Tandem creates branded credit cards for direct-to-consumer brands, similar to how Best Buy or Macy's have store cards. The model works for brands with consistent, frequent purchasers where you can reduce payment processing fees from 2.9% down to maybe 0.8%. But for direct-to-consumer, it's hard to imagine most customers purchasing frequently enough from a single brand to justify getting a branded credit card.

Another company is Inveterate, which provides paid membership infrastructure. They have analytics and integrations with PostScript, Klaviyo, and Gift on Your Next Order to automate the whole membership process.

McKinsey research shared by Inveterate shows that members of paid loyalty programs are sixty percent more likely to spend more after subscribing, while free loyalty programs only increase that likelihood by thirty percent. That might make sense for luxury brands like Nordstrom or Best Buy, but for direct-to-consumer, like Harry's razors, it's much harder to justify.

The real issue with paid memberships in direct-to-consumer is that the benefits are weak. What makes someone pay for a membership? It's at the intersection of passion and content, like paying for a Patreon, YouTube subscription, or paid newsletter. Or it's passion plus experience, like Equinox where you actually go somewhere. With just an annual fee, you need something better than priority customer service, free shipping, and early access. Those three things come with just about any subscription.

Harry's was doing an interesting sweepstakes recently. They had a giveaway for a hundred thousand dollars, and the more you spent, the more entries you got into the sweepstakes. It's no longer on their website, but they were testing it. This is different from their original referral program, which was one of the most successful referral programs ever created. When they first launched, they offered a tiered system: give away a free razor blade if you sign up three friends, a t-shirt for ten friends, five hundred dollars for fifty friends, and a trip to New York with the founders for a thousand friends. Back in 2012, nobody was doing this, and it might have been the most successful referral program ever. They got over a hundred thousand referrals from it.

Looking at Harry's checkout flow, there are some interesting choices. They have a pop-out cart that comes from the side, then you click checkout and go to another cart page with more upsells before you finally get to the actual checkout page. This is basically two cart pages before the final checkout, which probably causes some friction. They're constantly testing and removing things—the upsells were testing different product bundles and offerings.

The positive things Harry's does really well include the way they present subscription options. If you click "get started," it's a click funnel-style flow where you choose products. The copywriting is optimized: "Save 5% free shipping" is called out in red. "First refill ships December 1st" is bolded. They have "Not charged today, cancel anytime" to reduce friction. They cross out prices and highlight "best value" options. They ask how often you shave so they understand shipping cadence correctly.

On the cart page, they show the difference between economy shipping and ground shipping. They say "ten dollars away from free economy shipping" at first, then when you hit that threshold, they switch to "free economy shipping unlocked" in green. These little serotonin signals as you're spending more are designed to make you feel good.

The one friction point is that they require you to create an account before checkout, rather than letting you checkout first and then create an account. That's a mistake that probably causes some dropoff.

After you buy, Harry's asks a bunch of questions: When's your birthday? Do you shave with the grain or against the grain? They're collecting data about how you use their products, probably to tailor communications later.

Other brands doing checkout really well include Ridge Wallet. Their checkout is on Shopify, but you can't tell it is. It's completely custom. Obvi also has a branded checkout that doesn't feel like Shopify. They have a gamified cart where you get free gifts at certain spending thresholds. When you hit seventy-five dollars, you unlock a free face mask. The checkout page itself is completely branded with their colors. They have a scrolling carousel showing customers with the product. They highlight social proof: "Why over 150,000 customers trust Obvi." They have satisfaction guarantees and free shipping called out.

The one criticism is they might have too many payment options available. On mobile, if you're showing all five, that could take up thirty percent of the page. Most brands probably only need ShopPay, PayPal, and maybe one other option.

One big criticism with Shopify historically was that there was never an easy way to apply a discount code on the cart page. You always had to go to checkout. At Hint, when we switched from WooCommerce to Shopify, people were dropping off on the cart page because they didn't see where to enter a coupon code. We didn't realize they thought they had to go all the way to checkout. Native had to build a custom version to apply coupons at the cart page level. That change definitely improved conversion rates. Everyone should have discount code entry on the cart page, not just at checkout.
