# Limited Supply: Refreshingly Real Takes on D2C

Stamps.com was purchased by private equity firm Thoma Bravo for $6.6 billion about two years ago. The company owns ShipStation and ShippingEasy, effectively consolidating the shipping software category. When customers call to inquire about the software and mention they were considering ShippingEasy, they're told that's also owned by Stamps.com. This is a textbook private equity move that's excellent for business but terrible for consumers. The company was originally undervalued because it charged so little for its SaaS software. Native Deodorant used ShipStation for years while doing $70 million annually and only paid about $200 per month. Unlike other software companies that scale pricing with volume, ShipStation didn't increase costs proportionally, making it attractive for acquisition. Since the private equity takeover, pricing has become increasingly aggressive and customer experience has declined significantly.

Sweetgreen is a publicly traded company with a $1.1 billion market cap doing $470 million in revenue with 186 stores across the country. The company has 6,000 employees, averaging about 30 employees per store. With $130 million in cost of goods sold and $147 million in labor costs, labor actually exceeds COGS. Q1 and Q4 are slower due to cold weather. The company maintains 11 core signature salads at every location and rotates five seasonal salads five times per year. Custom salads are their most popular item, and 21% of all orders come through marketplace channels like DoorDash and Caviar. Same-store sales increased 13% year over year, and they added 36 new stores in 2022, generating $2.9 million in sales per store annually.

Lunya filed for bankruptcy recently after appearing to be a successful direct-to-consumer brand focused on comfortable, temperature-regulating apparel. The founding story presented to the public was that the founder invested $50,000 of personal savings to build the brand to $25 million in revenue. However, bankruptcy filings reveal the family actually invested tens of millions of dollars into the business. The founder, Ashley Merrill, was featured in countless podcasts, conferences, and magazine covers as an example of successful entrepreneurship. The family owned a swanky Malibu house that served as a vacation property, suggesting significant wealth behind the scenes. This is a cautionary tale about the gap between public narratives and financial reality in the D2C space.

The Pill Club, a birth control delivery service, was charged with Medi-Cal fraud. The California Department of Justice alleged that the company billed Medi-Cal for services it never provided, specifically submitting claims for 30-minute face-to-face counseling sessions with nurse practitioners when no direct or real-time contact with patients actually occurred. The company settled for $18.3 million and its assets were sold. The business model appeared brilliant on paper: advertise free birth control, send it to customers in branded boxes with chocolates and stickers, bill insurance companies, and profit the difference. However, the regulatory complexity of getting insurance approval and the actual reimbursement rates proved far more challenging than anticipated.

30 Madison acquired the assets of the Pill Club out of bankruptcy for $32 million, including 100,000 patient files and intellectual property. If valuing the IP at zero, this works out to $320 per patient file, which is a substantial customer acquisition cost. However, 30 Madison likely has the infrastructure and customer base to make this acquisition profitable long-term. Most telehealth and prescription delivery companies partner with existing manufacturers and pharmacies rather than producing their own medications, focusing instead on the customer acquisition and prescription management sides of the business.

Apple announced in iOS 17 that they would automatically remove tracking parameters from links, moving toward their private click measurement system. This severely limits the ability to use UTM parameters for attribution. Previously, marketers could use multiple UTM parameters to track campaign, channel, medium, and other data points. The new system appears to restrict this significantly, though details remain unclear. This change hurts Facebook's advertising tracking capability since they rely on UTM parameters to measure ad performance in their ad manager. The move pushes advertisers toward Apple's own ad platform while limiting the effectiveness of third-party attribution tools. Companies like Triple Whale and Northbeam are likely developing workarounds, possibly through custom URL structures that redirect after landing.

Apple has been strategically removing advertising tracking capabilities while building out their own advertising infrastructure, including a data management platform and demand-side platform. They don't spend on Facebook or Instagram advertising but are among the top advertisers on Twitter and TikTok. This pattern suggests Apple is consolidating power in advertising by removing competitors' tracking abilities while keeping their own advertising options available.

A founder in the bedding category observed on Twitter that while other direct-to-consumer brands appeared to be growing rapidly, his company felt like it was failing for the first two to three years. In hindsight, he realized he was simply being disciplined about spending while competitors were burning through capital on expensive agencies and high-budget creative shoots. Now, as those undisciplined brands struggle or go bankrupt, his company is in a position to acquire their assets. This pattern is likely to repeat as more direct-to-consumer companies fail and disciplined operators pick up their assets at distressed prices.

Marketing organization structure varies significantly by revenue stage. For companies doing zero to five million, you need maximum two people: a brand marketer overseeing press, influencer relationships, packaging, and design, plus a head of growth managing paid media agencies, site development, and email. From five to twenty-five million, split the growth role into a performance marketer and e-commerce manager, and add a content and social media person for four total. At twenty-five to fifty million, add a VP of marketing for optics, bring video editors in-house to reduce creative production costs, but keep PR, website development, and design outsourced since they're less frequent needs.

The specific marketing structure depends heavily on where the company has found success. A brand relying on podcast advertising needs different resources than one built on YouTube. A video-heavy brand like Mini Katana needs multiple video editors and fewer performance marketers, while beauty brands might need equal balance. Generic advice has limited value without understanding the company's specific channel mix and growth drivers.

Apple Vision Pro will likely transform how people consume digital content while traveling and working remotely. The ability to display a 40-inch screen without physically carrying a monitor is compelling for frequent travelers. Some San Francisco professionals are already traveling with portable second monitors, representing an extreme version of this problem that VR headsets could solve.

A refillery concept next to Whole Foods or similar upscale retailers could reduce plastic waste while offering price savings. Customers would bring their own bottles for shampoo, conditioner, laundry detergent, and other household products, refilling from bulk containers. The business could differentiate through design, scent customization, and transparent environmental impact tracking. However, competing with large companies like Procter & Gamble on margins would be extremely difficult. Automated vending machine dispensers could reduce labor costs. While the idea appeals to environmentally conscious consumers, scaling to mainstream customers would require competing on price with big retailers, which may not be feasible given the plastic savings advantage alone.
