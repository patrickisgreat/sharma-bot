# Limited Supply: Deep Dive Into Landing Pages

I was going through a lot of emails recently, and there's been consistent feedback on landing pages. There's always this pendulum swing between creative approaches—whether it's professionally done studio shots and cinemagraphs or user-generated content that crushes it. The same pendulum exists for post-click experiences, where sometimes your homepage outperforms a landing page, and other times your landing page outperforms everything. It really depends on who you're targeting. Are you targeting somebody who knows about the brand, or somebody who needs education on why they even need the product you're selling?

I really wanted to focus on landing pages because a lot of times we talk about growth marketing as something that's siloed to Facebook ads or media buying. But growth marketing should be a full funnel approach. It's not just about optimizing Facebook ads. It's about testing whitelisting, advertorials, landing pages, bid structures, and everything else. But growth marketing also applies to your Instagram bio, your pinned posts, the URLs behind your profiles, what URLs you give to influencers, whether coupon codes are automatically applied, how you're tracking and segmenting customers based on UTMs, email pop-ups, whether your pop-up is capturing emails effectively, your welcome flow optimization, and if you're doing PR work, whether you're maximizing those relationships through affiliate platforms.

One of the biggest levers within growth marketing that most brands don't test is landing pages. If you were to ask me what's one of the bigger levers you can play with that most brands don't, it's landing pages. It's basically everything that happens after somebody clicks an ad. Regardless of how good your media buying or creative is, if whatever happens after the click isn't doing the job, you're screwed. You're just wasting dollars on the paid side. Your ad is supposed to get a click. Your ad isn't supposed to sell somebody right away. Once they get to your site or landing page, that's where you have to sell them. If your landing page, website, product page, or collections page isn't actively focused on bringing that customer down the funnel, you're wasting money. Most brands mess this up. They run hundreds of thousands of dollars a month on media and creative and agencies to support that, and then people click and end up on a site that looks like it was built in 1990 on a plain Shopify template, with no optimized cart, no upsells, no bundling, no merchandising, and no new customer offer.

The first thing I want to talk about is the difference between the problem versus product funnel. There are two funnels that most people play within, but I think there's a third one—funnel zero—which comes before the first funnel. It's about grasping relatability with your audience. Let me give you an example with an infrared sauna blanket from Hiradose. Funnel one is educating people on why there's even a need for the product you're selling. If you're selling this blanket, maybe it's that their sleep is really bad, so you need to educate them on why infrared light and sauna products will help lead to better sleep. The second funnel is distinguishing why your product is the best option.

Take Joule as an example. Funnel zero could be the Joule water report, which essentially asks: did you realize how bad the water is that you're showering in? Funnel one educates them on the need for clean water and what that does for them benefits-wise. It makes it about them, so they care enough to get to funnel two. Funnel two answers why Joule is the best option for this product currently on the market.

You have to understand your objective when running ad traffic. Is it to sell somebody on the individual product, or is it to educate them and get them to understand what the need is? Some landing pages can do this in one step if you have a product that's a bigger impulse buy. Other brands have to break these out into two separate things. Your upper funnel does cold prospecting with people you're educating on why. Funnel one is literally "why brand name question mark." Funnel two is "why this one question mark."

There are several types of landing pages that work well. The first is a hero bundle or hero product landing page. This focuses on a specific bundle you're merchandising for new customers or a new product. For example, supplement brands create net new customer offers like half off your first month subscription plus a free mixer, carrying case, or meditation app access. You can use these pages to create the entire experience. You've got a merchandised bundle, similar to walking into Costco and seeing pre-made bundles when you buy a video game—it comes with an Xbox, extra controller, and free games. The idea is to put somebody on a red carpet, help them understand what the product is, why it exists, who it's for, how it benefits them, and when it arrives. You're answering these questions repeatedly and making a really good offer. They should scroll this page without having to leave and ask Google or ChatGPT anything. Everything from social proof to comparison charts to reasons to believe should be on this page. If you can get one of these pages cranking with good conversion rates, you can easily triple or quadruple your normal conversion rate.

The second type I love is a listicle landing page. I often pair a Facebook ad that goes to a listicle page, which then sends people to a hero bundle page, product page, or collections page depending on where you want to go. Listicles perform phenomenally and are easy to build. You can go through your reviews and create a tally of traits and benefits. For example, with Caraway cookware, you might find that it's nonstick and non-toxic, easy to clean, requires less oil because of its nonstick properties. You create tallies for each benefit and then sort them from most to least tallies, taking the top seven, five, or nine. You build a listicle like "Seven Reasons Why Home Chefs Can't Get Enough of Caraway Cookware," starting with the most talked-about benefit. Throughout the page, every two or three benefits, you include a CTA like "Choose My Color" or "Explore the Cookware Set." You've basically taken what people are actually talking about and reversed it to match what consumers are looking for. Looking at TikTok, Reddit, and customer service questions helps you understand what people are confused about and what you can address in a listicle. You can now download all your reviews and give ChatGPT a prompt to sort the top five, seven, or nine most talked-about benefits and provide supporting reviews as evidence. Judge success on this page by click-through rate to the next page. Anything under 30% means you're missing the mark. You want to aim for 40 to 60% click-through rate, meaning about half the people who read end up going to the landing page. Your conversion rate on the landing page should obviously be higher from listicle traffic.

Another type that works well for apparel, high SKU categories, and online retailers like Pet Meds or Chewy is collection style landing pages. These combine the concept of a landing page—educating people, giving social proof, answering questions—with the ability to view a larger number of SKUs in a collections format. Collections pages get so much engagement because people can scroll through them. If a collections page is done nicely, you can swipe through, look at different colors or variants without having to click through each one. These perform well. You add key elements you'd normally have in a landing page—social proof, nice hero section explaining the product and its benefit, comparison charts. Break up products every eight to twelve SKUs and reiterate social proof, comparison charts, or unboxing videos showing how the product looks or what sets your brand's experience apart. You can supercharge a collections page by taking the concept of a landing page and making it your active collections page. Some brands think this feels too marketery, but with modern advertising, I don't think it's a big issue.

For funnel one, specifically when answering "why brand question mark," a social proof landing page works really well. This works especially well for high AOV products and brands selling something expensive for their category. If you tend to be one of those premium brands selling something more expensive for the category, social proof pages do really well, as long as your product delivers on everything it says. This means embedding TikTok clips, Instagram stories, posts people have put up. I'm even a fan of taking screenshots of Facebook ad comments or text conversations because these are perceived as much harder to fake, which creates higher trust with viewers. TikTok comments are another incremental way to add social proof and help with conversion rate. TikTok Shop reviews are another underused resource because brands try to isolate traffic to TikTok Shop. But there's no reason you wouldn't take those screenshots or integrate them somehow. The framing of TikTok Shop reviews is powerful because consumers know you can't fake them, whereas press articles, blog articles, influencers, and customer reviews are perceived as potentially fakeable. Screenshots of ad comments, texts, or TikTok Shop reviews are much harder to fabricate.

A lead gen style page is useful pre-launch, post-launch, or in advance of launching a new product. It's essentially a form collection landing page. We recently launched POV Beauty for a beauty influencer. We used this type of page pre-launch where you could enter your email and phone number. Post-launch, we sold out inventory in a couple of hours and switched to a sold-out version. One version was a full site takeover saying we're out of stock and asking for your email in the hero. Conversion rates are really high. If you're using this for a pre-launch, this is your opportunity to reiterate why people love the brand, provide social proof, give reasons to believe in the new product, and build excitement. If you're launching a product without treating it like a big deal, you're missing out on revenue. There's no negative impact to building an additional list out of the one you already have. Sure, you'll email your whole list, but having a group of people ready to buy the product is valuable.

Lemmy does a really good job treating every product launch like a whole brand launch. Sniff does well collecting SMS launch lists for products prior to launching. Another example is New York or Nowhere, a clothing brand that builds beautiful wallpaper packs—essentially a ten-image zip file—and runs traffic to them for free. They still check out on Shopify, becoming customers and opting into emails and texts. When they have a product drop, they have a massive addressable list. You want to keep building more targeted or segmented lists so you have opportunities as you continue launching new stuff.

The BYOB—build your own bundle—is a page I've been using for eight or nine years. It's a simple concept similar to going to Costco, mixing and matching beverage flavors, and buying your mix. Online, you choose flavors you want, build your pack, get a discount. This works really well for net new customers in the second funnel. Once somebody is educated and ready to make a purchase, having them build their own bundle and get a discount works well. You still have education components depending on your AOV and what stage of the funnel these people are in. If you sell any consumable product, you should absolutely have a BYOB page. I'd test it even if you think it won't work. If you sell hair care or sweatpants, try it anyway. Engagement rates are insanely high, and you can usually triple the site conversion rate just by driving traffic here, especially qualified prospecting traffic.

The last type of landing page is article and blog style landing pages. If you scroll on Facebook or Instagram, you'll absolutely see examples. This is where publishers advertise on behalf of a brand. You don't see BarkBox running the ad; you see a publisher running the Facebook ad, looking like it's coming from a publisher because there's no "learn more" or "shop now" CTA. It goes to an article about being a new pet parent, figuring out what products your new puppy needs, or what you need to be aware of as your dog gets old. It leads with value—something funny, insightful, educational, or relatable. For Caraway, it might be an article about moving into a new apartment and not knowing what to buy. You find somebody and connect with them. Then they click, start reading, get your buy-in, and then you can sell them Caraway.

This concept of advertorials—from "ad" and "editorial"—is one of the biggest, most underrated tactics that exists right now. You can expect CPAs to be 30% lower, and as a result, you're able to educate people significantly more. If you sell in retail, this is a no-brainer because people may not buy right away, which is fine, but you'll have higher conversion rates from traffic coming here, then they'll buy in retail. At Hint, we ran an incrementality study on advertorials seven or eight years ago looking at target sales. We did this recently with another brand and realized that new audience reach shot up significantly as a result of advertorials because advertorials are a much nicer way to finesse your way into a net new customer audience. Imagine walking on the beach in Mexico and somebody comes up trying to sell you something—you want them to get away. But if somebody compliments your watch or shorts, they're smoothing their way in. That's what an advertorial does—it smooths the entrance right where the ad is placed in the article.
