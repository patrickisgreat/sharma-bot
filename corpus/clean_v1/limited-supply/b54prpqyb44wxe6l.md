# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

You may remember that I hosted an event called the Q4 Summit with Ecom Founders, and during the morning sessions I had the opportunity to speak with Aaron Nospich, founder and CEO of Breeze, the cannabis drink brand. Aaron has experienced explosive growth, and the company is on track to do approximately seventy million dollars in revenue this year, completely bootstrapped. What makes Aaron particularly interesting is his transparency about his business metrics, sharing everything from his COGS to his P&L publicly.

When I first met Aaron a few years ago at a conference in Los Angeles, he told me he was going to build a weed brand into something massive. I thought he was crazy, especially given the challenges of the beverage industry. Beverage is notoriously difficult to scale, particularly direct-to-consumer, with complications around weight, shipping, packaging, and breakage along the way.

Aaron explained that his approach to building Breeze started with identifying a personal need and looking for where he could add value. He noticed he was drinking a lot of alcohol, and while it was fun, it came with significant consequences. He was also smoking cannabis, but traditional consumption methods had their own issues. Smoking affects your lungs, and edibles hit thirty to sixty minutes later with unpredictable effects. He wanted to create something that would deliver the positive aspects of both experiences without the downsides.

So Breeze was born as a THC, CBD, and lion's mane beverage. The THC comes from hemp, which became legal at certain concentrations under the 2018 farm bill. Aaron emphasized the importance of actual product innovation, noting that many e-commerce businesses forget they can innovate and create real competitive moats. The formula they developed creates a happy, euphoric, uplifting feeling that hits in about five minutes and wears off in an hour to an hour and a half. It bypasses the liver so you don't get the typical edible feeling, and it's a clean, chill experience.

They launched on April 20th and did one point two five million dollars in their first eight months. Their first full year brought in twenty-eight point nine million dollars, and they're pacing to hit somewhere between fifty to seventy-five million dollars this year, all while remaining profitable throughout.

From the beginning, Aaron thought about Breeze as a platform rather than a single product. The goal was to create a range of feel-good tonics that would allow people to tap in, turn up, or tune out depending on the vibe they wanted, all under one centralized brand. He referenced how Coca-Cola owns multiple brands like Sprite and Coke, but you don't drink them as "Sprite by Coca-Cola." He wanted to do something different, more like the Apple model where everything is unified under one ecosystem. The idea was to create a beverage platform that could eventually become the biggest beverage brand in the world by combining traditional beverage innovation with an internet layer that most beverage companies never use.

While Aaron started with direct-to-consumer, he's now expanded to retail and successfully balanced both channels. When they launched on Sprouts, they gave the retailer four months of inventory, which sold out in two weeks, making it the largest product launch Sprouts had ever experienced. This happened because while people don't really want to buy beverages online, they absolutely want to find them in stores once they know about the brand. The awareness generated through online marketing creates demand that transfers beautifully to retail.

Aaron emphasized an important perspective on scale in entrepreneurship. Most people in e-commerce get excited about hitting one hundred million in revenue as a major milestone. But he encouraged thinking bigger. Billion-dollar companies are created regularly by entrepreneurs building legitimate value. With the internet, AI, and modern logistics infrastructure, the opportunity is genuinely massive compared to what was possible even decades ago.

When it comes to paid channels, Aaron has explored various platforms but emphasized the importance of staying tapped into where the market is shifting and testing whether that shift applies to your specific brand. He hired affiliates early on TikTok Shop when he saw the platform's potential, running what was essentially a mass outreach campaign to creators. They had creators make videos about the product and ran ad spend behind the content, which exploded quickly. While TikTok can be unprofitable as a direct channel for most brands, Aaron sees its real value as a demand engine that drives overflow traffic to DTC sales, Amazon, and retail.

On the creative side, Aaron's approach is deliberately raw and authentic. He uses his iPhone to record videos that feel like he's FaceTiming friends rather than creating polished commercials. People are tired of high-production bullshit, he noted. There's a level of energy and authenticity in authentic content that people can feel deeply. His most successful ad was literally him calling customers and asking them about their experience. One memorable campaign involved him calling a customer who had just ordered, then flipping a coin to decide whether to refund their money. The customer won the refund, it cost him five hundred dollars, but when he ran it as an ad, it generated approximately five hundred thousand dollars in revenue.

Aaron talked about the importance of offers, which he sees as a black box that many people don't fully understand. An offer isn't just about discounting. It's about how you present the value of your product. It could be a variety pack with multiple products, or bundling with bonus items. The key is making people feel like they're getting something or getting one over. By creating different offers and iterating through them quickly, you can optimize conversion rates while keeping customers interested and engaged.

Regarding brand and performance marketing, Aaron is adamant that these aren't opposing forces. He combines organic content shot authentically and run as paid ads with performance optimization. This hybrid approach has been crucial to Breeze's success. He defines a brand as a capsule of meaning where everything you do under that brand becomes part of what it means. Truth and authenticity resonate at a deeper level than persuasion tactics. His recommendation is to bring everything as close to authenticity as possible, capture it on video, and put it out there.

Looking at last year's Black Friday and Cyber Monday results, Aaron acknowledged significant mistakes. They started their promotion way too early in October to capture Halloween and Thanksgiving, but the extended promotion caused them to become a discount brand for two to three months. By the time Black Friday actually arrived, he could feel they were completely off the wave. He called for help from me and Brandon, made major changes in real time on Black Friday itself, and relaunched the promotion, which helped them capture at least some of the wave. His lesson from that experience is that if you're going to run a longer promotion, change it significantly before Black Friday and Cyber Monday, not with small tweaks but with big, novel changes that feel different.

For this year, Aaron's main innovation is integrating retail and digital simultaneously. While retailers were initially nervous about competing with DTC, he found that driving awareness through digital channels actually brings net new customers into physical stores, who then buy other products, ultimately making retailers more money. He's running Black Friday and Cyber Monday promotions early on both channels while planning a significant refresh right before the actual event to avoid getting lost in the noise.

Aaron raised an important point about AI and language models actively training on content that currently exists on the internet. If you're not writing your own story and putting it out there, these systems will make up their own narratives about you. He shared a personal example about his partner Nick Shackelford, who had joined the company to help with marketing but whom GPT wrongly identified as a co-founder rather than a partner. While this initially hurt his ego, Aaron realized that whatever gets attention to your brand ultimately funnels back to the truth. He learned that removing the ego attachment to "I did this" and instead focusing on what the brand is becoming is ultimately more valuable.

His final advice combined humility with ambition. Take yourself out of the equation, recognize that whatever is driving attention to your brand is generally good, and focus on building something real rather than claiming credit for it.
