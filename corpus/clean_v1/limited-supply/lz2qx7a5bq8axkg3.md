# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Aaron Nospisch is the founder of Breeze, a feel-good social tonic brand that has become one of the fastest-growing beverages in the country. Breeze makes microdose cannabis and mushroom-infused drinks, as well as non-infused versions. The company launched on April 20th, 2023, and grew from $1.25 million in its first eight months to $28.9 million in its first full year—a 19x year-over-year growth.

Aaron started his entrepreneurial journey at 13 years old, designing t-shirts on Photoshop and selling them through Big Cartel while sponsoring bands on MySpace. He later became the CMO of Monk, a portable aromatherapy diffuser company, scaling it to $15 million annually in two years. After being pushed out of that position despite believing he owned equity, he started a CBD brand in 2018 when the farm bill legalized hemp. When Facebook and Instagram shut down his advertising efforts repeatedly, he recognized a major problem to solve.

He founded Lucid, an advertising agency specializing in cannabis and hemp product marketing. The agency became the largest cannabis social advertising agency within five years. Through Lucid, Aaron watched market trends evolve from CBD to Delta-8 THC to Delta-9 THC extracted from hemp. When he discovered that people were excited about cannabis beverages but found them too sedating for social situations, he saw an opportunity. He met people at a cannabis and psychedelic convention in Miami who had a nano-emulsified lion's mane extract that created an instantly bioavailable rush feeling. He combined this with low-dose THC, CBD, and lion's mane to create Breeze.

Aaron emphasizes that brand is fundamentally "a capsule for meaning" where everything the company does defines what it is. He was intentional about every aspect—colors, can design, who supported the brand early, and who the customers would be. He applied lessons from his e-commerce background to build Breeze's infrastructure from day one. High unit economics were critical; knowing that ad costs continuously rise, he focused on high average order value, subscription-first approach, and products with stickiness to build long-term customer value.

He kept the e-commerce system lean and aerodynamic, starting with one homepage and one product page for the first year. The system was designed to be rebuilt quickly as the business scaled, supporting everything from high-price premium positioning to rapid testing of new offers. Aaron passed shipping costs to customers unless orders exceeded $100, which in the beverage industry—where shipping is typically prohibitively expensive—was a novel approach that enabled the business model to work.

On timing and market awareness, Aaron notes that people, especially younger generations, have been pulling back from alcohol and becoming more health-conscious. He launched Breeze at a moment when consumers were searching for alternatives that provided a similar social experience without the downsides. His own experience of quitting drinking but missing what alcohol provided socially informed the product development. He felt that whatever part of his own desire was shared by a broader population.

Regarding distribution strategy, Aaron recognized that in retail, it's a game of shelf space, while online it's about who's best at bringing customers to their virtual shelf. He built Breeze to dominate the digital aisle. He created a non-infused product line to access channels where THC products couldn't be sold, like Amazon and many traditional grocery stores. This allowed him to build a broader brand identity as a feel-good tonic company rather than just a cannabis product.

When TikTok Shop launched, Aaron recognized it as revolutionary—a platform that would convert all users into potential affiliates. He leaned in early, and Breeze became the number one TikTok Shop beverage within weeks. While not always the most profitable channel individually, the halo effect on website sales and Meta performance made it clearly valuable. He learned important lessons about positioning, ensuring the same product message across channels to avoid customer confusion about whether they were getting infused or non-infused versions.

Aaron approached AppLovin similarly, recognizing that being able to advertise cannabis and THC directly in a channel with no restrictions gave Breeze a competitive advantage over other brands that couldn't make such clear value propositions. His high unit economics allowed him to win those auctions profitably when other brands couldn't.

On TikTok's algorithm versus Meta's, Aaron notes that TikTok's algorithm moves so fast that creatives rot quickly, but this is because the algorithm is so effective at distributing valuable content to the right people immediately. Success requires creating something worth sharing. He found that directly responding ads weren't effective, but when TikTok Shop integrated micro-influencer content with spark ads similar to Meta whitelisting, clear attribution and sales drove profitability. He sent 500 to 1,000 products weekly to new creators and focused on creator quality and content rather than follower counts.

Aaron acknowledges the TikTok Shop game has changed. Today's success requires building a strong group of indoctrinated creators who deeply understand the product and can pump out frequent, varied, high-quality content, then amplifying and rewarding those creators at scale. He emphasizes this channel remains undervalued but challenging, especially as competitors give away expensive incentives.

On red flags he sees in founder pitches, Aaron identifies several critical issues. Most founders don't think big enough—solving meaningful problems that impact humans' lives significantly rather than launching yet another commodity variation of an existing category. They lack true innovation underlying their products. They don't have unique channels, matrices, or systems that leverage their specific skills and advantages. Most importantly, they lack a big enough vision for how their product integrates into the world long-term.

Aaron explains that in early technology cycles, tech differentiation alone is often sufficient for success. But as cycles mature, true underlying innovation becomes essential. Most e-commerce brands don't understand what innovation actually means. Innovation isn't necessarily difficult to accomplish, but it requires proper iteration that goes beyond existing possibilities and invites people into that larger story.

On brand feeling and emotion, Aaron notes that people remember how brands make them feel. He wanted Breeze to create such compelling desire that seeing it would make people wonder what it was until trying it. He deliberately cultivated mystery, desirability, and excitement in the visual and brand experience. The product should make you feel something when you drink it, and the brand should tap into humanity's desire for expansion and more.

Aaron learned from the CBD market cycle that once a category gets commoditized, brands race to the bottom on price. He deliberately chose a different path with Breeze—creating a premier, desirable experience worth paying for while remaining accessible. He uses alcohol as an example: every type of alcohol contains the same active ingredient, ethanol, yet the industry supports a wide landscape of products and prices with large businesses at every level. If alcohol can create such diversity selling a single compound, Breeze selling a unique mixture of high-quality compounds could command premium pricing based on the value delivered.

From his agency experience, Aaron learned that building an accomplished brand requires capital. Rather than fearing returning to employment temporarily, he reframes it as strategic resource acquisition for longer-term goals. He also learned that while agency can generate income, it's unsustainably demanding long-term—a "what have you done for me lately" business. The key learning was understanding what fundamentals are required at each stage to build resources for the next stage.

Success, Aaron emphasizes, never comes from a single variable. It's the combination of multiple variables aligning at the right time with the ability to execute. For Breeze, that meant a genuinely innovative product addressing a real consumer need, launched at the moment consumers were actively seeking alternatives to alcohol, using his unique expertise in both e-commerce and cannabis marketing, with premium positioning and subscription-first unit economics, entering new channels early before competition, and staying connected to the community that drove the category forward.
