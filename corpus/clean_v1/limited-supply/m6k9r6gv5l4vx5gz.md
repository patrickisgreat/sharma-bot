# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Danny, the founder of HexClad, built a billion-dollar cookware brand over the last eleven years. When asked how he accomplished this, he credits his upbringing in an Italian and Jewish household where food was central to family life. His grandmother, an Italian immigrant who became the first female head chef in Buffalo, New York in the 1950s, instilled a passion for the food space that influenced his career path.

Danny worked for another cookware company early on and pitched the importance of Facebook and community building to the owner in 2010. When the owner dismissed the idea, Danny and his co-founder decided to start their own company. Rather than becoming a chef himself—a path he felt he wasn't talented or crazy enough for—he wanted to find another way into the food space.

The key to HexClad's brand identity was storytelling. Danny recognized that cookware alone wouldn't be enough. When he discovered the hexagonal technology, he had to tell a compelling story about what these hexagons were and why they mattered. He diagnosed what the brand should be and created multiple ways to present that identity. In 2015-2016, he decided HexClad would be "badass cookware." The concept of being badass applies universally—to grandmothers taking care of families, ten-year-olds making their first grilled cheese, and single mothers. This foundation guided everything from their first pan sold in November 2016 onward.

Before HexClad, Danny had tried to build a juicer business, which failed spectacularly. The juicer went from being a business on Tuesday at 5 p.m. to being completely abandoned by Wednesday at 8 a.m. This humbling experience taught him a critical lesson: he couldn't impose his will on consumers. He had to respect the market and listen to actual pain points. This learning shaped his approach to HexClad.

When seeking funding for HexClad, Danny faced rejection from investors who believed cookware was too competitive and that people wouldn't buy cookware direct-to-consumer. Danny maintained conviction, knowing he wanted to approach the marketplace through e-commerce and that he could tell the brand story effectively. He hired smart people to handle the operational and technical aspects of e-commerce while he focused on branding and strategy.

For product launches, HexClad maintains a philosophy of reinvention. If they can't reinvent the wheel with a product—like their pepper mill, which became "a weapon"—then they focus on making products sexy and badass, like their knives. Products that don't fit either category stay in the drawing room.

HexClad transformed itself from a product company into a media and lifestyle brand through content strategy. This decision came a few years ago when Danny and Jason, the president of the company, decided that content would be vital to becoming a lifestyle brand. They began sending products to Michelin-starred chefs, building relationships, and eventually established a partnership with Gordon Ramsay.

The Gordon Ramsay partnership began simply. In summer 2020, an Instagram manager noticed that Gordon was following HexClad. A message was sent, and they received a response indicating Gordon had been sent a pan and really liked it. Within less than a week, Danny was on a call with Gordon's business manager. The manager suggested Gordon might do a commercial for two million dollars, but indicated Gordon preferred to be more involved since he's hands-on. During his call with Gordon, he expressed enthusiasm about the pans and suggested cooking risotto in his restaurant.

What impressed Danny most was that Gordon sent a video promoting HexClad before the deal was even finalized. Gordon regularly checks on business metrics, asking about Prime Day numbers and showing genuine investment in the company. Danny credits the Gordon partnership significantly with HexClad becoming the fastest-growing cookware brand globally, though he acknowledges other smart decisions and quick recognition of mistakes contributed to the success.

On promotional strategy, Danny believes in promotions but dislikes calling them discounts, which he associates with bruised fruit. HexClad plans promotional offerings well in advance due to the months-long production timeline. They target specific occasions like Prime Day with compelling offers. The company's strategy works because they have a high average order value and know the lifetime value is almost double the average order value, meaning customers return. Rather than wanting to be like Apple, which relies on planned obsolescence, Danny wants to be like Sony—a trusted brand known for quality and backing its products. HexClad offers lifetime guarantees because he wants customers to use products for a lifetime, not repurchase the same items, but rather expand into complementary categories like knives, pepper mills, and cutting boards.

For customer acquisition at scale, HexClad employs three strategies: acquiring new customers with hero products, retaining existing customers by bringing them into new product categories, and opening new marketplaces. Their average customer is over 35, the typical age when people invest in quality cookware. However, the brand is increasingly reaching younger customers. To build long-term awareness, HexClad does challenges with younger audiences like MasterChef Junior participants, introducing the brand to people before they're ready to make significant cookware purchases.

The company's retail strategy is selective. Besides HexClad.com and Amazon, they partner with Costco. They don't aggressively pursue additional retail channels. Instead, they focus on developing new quality products and treating customers with utmost respect. Danny remains personally involved in customer service, sometimes responding to complaints on social media and emails. This personal touch creates brand advocates who continue purchasing across product categories.

For geographic expansion, HexClad is successfully entering new markets. Europe, the UK, and Australia are already performing well. They're launching in China, Japan, the Middle East, South Africa, and potentially India by year-end. Gordon Ramsay's global fame provides a significant advantage in these markets—when he appeared in Korea, crowds mobbed him like the Beatles at Shea Stadium.

For Q4 specifically, HexClad focuses heavily on hexclad.com since that's where the company owns the customer relationship. They spend less on Amazon during this period and have increased Costco.com investment as that platform has grown in importance. Costco remains an unusual but powerful retail partner—while its website looks outdated and frequently breaks, its revenue is incredible. Customers at Costco regularly purchase high-ticket items, spending $400 on cookware without hesitation.

Danny's key takeaway for Q4 success is viewing it as a twelve-month period, not a single quarter. HexClad plants seeds in January through July, knowing they won't see results until Q4. This requires patience and a long-term perspective. Many companies try to capture revenue immediately, but this short-term thinking damages brand building. The critical distinction is understanding what's a short-term goal versus a long-term goal, and this distinction permeates every business decision.

Playing the long game is the only way to retain customers and build a brand that transitions from being a product to being a genuine brand. When customers sense that a company is only focused on extracting money today, they smell it. By contrast, companies committed to long-term value build deep customer relationships. For Q4, once HexClad determines the product offering they want to present, they focus on planting seeds from March through July, allowing them to reap the harvest in the final quarter.
