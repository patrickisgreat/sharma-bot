# Limited Supply - Season 9, Episode 12

The fundamental principle of turning customers into brand ambassadors is that it has to come from the brand itself. You cannot simply be selling wellness products, powders, supplements, and gummies and then suddenly expect customers to become ambassadors. Instead, you need to be a brand that customers want to publicly associate with. This is similar to how someone decides to retweet content on Twitter or reshare something on Facebook—they're using their own real estate to promote something. When you ask customers to become brand ambassadors, you're essentially asking them to become a billboard for your brand. They'll only do this if your brand is cool enough that they want to. There's no software or silver bullet strategy that will turn a mediocre brand into an ambassador powerhouse.

Regarding organic traffic opportunities, YouTube influencer ads are highly effective because the links stay in video descriptions forever. Whether someone watches a video today or three years from now, they'll still see the ad in the description. This evergreen placement means you continue to reap the rewards long after the initial investment.

Blog content and SEO require strategic distribution. Many brands make the mistake of simply posting blog links on social media without any creative hook or context. You need to treat each platform differently. For every blog post, consider creating a Twitter thread explaining the key points, a shortened version on Medium, a LinkedIn post from the founder, TikToks highlighting interesting facts, and Instagram stories with teasers linking back to the full article. Use vanity links instead of just telling people to click the link in bio. The goal is to plant breadcrumbs everywhere and get as many eyeballs on the content as possible.

Creating more organic content is essential, and there's no silver bullet here—you either spend money on ads and influencers, or you create the content yourself and do it well. Content can take many forms: addressing your top 25 most-asked questions through videos, partnering with smaller high-engagement creators for collaborative posts, seeding products to influencers, or running cohesive campaigns where all ad creative, emails, site messaging, and brand content share the same overarching theme.

On the question of omni-channel customer acquisition beyond Meta, TikTok, and Google, there are numerous options including out-of-home advertising, events, television ads, podcast sponsorships, and Amazon ads. The key is measuring properly. A platform like House can help you run incrementality tests to understand what's actually driving incremental revenue across retail, Amazon, and direct-to-consumer channels.

Regarding user-generated content, the fake influencer era of UGC is dead. People don't want to see failed actors poorly selling high-margin wellness supplements. However, genuine UGC from actual customers—people who bought the product, used it, and found real solutions—still works extremely well. Real customer stories about finding solutions to actual problems outperform scripted content. Realistic customer videos and compilations of verified purchases still drive strong performance.

For Black Friday and Cyber Monday, keep it simple. Consumers are already overwhelmed with emails and texts. Focus on a strong offer with good product variety, and communicate loudly about what that offer is. Send enough reminder emails to ensure people know what you're offering. Don't try to get too complicated with different creative for different days unless you have significant resources and customer volume. Most of the time, a simple discount, buy-one-get-one offer, or gift-with-purchase at a certain threshold works best. Anything more complicated confuses people who already have many brands competing for their attention.

Regarding post-purchase follow-ups, start with a post-purchase survey asking simple questions like how customers heard about the brand, how long they knew about it before purchasing, who they're buying for, and what motivated the purchase. Ask your top three or four expected answers but always include an "other" option. The follow-up strategy depends on what you're selling. For consumable products with high repeat purchase rates, prioritize selling additional products in the first few emails. For one-time purchases like weighted blankets, wait a couple of weeks after delivery before following up with surveys. During holiday seasons, adjust your questions to understand gift purchases better.

Balancing brand and performance marketing requires doing both in parallel. You cannot sacrifice one for the other. While you can theoretically reach ten to twelve million in revenue running only performance marketing, you'll hit a ceiling. The analogy is like paving a road: you need to smooth out the road with brand building first before running high-velocity performance marketing on top of it. Every successful brand first builds awareness and excitement through brand marketing, then capitalizes with strategic performance ads. You can't run an unbalanced funnel where one element is great while others are weak.

Achieving product-market fit on a limited budget can be done in four ways, from most to least thorough. First, run a pre-launch landing page collecting full purchases, then follow up with buyers asking for feedback and offering the product at a discount when it fully launches. Second, collect emails on a pre-launch landing page—this is what Jolie did with their water report and saw massive success. Third, understand click-through rates from ads to gauge if your messaging resonates without focusing on email collection. Fourth, the most authentic approach is simply putting out organic content on social platforms and seeing what naturally resonates with the market. Strawberry Milk Mob took this approach and is now crushing it.

For solopreneurs running Facebook ads with products at thirty-five to one hundred twenty dollar price points with eighty percent margins, the reality is this isn't a Facebook-friendly product structure. With an average order value of thirty-seven dollars and eighty percent margins yielding roughly thirty dollars in profit, you have to cover all your personal expenses, Facebook costs, and everything else from that margin. Facebook works best with either high AOV items over a couple hundred dollars or products with high consumption rates requiring repeat purchases. With low-price, non-consumable, non-repeating products, Facebook ads won't be profitable. Instead, focus on building brand awareness through other channels or find ways to increase the lifetime value of customers through complementary products and repeat purchases.

The key takeaway across all of these strategies is that sustainable growth requires building a real brand that people want to associate with, combined with strategic performance marketing amplified by proper measurement and testing. Silver bullets don't exist, but intentional, multi-channel approaches backed by incrementality testing and authentic brand building will get you to the scale you're looking for.
