# Limited Supply: Ryan Shores on True Classic, Creative Excellence, and Company Culture

People often approach creative work like stepping into a room with only a bucket of green paint. They paint it on the canvas and walk out, thinking that's sufficient because somewhere along the way they've convinced themselves that when it comes to digital ads, they can get away without being creative. We're living in a time where everyone is doing the same thing. What's great about being creative is that you instantly stand out when you create something genuinely good, because ninety percent of the market is completely mailing it in on creative. Businesses need to go deep on hiring creative thinkers and bring in a lot more of them.

Ryan Shores came on the podcast as the CEO of True Classic, a direct-to-consumer apparel brand that has grown explosively in recent years. What made this conversation particularly valuable wasn't just marketing tactics, but the CEO perspective on building a scalable business from the ground up. Ryan explained that when they first started True Classic, the three co-founders did essentially everything themselves for thirteen months before hiring their first employee. They kept operating expenses in the low single digits, which meant a lot of bleeding for an extended period, but it was necessary to reach a point where they could afford talent at the level they needed.

The turning point came when they brought on Ben, a former Facebook executive, as their president. This hire wasn't initially planned as a full-time role. Ryan had approached his co-founder Nick looking for someone at Facebook who could serve as an advisor on their media buying strategy. They found Ben and started with a weekly retainer arrangement. Over time, as the business grew and Ryan and Ben developed a genuine friendship, Ben kept reinforcing how special what they were building was. After True Classic hit one hundred million in revenue, Ryan had a dinner with Ben and asked what it would take to get him to join full-time. The negotiation took a while, but when Ben finally came on board in March 2022, the impact was immediate.

Within the first month, Ben uncovered a massive email deliverability issue. True Classic's open rates were sitting in the low twenties, while industry standards were closer to forty or fifty percent. Someone had told them that twenties were acceptable, but Ben knew better. He brought in a team to clean up their email infrastructure, and within three to four weeks, their open rates jumped to fifty. Since email is essentially the entire EBITDA game for True Classic, this single fix had enormous impact on profitability. Beyond that, Ben discovered they weren't segmenting their email list properly. They were essentially spamming their entire audience with every email rather than creating different paths for engaged customers, lapsed customers, and other segments. This was a fundamental revelation about the gap between consulting and having a world-class operator actually working inside your business full-time.

That experience changed how Ryan thought about hiring. Instead of bringing in people with prestigious pedigrees from Harvard or other elite institutions who often came off as knowing everything about everything, Ryan became focused on finding people who had proven track records in the direct-to-consumer space and who had high emotional intelligence. He had carried some bias against Harvard grads because of bad experiences with arrogance and lack of self-awareness, but the real issue wasn't where someone went to school. It was whether they could check their ego at the door and genuinely contribute to the culture.

This led to a critical insight about company culture. Ryan described a pattern they'd noticed with every high-performing person they'd had to let go: complete and utter lack of awareness. They didn't understand how they came off to other people. They didn't realize how they treated colleagues. And when they were fired, they were clueless about why it was happening. The common thread was low emotional intelligence. No matter how brilliant someone was technically, if they didn't have high EQ, they were eventually going to damage the culture. Ryan had come to believe that EQ needed to be literally number one when hiring, above IQ or pure technical capability.

To build a genuinely bulletproof culture, True Classic identified five core pillars. The first is going really fast—not recklessly, but with the understanding that as a startup, speed matters. The second is executing like a pro, which means doing your best work and following through on commitments. The third is moving the needle, focusing on work that actually creates value for the company rather than busy work. The fourth is being creative, which bleeds into every function from contract negotiations to logistics to customer service. And the fifth, which Ryan emphasized as most important, is leading with empathy.

To operationalize these values, Ryan implemented a system using an app called Matter. In a Slack channel, employees can give each other kudos tied to these five pillars. Every employee gets 250 coins per week that they can distribute to colleagues, and these coins can be redeemed for actual dollars. The coins expire at the end of the week, so people are incentivized to give them away. This simple system transformed the culture by making it a game where people are constantly recognizing each other and celebrating aligned behaviors.

When it comes to hiring, True Classic stopped using recruiters entirely because of the cost. Instead, Ryan's co-founder Nick is a machine on LinkedIn, reaching out directly to people in the D2C space with thoughtful messages. Rather than posting generic job descriptions, they figure out exactly what role they need and go find the killer they want. The best people aren't job hunting or responding to ads. They're heads-down working. You have to go get them.

One significant mistake they made was hiring people with traditional corporate backgrounds for roles like Chief Marketing Officer. When they interviewed CMOs from major companies, these candidates often couldn't answer basic direct-to-consumer questions. When asked what the most important metric on a website was for diagnosing growth, one candidate answered that time on site was important. That's corporate thinking, not D2C thinking. They eventually abandoned the search for a traditional CMO and created the role they actually needed: a growth-focused leader who understood performance marketing across channels.

For hiring at the director level and above, True Classic gives candidates homework assignments. They might be asked to forecast two years of business and explain how they'd allocate resources, then present their work to the full leadership team. Some candidates drop out when they see the assignment, which is actually useful information. If someone won't do a little work to earn the job, what kind of work ethic will they have once hired? This process has saved them enormous time and money that would have been wasted on bad hires.

Ryan emphasized that being upfront about the downsides of the job is crucial. People should know that working at a startup might mean getting pinged on Saturday mornings when something breaks. They might need to work late nights and potentially give up some weekends. They should understand the culture is casual but the work is intense. Better to scare people off upfront than have them come in with false expectations and resent the reality.

When it comes to compensation, Ryan and Ben built a calculator that shows employees exactly what they can expect to earn based on the value they create. For high-level executives, it's typically a significant combination of both cash and equity. The way someone structures a compensation negotiation is actually revealing. If they push hard on cash and light on equity, that telegraphs they're not planning to stick around long-term. True Classic tries to structure deals with more back-end upside so people are incentivized to stay and build something lasting.

On the creative side, Ryan described True Classic's journey from overly aggressive comedy that alienated women to finding a better balance. Early on, they had ads with physical violence and jokes that came off as contemptuous toward men or women. The algorithm loved the controversy, but the blowback in comments hurt the brand. They learned to walk the line between getting engagement and maintaining positive brand sentiment. The real pivot came when Ryan realized the game wasn't about selling. It was about creating emotional connection through making people feel something, whether that's humor, helpfulness, or delight.

They started adding details that they thought competitors would never add, like putting the size, height, and weight of the person modeling each piece of clothing on their ads and product images. This seems obvious in hindsight, but nobody was doing it. Customers loved it because they could see themselves in the product. This goes back to thinking about what the customer actually needs and being thoughtful about how to serve them better.

Ryan stressed that if you're going to do comedy in your ads, you need to come correct. It needs to be genuinely funny, Saturday Night Live level sketch writing. Otherwise it comes across as cringe. Most brands shouldn't even attempt comedy. They should focus on being informative and educational instead. Everyone is tired of the cool guy walking in the park wearing nice apparel. Stand out by providing actual value.

Another major element of True Classic's success is retention. Ryan explained that they're essentially break-even on acquiring new customers. They only make money when customers come back and purchase again. This means email and SMS are the entire EBITDA game. Many brands are still obsessed with driving top-line revenue growth, but in today's climate, private equity and other investors only care about EBITDA and the history of it. Valuation multiples are compressed, and risk-averse investors want to see proven, sustainable profits.

True Classic went through the process of talking to numerous private equity companies and venture investors about funding rounds, but Ryan came to realize the deals weren't good. They'd offer half the valuation he expected, with heavy structuring in the company's favor, not his. Since True Classic is profitable, they don't need the money unless they were going to do something massive like open a hundred retail locations. So Ryan stopped taking calls. He'd rather run the best business he can and maintain control. In his words, he used to be all about the deal and the valuation, but now he just wants his customers to be happy and his employees to be happy.

The core principle Ryan keeps coming back to is intentionality. His LinkedIn profile, in the About section, simply says "being intentional." This isn't just about the product. It's about being intentional in customer service, surprising and delighting people when they're upset, being intentional with the culture through systems like the kudos app, being intentional with creative by making people laugh and creating emotional connection, and being intentional in literally every touchpoint. Even when speaking at an award ceremony where some people wouldn't win, he made sure to give the audience something of value they could take home, offering True Classic as a donation arm for charities and families in need. This commitment to intentionality across every function is what Ryan believes explains True Classic's success.
