# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

Ian gave a background of his unconventional path into business. He studied international policy and nuclear naval security before accidentally entering banking, which he disliked. He then moved into venture capital, but eventually felt compelled to build rather than invest. His first company, Newspix, was a business app that allowed CEOs and thought leaders to comment directly on news stories. The acquisition taught him he didn't enjoy app building, leading him to start Oceans, a high-skill operational offshoring firm that has been running successfully for four years.

Oceans works with hundreds of startups, big companies, mid-market firms, and e-commerce brands across diverse verticals. Their customer base ranges from dental offices and medical practices to major DTC brands and media companies. One particularly unique customer owns the Northwestern American market for hazardous materials recycling. The company appeals especially to e-commerce and DTC brands because founders in that space understand the severe impact of inventory on cash flow, making them constantly look for cost-cutting opportunities.

The conversation turned to how elite e-commerce operators are restructuring their teams. Several brands Nik spoke with this week had done a complete reset on their internal e-commerce team and adopted a playbook featuring one senior person and one or two offshore talent members. This three-person team can handle a direct consumer business doing around thirty million dollars without issues. This works because of advanced tooling and increased efficiency in how work gets done.

The strategic gap between the United States and other countries is significant. America moves quickly and remains the world expert in selling things and marketing effectively. Most countries are three to fifteen years behind the US in marketing and selling domestically. This creates an opportunity where teams move the strategic layer to the US with people who understand the market and are at the cutting edge, while moving support and execution layers offshore. This approach allows teams to accomplish much more with less.

The topic of inventory and cash flow problems led to discussion about how having inventory keeps business owners up at night. Inventory means cash problems, and cash problems mean constant cost-cutting. This is what attracts DTC and e-commerce customers to Oceans, unlike software startups that just raised massive funding and treat these expenses as rounding errors.

Nik shared his experience hiring from Oceans for Sharma Brands, where he had eight or nine people. He appreciated the cost efficiency—spending money on team outings in places like Colombo would never exceed one hundred eighty dollars total because prices were so low. He noted that the Oceans team loved hanging out together, which challenged his assumption that remote hiring makes culture difficult to build and scale internationally. Nik realized that with the right approach, it's entirely possible to create culture with people hired internationally.

Ian explained that Oceans has boots on the ground in their hiring locations, actively cultivating culture and hosting events and educational things. Building culture overseas requires the same fundamentals as building remote culture domestically, which itself is harder than building international remote culture. The key components include visibility, solid communication plans, synchronization systems like Slack and Asana, and regular check-ins because context gets missed when people aren't sitting next to each other.

Culture has become increasingly multinational, making international hiring easier than ever. In 2011, studying abroad meant culture shock because people couldn't discuss the same shows or products. Now, people across countries watch the same Netflix shows, see the same TikToks, and consume similar media. Ian and Nik both watch the same comedy show every week, and that shared cultural touchpoint becomes a bridge for connection.

Nik explained his approach to onboarding offshore talent. He views it as similar to feeding information into an empty ChatGPT instance. The person needs massive amounts of information about the process, how you think about something, and why you think about it that way. He emphasized screen sharing, FaceTime calls, and observation. The difference between success and failure comes down to whether the offshore person can simply do the work or can own the work. These are two very different things, and people often confuse them when hiring abroad.

Ian agreed this is the differentiator between talented staff and mediocre staff. Some people can execute work but can't take ownership. Others seem like they should own work but can only execute. Sussing out this distinction is hard, which is why many people cycle through numerous offshore hires before finding someone who truly owns their work. Nik didn't experience true ownership until he hired Ramiz, who came in with the mindset of wanting to be an owner in the organization.

Oceans runs a whole department called Integration Success designed to work with any client for the first one hundred days, setting up communication platforms, cadences, and thirty-sixty-ninety-day plans. This intensive onboarding process is difficult to do independently, but it's possible if someone is open with their life, schedule, and calendar. Building trust is essential, and the more someone shares, the more quickly the offshore hire can become a second brain.

AI came up as an unavoidable component of modern work. Oceans has a month-long training program where an entire week is dedicated to AI. The US remains at the forefront of AI innovation, with people in America using it more creatively and understanding prompt mechanics better than people anywhere else. English being the interface language for most AI tools creates a barrier for non-English speakers. This is why Oceans includes AI training before matching clients with talent.

The synergy between offshore talent and AI is remarkable. What was already a great deal becomes incredible when properly paired. Nik, Ramiz, and Manu screen-share in Claude to find cool workflows and show each other tools they discover. This simple collaboration multiplies the person's effectiveness regardless of where they live.

A critical hiring mistake Americans make involves overweighting spoken English ability. Ramiz speaks excellent English, but this isn't always necessary for successful offshore hires. English is taught worldwide but primarily through written tests and reading, not spoken interaction. Many populations have strong reading and writing abilities but weaker speaking skills. Unless the role is client-facing, perfect spoken English shouldn't be the primary criterion. A written component in the hiring evaluation is much more important than spoken fluency.

The inverse problem also exists: people can be excellent at speaking English and have great conversations while producing terrible work. Americans often make the mistake of assuming that someone who speaks English fluently and is fun to talk to must be a genius. This is completely untrue. The quality of spoken English has little bearing on work product.

Writing comprehension and written ability are the top indicators of success in a role. When building aptitude tests, including both short-form answers and long-form written responses reveals a lot. Short sentences are often rushed and full of errors, while multi-paragraph responses are rewritten, edited multiple times, and checked using grammar tools. Someone might produce a flawless college-level paragraph while having grammatical errors in a simple sentence, which indicates something suspicious is happening.

Live screen sharing during the hiring process is invaluable. A prepared activity conducted in real-time cannot be faked. While this approach might seem like Orwellian surveillance, the reality is that in modern times, faking work has never been easier. It's reasonable for employers to ask people to prove they're the ones doing the work rather than relying on tools.

Personality tests are often recommended but aren't particularly useful for hiring small numbers of people. Most personality assessments assume a native English speaker, making them problematic for evaluating non-native English speakers who are nonetheless fluent enough to do their jobs effectively. These tests can produce false signals that mislead hiring decisions.

The most important hiring step people overlook is treating candidates like real people by asking about their career goals and what motivates them. This matters enormously if you're making a permanent hire rather than a short two-week task on Fiverr. Understanding what someone wants from their career helps ensure they'll stay. If an offshore hire is going to be let go immediately after a project ends, you'll never attract top-tier talent. People want stability because they have families, marriages, college debt, and real lives.

Oceans maintains its people on the bench for a few months between clients without missing paychecks, ensuring stability and security. This structure attracts higher-skill workers because it removes the fear of sudden unemployment. Compared to US employment standards, structuring offshore offers with security produces much better results.

Nik emphasized that competitors are already using offshore talent effectively, and offshore hires like Ramiz and Manu outperform talent globally. Not leveraging offshore hiring as a talent multiplication strategy is ignorant and represents a missed golden opportunity. If you're not using offshore talent to multiply what your US-based talent can accomplish, you're leaving value on the table.

The path to success in offshore hiring starts with deciding to do it. Step two involves figuring out how to get the highest-skill folks in that geography rather than cycling through fifteen people who don't meet expectations. This requires understanding the companies operating there, knowing which universities produce quality graduates, and creating incredible support systems for hired talent. The goal should be finding the top ten percent of available talent in any given geography and setting them up for success and retention.
