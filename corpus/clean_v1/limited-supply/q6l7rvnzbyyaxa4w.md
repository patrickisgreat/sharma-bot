# Limited Supply: Product Launches for Maximum Impact

I love getting text messages from brands announcing new product launches. They'll say something like, "Hey, we have this new product that launches on this day. Click this link to be the first to know when it launches," and that gets me excited because I want to support them, but also I love their products. I know they sell out within a couple of hours of going live, so I want to make sure I'm in there. You want your audience to understand that this product sells and that your brand always sells out of their products as soon as they go live. It gets a lot of people excited about the wait list and gets you to play a drop strategy. This is something we've done a lot with Skinny Confidential where when we launched Mouth Tape, we had basically pent-up demand with a bunch of people really excited for the drop. It sold out within less than an hour. Then the next time we restocked, we already had that list ready to go, dropped it, and sold out again.

One thing that's really apparent and a common problem for multiple brands is this: so many brands have a phenomenal product and their performance media and ad creative is dialed in, but they don't do anything to get brand awareness out there. I like to think about it this way: you've got brand awareness as pixie dust that gets sprayed all over the floor. Someone's just putting it all over the floor—that's brand awareness, brand recognition, brand excitement, brand affinity. Everything to do with the brand basically: your logo, your name, what it means to people, how people feel when they see it.

Our job as performance marketers is to take that awareness and engagement and turn that into customers. As a media buyer, director of performance, or leader on the DTC side, you're probably thinking, "I'm doing everything I can to drive ads effectively. Why am I still not hitting the CPA target?" People clearly say in the reviews that they love the product, but how come we're not able to get low CPMs and good CPAs? The biggest reason is most likely that your brand awareness is lacking. Your brand team isn't doing enough on social, isn't doing enough to be out there physically in real life, isn't interacting with customers enough, isn't putting themselves out with creators, isn't getting other people to talk about you.

The root of this problem is just a lack of excitement around building brand awareness. Yes, there are brands you don't need to build brand awareness for—you can just run Facebook ads. I know of them and we've worked on some of them. But for the majority of brands, that's just not the case. Most people aren't sophisticated enough as performance marketers to do that. The best combination is to pair performance marketing with some sort of brand awareness, whether that's working with a creator or multiple creators. Look at Jolly—they pump out tens of thousands of pieces of content with content creators on an annual basis. Look at Saloon, which is like Mexican Liquid IV—they pump out so much content with their content creators and with Joelly himself as a content creator and co-founder. The biggest thing that holds people back is they just don't focus enough on excitement for the brand. People should see your logo or brand name and feel some type of way. That is the only way your ads work. If people see your logo and brand and they don't feel some type of way, and you're wondering why your ads aren't working, well, that's the answer.

This brings me to what I want to talk about today: product launches and how to maximize them for awareness and driving revenue. I'm excited about this because it's actually inspired by brands like Lemme, Courtney Kardashian's supplement brand. They treat every single product launch like it's a huge brand launch. That's what I want to talk through today—how to create the perfect product launch. It essentially spans across a few things: your site, your ad creative, your email and SMS, and your organic social, seeding, and creators. My hope is that you can listen to this episode, take notes, and implement these fixes into your next product launch.

Let's talk about the website first. Think of this as your retail store. If you had a store in Soho or Rodeo Drive or South Congress in Austin, you would make such a big deal out of the fact that you have a new product launch. You would change the windows, maybe change the stickering or design on the doors. You'd make call outs as soon as people walk in. You'd have a different layout for the first ten feet. You really want to show off that product.

So what does that mean when people go to your site? First, you want to update your above-the-fold site experience or your site banner to show the new product. A good site banner or site hero doesn't just show the product—it also shows benefits. Chunky text doesn't work, and paragraphs don't work. Stick to bullet points and headlines that talk through benefits. Everything should be benefit-driven, not chunks of text that focus on value props.

Also make sure you have some social proof. If you don't have a number of reviews, you can put maybe a number of units sold across the brand. If you don't want to do that, maybe it's a quote from an early reviewer of your product, designed into the hero itself. Maybe it's that there's some new technology you want to address. Figure out how to make that hero pop. You don't just want a picture of the product. Ideally you have the product in use. You might have a small editorial corner showing a still image of the product so that the customer can see it, understand it, and get the punch line without scrolling past the hero.

On your collections page, the new product should be the first product that shows up. It should be the center of attention. Some brands do this thing in the collections page where every two to three rows they reserve one product card to be almost like an ad unit where they can put an image they upload or a small video that auto-plays. You can use that spot to reinforce the new product and its messaging. But definitely make sure the new product is first in your collection.

In your slide-out cart and Shopify checkout extensions, prioritize that. If this product is not in the cart as part of your checkout, it should be the first product featured there. Let's say David had already launched and we just launched the new cake batter protein bar. I would make sure that the cake batter protein bar is the first on the collections page and that if someone ordered the chocolate chip protein bar, the cake batter is the first suggested upsell in the cart as well as during checkout.

Next, use your on-site pop-up. We've talked about using the on-site pop-up to explain when you have a promo or you're running a site-wide sale, and that's where you'd put your email and phone number. I would do the same thing here. If you're launching a new product, use that on-site pop-up to include messaging around the new product and whether you're offering a discount. Remember, it's not just ad traffic coming there—there's also direct traffic. That's really what you're trying to capitalize on. I would probably do the pop-up for like 48 to 72 hours and then switch it back to whatever converts best. Obviously after 24 hours if you see a huge drop in signups or conversion rate, switch it back. We've seen that sometimes when you edit the email module in the pop-up, you can see higher than average collection rates.

Definitely build additional modules for the product detail page. If you're launching a new product, it may be part of the same types of products you've launched. Let's say you're a milk company—it may be another variation of milk. Or it might be that you're launching yogurt, and if your site PDPs are built to sell milk, you might need to add extra modules. You want to explain what it does, how it works, what comes in the box, how to use the product, how to prepare or set it up, any common frequently asked questions. Really think of this as your landing page. Make sure you thoroughly explain everything.

The last thing with the website is if you decide to run some sort of a pre-launch sale for your best customers, definitely direct that traffic to a dedicated landing page where you can speak to those customers in a slightly different manner. These are people who understand your brand. You don't need to teach them about the brand or why they should buy or why they should trust you. You really just need to focus on selling the product. That's why you should have a separate landing page focused on that—it's lower funnel. Your PDP should flex up and down the funnel. If somebody's ready to buy, your PDP should work. If somebody has no idea who you are and needs to build that trust and understand who you are and compare you to other options, your PDP should do that too. Your PDP should be your catch-all landing page. But if you run a pre-launch sale for your best customers, subscribers, or VIPs, definitely separate that traffic. You can then judge what is your conversion rate of traffic that's already a customer, and that helps with future projections.

Now let's talk about ad campaigns. For new products, you want to make sure you have ad creative ready to go and you want to set it for three kinds of audiences. First is prospecting—think ads that go out to people who've maybe never heard of this product or your brand before. Second is retargeting—people who haven't purchased but know who you are, engaged with you through your email list, website, or social. Third is remarketing—people who have made at least one purchase. These are your most likely to buy people again.

With product launches, you want to make sure that the ads specifically mention the new product or that it is a new product. Show the product in use and directly address all the benefits. You really just have to earn the click to the site. Let your product page or landing page do the selling.

As you're creating this paid media funnel around the new product, focus on five main things. One is the angle—what's going to get your foot in the door and allow you to say what you're trying to say? Second is your audience targeting—prospecting, retargeting, or remarketing. Third is the creative—whether it's a static ad, animation, video, or you as the founder recording on your iPhone with subtitles. Fourth is the experience—where are you driving them on the site and what information are they getting? Fifth is the offer—how are you going to make this fiscally compelling to your prospect?

Sometimes with new products you just have to get crafty. That might mean testing new audiences through interest-based groups or different variations of lookalike audiences. Also retest some of your creative thinking. If you know that, for example, UGC has historically not performed well, retest it with a new product. Or if listicle style landing pages haven't performed well, retest that. In fact, if you find that education is the barrier for people to buy the product, definitely explore listicles and ad tutorials. You can have your creative just focus on earning the click, then the listicle or ad tutorial focuses on building intent, and then the product page where they're going to buy does the actual selling.

Now let's talk about email and SMS. These lists are yours—this is first-party data and your first opportunity to generate awareness and revenue with your product launch. My recommendation is that you entice your best customers to purchase early, maybe your top five to ten percent, and then for the rest, send them a teaser. It might be a blurry product image over email or text that gets people excited to sign up for a notification. It could be a review where you're not showing the product. It could be a video of the founder explaining how excited they are about what's launching or showing behind-the-scenes of creating the product.

Lemme does a really good job of this. They show imagery of gummies, capsules, or tinctures but don't exactly tell you what it is for or what purpose it serves. Their overall excitement gets everybody excited, and the excitement people see from other people gets them excited. It's this ripple effect of excitement that drives social engagement and awareness for the product.

Another strategy is sending a text to get an opt-in for a notification. Sniff, a fragrance and candle brand, does this really well. They send messages saying, "Hey, we have this new product that launches on this day. Click this link to be first to know when it launches." That gets people excited because they want to support and they know products sell out within a couple hours. This is a good practice because you want your audience to understand that this product sells and that your brand always sells out. It gets people excited about the wait list and lets you play a drop strategy.

Once the product goes live, send at least an email per day for the first three days. Make sure you exclude past purchasers. You could even exclude past clickers so you're not bugging them. If your best customers or VIP customers have a discount or early bird special, make sure that discount is auto-applied. Don't send an email with a discount code and have them put it in. Tell them the code and even better, include it in the URL with a Shopify redirect so it applies automatically. You want to make sure you're putting all your customers on a red carpet, especially your best ones.

Once the product launches, have a sold-out message ready to go. Something like, "Thanks so much for your support. We completely sold out. Here is where you can put your phone number or email to be notified when we're back in stock." The messaging should be urgent—sign up now, be the first to know, don't miss it again. As soon as that product is back in stock, use email and SMS to notify those people.

Anytime you mention that something sold out before, the conversion rate goes way up. If in your email you mention this sold out before, get it now—I bet your conversion rate and click-through rates are going to be significantly higher.

Now moving on to organic social. Regardless of how small or large your brand is, if you have a new product, the first place most people are going to check is your social channels. People naturally search Instagram or TikTok, which is why you need to think of these platforms as their own funnels. With Instagram, make sure you have the highlights updated. With TikTok, which is becoming a major player in the search engine world, make sure you have product reviews and pinned posts to your profile. You want to think of these as funnels. My standard would be updated highlights and reviews on your profile page.

From a seeding standpoint, if someone were to search for a specific product, there should be at least fifteen to thirty TikTok product reviews already posted by other people. On Instagram, make sure you have enough information. Maybe pin three posts at the top or at least pin the first one with more product detail. That might be a carousel with the product image featured on your grid when pinned, and then all the other posts—Instagram now allows twenty slides—can be a compilation of comparison charts, ingredients, benefits, reviews, and all that.

I'm a huge fan of making sure your organic social is super optimized. The biggest miss I see, even on Black Friday, is when brands have a big sale and don't run it on organic social. Organic social is not really a place to be romantic about your feed or grid anymore. It's really a place to pump out as much content as possible and see what sticks. Put it out, see what sticks, and you can archive stuff that doesn't do well. If you're romantic about what goes on your social channel, you're just missing out on hundreds of thousands of impressions because you're not open to trying stuff.

When you have that new launch, be super loud on organic. Make those three pin posts, add a highlight on the profile, and talk through the product. Unbox it, talk through its benefits, what makes it different, why it's such a big deal. It should really feel like you're walking someone through a QVC-style shopping experience and leave them with really no questions.
