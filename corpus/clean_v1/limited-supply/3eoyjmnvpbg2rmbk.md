# Zero to One: Making Your First Million as a Brand

When you launch a brand, there's often a predictable pattern. Day one is huge—your friends, family, and former colleagues all buy from your store. Conversion rates are high, maybe 7%. You feel euphoric, on top of the world. Then day two hits and everything drops off a cliff. Site visits are down 50%, conversion rates plummet to 2%. By day three, you're down another 50% with a 1% conversion rate. Nobody's buying. The buzz dies. You panic. This is the zero-to-one problem, and it's the hardest part of launching a brand.

The emphasis in entrepreneurship is usually on what comes before launch—the branding, tech stack, site design, product, packaging, unboxing experience—and what comes after you hit significant scale. But the in-between, that critical zero-to-one phase, is rarely discussed and yet it's the hardest challenge to solve. This is also why about 80% of the inquiries and mentor calls I do revolve around this exact question: "I just launched, but sales aren't happening. How do I get this going?"

## The Pre-Launch Test (Optional but Highly Recommended)

Before you even officially launch, you can validate demand and test your concept. This is what brands like Kettle and Fire did exceptionally well. They built a fake landing page for a fake brand, created value propositions and benefits, and ran ads to test what conversion rates they could achieve. The goal wasn't to actually sell—it was to understand if people would give their email and potentially make a purchase.

You can test different headlines, creative styles, and messaging to see what resonates with your target demographic. If you're getting a 30% email opt-in rate, that's solid—roughly one in three people interested. At 50%, you've got something special. I've seen brands hit 80% opt-in rates, and I was confident enough to write them a six-figure check because I believed it would become a billion-dollar business. It turned out exactly that way.

This entire pre-launch testing phase costs around five to ten thousand dollars max. You're using Meta or Google ads, driving to a landing page, and measuring email opt-ins or purchase intent. What you're really validating is whether there's product-market fit before you invest heavily in manufacturing, packaging, and everything else.

## Launch Strategy: It Doesn't Have to Be Big

Many people think launch has to be the biggest moment ever—huge PR push, influencer partnerships, major announcements. You don't necessarily need that. Some of the best brands launch quietly and slowly scale themselves. I talked to a brand this morning doing four million dollars a month that launched eight to ten months ago very quietly. It was a side project they didn't think would work, and now it's a fifty-million-dollar annual revenue business.

That said, launch day is still big and you should maximize the buzz, but you need a plan for day two, week two, and beyond. As soon as you launch, there's a ticking time bomb on inventory sitting in your 3PL, the fees accruing, and the ongoing costs to keep the business running.

## The Ingredient List

To execute zero to one effectively, you need several things:

**A landing page you can modify.** Build it in Shopify so you can easily update imagery, text, and offers without needing a developer. Your landing page should answer six core questions: What is the product? Why does this brand exist? Why will this product better my life? Why is this company the best option on the market? How fast do I get it? Why should I trust this brand?

These six questions should be answered throughout your page, with your hero section hitting at least five of them since most people won't scroll past what's above the fold. Don't assume people know what you know—you've spent two years building this. They haven't. Make it simple. Your mom should understand it. Someone two drinks deep should navigate it without questions. A fifth grader should get it. Assume everyone on the internet needs straightforward, simple language.

**Static Meta ad templates.** Have ten to twenty static ad templates in Figma that you can edit and modify based on learnings. Static ads have about a third of the CPM of video ads, giving you more room to test.

**Advertising budget.** You need fifteen to twenty thousand dollars that you're completely okay potentially losing. This is real money, and you need to be prepared to spend it and potentially not see returns if things don't work out. That said, if you execute properly, this should work.

**A video editor.** You need someone who can iterate on concepts quickly—turning your ideas and content creator footage into ads. If you can't find someone locally, look overseas. Sri Lanka, Philippines, and South America all have amazing talent at much better cost structures.

**Content creators.** Find four to seven reliable people who can create content consistently. Don't just find people to create content once—find people you can work with repeatedly. Set pricing, usage rights, whitelisting rules, and timelines in stone upfront. If you have a new product launch, you want to know for certain that Kevin can deliver assets back within forty-eight to seventy-two hours.

## The Offer

Every good landing page should have an offer. Unless you've built a luxury brand like Louis Vuitton or Jolie that doesn't need discounts, your offer will be critical to converting customers. Some options:

Percentage off (10-20%, sometimes higher for food and beverage). Gift with purchase—something high-margin with high perceived value that also contributes to the product experience. For example, a travel case that costs you two dollars to produce but feels worth thirty. Spend X, get Y dollars off. Buy X, get one free—this is what we used to scale Cadence to eight figures. Cash back rather than percentage off, since the cost to you for the value you're giving is lower. For subscription products, incentivize future orders.

## Review Collection

Everyone knows influencers and media can be bought. Reviews from actual customers are the most powerful social proof. Don't rely on automated review collection—personally send plain text emails from a real person, a few days after delivery, asking customers to leave reviews. For your first hundred orders a day, send these one by one from your own email to maximize deliverability and ensure they hit the important inbox.

Offer incentives for reviews with imagery or video attached. A five to ten dollar Venmo or gift card is worth it for the content you'll get. Be honest about being a new business and how much their review means to you. People respond to authenticity.

## Organic Content Strategy

Before you run ads, test concepts on organic social where distribution is built in—TikTok, Reels, YouTube Shorts, and Twitter. Start by researching the problems your product solves. Google it, check Reddit, look at YouTube comments and TikTok videos. Go deeper than obvious problems to find what really drives people to want your solution.

Most good organic content comes from the angle and the first five seconds, not production value or timing. Write down twenty-five reasons someone would want your product. Turn each reason into a piece of content. Post it organically and see what works. Whatever over-indexes from your baseline, take that as an ad and see how it performs from a sales standpoint.

## Paid Ad Strategy

Start by listing angles. Why should someone buy? What was your reason for creating the product? That's often a great starting point. You can use ChatGPT to help generate angles—just be specific about who your customer is and what problem you're solving.

Create briefs from these angles for your content creators and video editors. Turn angles into static ads. Set a daily budget of four to five hundred dollars on Meta and start testing. Don't go broad on your first purchase—you don't have the pixel history to support it. Start with interests, move to lookalikes as you build your audience, then eventually go broad.

As you drive traffic to your landing page, use a heatmap tool to understand what's actually working. Where are people scrolling? What are they clicking? What are they trying to click that isn't a link? You'll definitely need to make changes. If you have zero changes to make based on heatmap data, you're lying to yourself.

Focus on creating new content and iterating on what you have. Figure out what's driving purchases and purchases. You might find people are adding to cart but not checking out. That's a different problem than people not clicking through from ads. Isolate exactly where the drop-off is—is it at ad level? Landing page scroll? Add to cart? Checkout page? This tells you what to fix.

## Doubling Down

As you find things that work—angles, creative styles, hooks, messaging—keep testing and learning. Most people fail with ads because they don't go through all these steps systematically. They run ads once, lose five grand, never try again. Or they work with an agency, spend twenty-five thousand dollars including fees, and write off paid ads entirely.

Don't forget low-hanging fruit once you're getting customers in the door. Use social proof from reviews in your ads themselves. Duplicate landing pages and test new angles. Optimize email subject lines for higher open rates. Set up SMS collection and abandoned checkout flows. Optimize your email pop-up from the standard three to four percent to twelve to fifteen percent if you're really executing well.

Send customers surveys to understand what they like, what they hate, why they bought, who they bought for, how long they knew about you before purchasing, and the purpose of their purchase. This context informs everything—your content, your marketing, your budget allocation. The more you know, the better decisions you make.

## Upsells, Cross-sells, and Product Expansion

Once you have customers, think about tier-two products and upsells. Native deodorant realized that offering the same product as a thirty percent discount post-purchase had the highest take rate. That increased their average order value by about forty percent without doing anything else. Suddenly their Facebook ads were more efficient, their P&L was more efficient, and they became profitable on the first purchase.

The same applies to pre-purchase upsells and cross-sells. Build tier-two products into your strategy—whether that's merch, gift with purchase, complementary products, or subscription incentives. These are the levers that drive higher AOV.

## The Real Goal: Get to 3K a Day

Making your first million sounds intense because it's a lot of money. But think of it differently: you need to get to three thousand dollars a day in sales. That's your only job. Three thousand a day equals one million dollars a year.

Once you hit three K a day, aim for five K. Once you're at five K, get to ten K. Once you hit ten K a day, a lot of your problems disappear. You've figured out product-market fit. You've proven that this product has a role in society. You can continue scaling.

Each threshold gets easier than the last because you've built systems, proven your angles, understood your customer, and optimized your funnel. But first, get to three K a day. Then five. Then ten. That's how you make your first million.
