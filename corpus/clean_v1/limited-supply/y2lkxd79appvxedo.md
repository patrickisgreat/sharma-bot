# Limited Supply: Imaginary Ventures with Kelly Dill

One of the things Imaginary prides itself on is really the network and relationships. There's always that conversation about what you're looking for over the next ten years, where you want to deploy capital, what you're looking to buy. There are great businesses that might dividend out profits, and there's opportunity there. But the real excitement comes when there's a ton of strategic appetite in a certain category. There are a lot of categories that are going to do a lot of revenue, but there's not a huge buyer landscape for them. That also informs where you invest.

Kelly is an investor at Imaginary Ventures, a consumer-focused fund that invests in all things consumer—brands, products, platforms, and consumer enablement businesses. They work relatively stage-agnostic, doing everything from ideation all the way through growth stage investment, which typically falls in the ten to forty million dollar range. When talking about what they call ideation or new business development, they're careful not to use the term incubation because they don't want to diminish how complicated it is to actually start and found a business.

Imaginary takes a more integral role in ideating and starting businesses, whether that's coming up with an idea, partnering with a founder, or being very involved in hiring an operating team. Every investment or company they invest in is really its own individual organism. They're not hiring one marketing person to run everything across multiple CPG companies or one ops person to figure out one single supply chain. That approach would lose soul. Being brand junkies, they believe that for a company to really have soul, it has to have its own lifeblood. When things become too platformed, they lose soul and become like Excel sheet businesses, just churning things out to turn them out.

The companies they get excited about developing in partnership with operators are the ones that are pretty thesis-driven. When it comes to investing in platforms, it becomes very hard for Imaginary to figure out how to deploy capital within another platform. If you're investing in another incubator, how do you ensure that all your capital goes toward the standout company versus company B and C? Generally, they take positions in specific companies versus other brand incubators. There are a few firms that have done this well, like Night Ventures, which launched Feastables, Matador, and Blocks. It's so hard to do this right, and there are three main things that seem to matter: the talent you can recruit, finding something with good product market fit, and access to eyeballs or distribution.

Gone are the days of relying on Meta and Facebook ads to get your first couple million dollars. You can still create a really great lifestyle business through arbitrage on Meta and Facebook and be profitable. But venture investing is different. The CAC to LTV conversation doesn't excite anyone anymore—the days of pouring ten million dollars into Facebook to grow are probably behind us. You can still do that and create a great business, but Imaginary isn't sure that's venture investing.

They're really brand junkies, and they think it's not enough to have a great product and a great idea. You have to have some competitive advantage in getting in front of millions of people in a way that other people can't. You can build a great business without that, but the question is whether that's a venture business. In venture, it's really about turning one dollar into ten quickly. They understand that great brands are built brick by brick, but you still need some sort of competitive advantage and a go-to-market in today's age.

Imaginary's thesis has always been to invest in generationally defining brands. The goal is to find brands that will be around for the next hundred years—not because they need to be around for that long, but because when L'Oreal or another major company buys this company, that's what they care about. They're not looking for ephemeral brands, but companies that can last a generation. When you keep that in mind, you cut away from brands that are on paper really exceptional companies with all the right metrics, but when you close your eyes and think about whether they'll be around in a hundred years, it puts things in perspective.

Native sold to Procter & Gamble for a hundred million dollars a few years ago and is now doing five or six times that number in revenue alone. No one could have predicted that as a Facebook ads machine, but as Imaginary sees it, you're looking for brands that fit into the next stage of life as well, not just what's going to make you a return immediately. Where is there appetite? Understanding the buyer landscape and what strategic appetite exists is crucial.

There is a revenue sweet spot for acquisition. Most founders overcomplicate where they need to get to. When you get there, they'll come. It's a champagne problem to worry about not wanting to get too big. Most founders aren't running into that problem. The real thing is not to get bigger than the actual balance sheet of the buyers that can buy you. But that's really a champagne problem.

Imaginary has invested in various beauty brands like Kosas, Westman Atelier, and Necessaire, apparel like Skims and Good American, Glossier, and even food businesses like Mila, a dumpling company. When it comes to deciding which incubations to focus on or create, all of them are very much thesis-driven. They see a huge white space or opportunity, a unique go-to-market, and they're partnering with a creator, talent, or somebody with a very authentic story. They see an opportunity that doesn't exist. What they're not trying to do is create brands for the sake of creating brands. It's like they wish that they would see a certain company, and since 2017, they've been waiting to invest in a certain idea. Rather than waiting for that idea to come along, they push the needle forward a little faster on bringing the pieces together.

The most important part of assembling a team is finding people who are founders. There are plenty of people who are super entrepreneurial, but being a founder requires more than that. You need to be a salesperson, but that isn't necessarily an attribute of a great founder. Imaginary searches for people who have passion for a certain category and are excited to build in that category. They don't have to think about needing to go out and do a seed pre-launch fundraise because that's where Imaginary's partnership comes in. The entire role at Imaginary is to support them, not the other way around.

Every founder needs different things. Network is really important. Being a pre-launch early stage founder is very heads down. It's nice for them to have a real thought partner on an intimate basis, someone they can come up for air and ask what else they should be saying, how this compares to other things. Really having a sounding board is helpful. Hiring resources are helpful. Being brand junkies, it's nice to have outside objective points of view on things.

When it comes to pitch decks, it's important to remember that anyone starting a business is doing something courageous. If you're going into an investor meeting, you should be incredibly proud that you've given up whatever you're doing to pursue this idea. Any investor talking to you is lucky that they get to talk to you. It's really hard as an entrepreneur to have the guts to tell someone else your idea.

The fundamental questions every pitch should address are: What are you building and why is it better than everything that already exists? Why can no one else but you do this? Why is the time now? And most importantly, why is it that if Imaginary or anyone gives you five million dollars, is it better than if they invest five million dollars in the hundred other businesses they see? Get ahead of the narrative. If you're starting a business and the first question people are asking is about market size, then on the second page of your pre-launch deck, tell them why their thinking about market size is off. Tell them how profitable it's going to be. Get ahead of every pushback.

You should be able to tell consumers why this is better in about ten seconds, because that's all the time you have. If you're an investor who's consumer-centric, focusing on those really broad key things is the first part. Just get ahead of every question.

If you've already launched a brand and it's in market, don't bury the thing that matters. If you hide your gross margin and traction until the last page or bury it in the appendix, that's already a red flag. Even if it's bad, get ahead of it. Be like, here's where we are. If it's bad, here's where we're going and here's how we're getting there. Tell them why you need five million dollars and why the capital you've raised to date hasn't worked to get you there. Explain the narrative of why the flywheel hasn't hit yet. Tell them why now things are going to change. If you don't control that narrative, the investor will tell the story for you, and their story probably won't be good.

The top things to look for when evaluating a pitch are: First, why does the consumer need this? What exists today and why is this better? Is it a better-for-you version? Is it led with technology? Is there an ingredient story? Is it a hit product? Is it a category and brand that people don't have yet? What's the reason for this to exist?

Second is everything around unit economics. Third is why are you the right person to do this? Are you exceptional at product? Exceptional at team building? Do you have a unique supply chain connection that nobody else has? If it's a great idea, you want to make sure the founder is the right person to execute it.

For launched stage businesses, understand what retail they're in and why they have a competitive advantage in that retail. There's a big difference between a launched brand and a pre-launch brand, and those pitch decks look very different.

On the SaaS and enablement side, Imaginary definitely has investments they love, including Black Crow. The way they diligence those opportunities is by sending them to their brands and asking if they would use this, why they like it, and if it's top of mind. There are so many tools today, and it says a lot when your brands are really excited about something versus saying it's interesting but they'll get back to it in six months. The best way to think about SaaS investments is how excited your brands get about them.

If a SaaS tool doesn't solve a top two founder problem, it's never going to work. Does it drive revenue immediately? Does it increase profit immediately? If it's not doing one of those two things, it's likely not a priority for most founders right now. Investors are asking if founders are doing more with less, being more profitable while growing faster. If a tool doesn't help them do one of those things, especially if it takes a year to integrate, it's hard to become a priority.

On the exit environment, over engineering an exit early on is probably not a priority for a founder. Great businesses get bought or have an exit. As a founder, focus on creating incredible fundamentals, growing, and being super profitable. There will be an opportunity to exit the business or take some secondary off the table. The days of early founder secondary from the 2020 era are gone. If you're a great business, you can do what you want. A great business would be one that's growing a hundred percent and is super profitable—a hundred million plus in revenue, spinning off thirty percent EBITDA. What are you going to do with extra twenty million dollars?

Imaginary is excited about consumer investing and is always opportunistic. They spend a lot of time in categories like better-for-you food and beauty. There are key areas where they see exciting changes, like the rise in GLP-ones and what knock-on effects that has in beauty, protein, and snacks. How can they better serve middle America? How can they focus on the mass consumer?

This year, one major recognition is that a lot of e-commerce brands that used to do well primarily on performance marketing can't performance market their way out of building a true brand. There's too much competition—Temu, sellers on Amazon from around the world. If you're not building a real brand, you have no moat and could be gone tomorrow because somebody else might undercut you by a dollar and sell the same product. The investment into building a brand is what gets businesses through Q4, versus brands that didn't invest in brand building and are having a really hard time acquiring customers and retaining market share.

The excitement comes from real brand versus performance arbitrage. It's not that you shouldn't have great unit economics and efficient marketing—it's all working together. Making the flywheel of TikTok, Meta, retail, and distribution all work in your favor is the unlock. On the seed stage side, it's about founders who have a unique point of view and want to solve a problem. That's the core of why a brand starts, and then everything else can work in your favor. Without that, Facebook and performance marketing might work, but it's hard to build a generationally defining brand.

Anytime you build a website or landing page, you should answer throughout the site how this is better than anything else on the market. If you can't answer that, you have no shot at converting people. Why even launch? Why build if it's not better?

There will always be opportunities to create and ideate and innovate. Twenty years ago, people asked why you needed another beauty brand when Estee Lauder existed. There's still so much opportunity left. Some of the technology that founders are taking advantage of and some of the innovation is exceptional.
