## The Wink Bankruptcy and DTC Challenges

Right now we're seeing more businesses struggling and declaring bankruptcy. This is particularly happening because companies that fundraised twelve to eighteen months ago are facing a much tougher market with lower valuations. Facebook ads may have improved, but they can't save a fundamentally broken business. Many companies are either trying one last push during the holiday season to generate liquidity for excess inventory, or they're deciding to shut down before 2023 starts.

Wink, a wine company, recently declared bankruptcy. I had talked to them before about customer acquisition. The story of Wink illustrates larger issues in DTC from the fundraising era.

Wink started as Club W, a wine subscription business that went through Y Combinator around 2010-2011, back when YC still funded e-commerce companies. They later pivoted and rebranded as Wink, expanding into direct-to-consumer wine sales. They went public in November 2021 but had already raised $54 million before that.

Throughout their history, they borrowed money at extremely high interest rates. In December 2017, they borrowed $5 million at 6.25% plus prime, with a minimum interest rate of 11.5%—meaning they paid approximately $550,000 annually just in interest. When they went public in November 2021, they raised $18 million, which they used partly to pay back that expensive loan.

In May 2021, just before going public, they acquired a wine importing business for $13 million—$8 million in cash upfront plus stock and an earn-out of $1.6 million. However, they later restructured the earn-out because they didn't have the cash, committing to pay $1.6 million over nine months starting in September 2022. Now that they're bankrupt, that seller won't receive the remaining payments.

This trajectory is shocking—just eighteen months before bankruptcy, they were so bullish they spent $8 million in cash acquiring another business. The stock is now down 94% over the past year and trades at a $5 million market cap.

## What Went Wrong

When you raise at ridiculous valuations during a frothy market, it becomes an ego thing—how high can I get my valuation compared to other companies in the category? While it's cool initially, generating TechCrunch articles and excitement, the reality hits later when you can't hit revenue targets or your churn is higher than expected.

Many companies raise massive amounts of capital to fund customer acquisition at unrealistic costs, hoping the LTV to CAC ratio eventually works out. However, this is building a business in Excel that doesn't account for human emotion and psychology. A CFO might project unrealistic session growth and cut marketing budgets accordingly, but that doesn't reflect how people actually behave.

Being public also carries significant costs. Wink needs D&O (directors and officers) insurance, which costs millions annually. They also have Sarbanes-Oxley compliance requirements following the Enron scandal, which require elevated accounting standards. These costs can range from $1 to $10 million annually.

## Wink's Business Metrics

Despite these problems, Wink's underlying business looks decent: north of $50 million in annual revenue with 40% gross margins. They spend only about 15% of revenue on marketing, which is surprisingly low—most businesses spend 25-40%.

Their business mix is 65% direct-to-consumer with a $90 AOV and 35% retail. They use creative tactics like subscription credits instead of shipping automatic boxes monthly, which reduces email volume since nothing ships unless customers select purchases. They also leverage gift cards heavily, with gift cards prominently featured as the second clickable item on their site.

Gift cards are brilliant because they provide upfront cash flow, guarantee future revenue, and benefit from breakage—the percentage of gift cards never redeemed. However, from an accounting perspective, public companies can't record gift card revenue when sold; they must wait until redemption. If 10% of gift cards are never used, you can only record that 10% as profit, not the full amount.

Wink spends 50% of revenue on headcount and general and administrative expenses—over $25 million annually. Combined with the costs of being public, this creates significant burn.

## Gift Cards as a Strategy

Sarah from Curie ran a Black Friday gift card promotion that outperformed traditional discounts. Instead of percentage-off sales, she offered gift cards with purchase: $5 for orders over $30, $10 for over $50, and $20 for over $80. This approach acquired more new customers than a typical Black Friday sale, with 70% repeat customers versus 28% for Black Friday. Her AOV was also 18% higher with gift cards.

This works because customers perceive gift cards differently than discounts—they feel like they got something free on top of a purchase rather than a percentage reduction. It keeps margins in-house compared to percentage-off promotions, and it guarantees secondary purchases.

Naked Wines uses gift cards extensively, putting $100 vouchers in customers' boxes. They pay other brands to include their vouchers in shipments, paying around $50 CPM based on the assumption that a small percentage will redeem for $200+ orders.

The hard part is accounting treatment. You can't declare revenue when you sell a gift card; you must wait until redemption. If a customer buys a $100 gift card and never redeems it, you can only record that as revenue when you've determined it won't be used—which requires proper accounting reserves.

## Cyber Monday vs. Black Friday Performance

I found something surprising this year: several brands performed better on Cyber Monday than Black Friday, with some doing 120% of their Black Friday sales on Cyber Monday alone. This contradicts what most people are experiencing.

One brand had a 4.9% conversion rate on Black Friday and 5.5% on Cyber Monday. I think this reflects that people spend time with family on Friday and are back working Monday evenings, when that critical 5-6pm email performs best.

Brands that succeeded with multiple holidays used different messaging. Black Friday focused on "the deal" while Cyber Week emphasized stocking up on gifts or finding stocking stuffers for Christmas. This messaging shift worked even with repeat customers from Black Friday.

The question of whether to exclude Black Friday purchasers from Cyber Monday offers is complex. If you have many SKUs, you can discount different products at different times. With fewer SKUs like Native had, it's extremely difficult. We'd try to exclude customers but many provide multiple email addresses—one at signup and one at checkout—so they'd see both offers anyway.

## The Native Sale Story

When we were selling Native a few years ago, our lead investor Paul Ferris from Azure Capital gave me memorable advice. He told me not to sell for less than $100 million and that I could reach that valuation. Six months later, when we had an offer for exactly $100 million, we were far along in the process with P&G with documents nearly ready to sign.

Paul told me I should call P&G and ask for $125 million instead. He explained that I wanted to sell because it would change my life, but P&G wanted to buy it even more. They had more resources, more incentive, and more "fibers." My father thought Paul was crazy. My brother said Paul had the biggest balls he'd ever seen—they weren't testicles, but cannonballs.

I didn't have the courage to renegotiate at that moment. Looking back, I don't regret the sale because I'm happy with my life, but I think about that advice often. Paul was absolutely right about the business being on fire. With the knowledge and resources I have now, I would have pushed for $150 million.

My mother initially sided with my father—sign the papers and don't risk it. But afterward, she admitted Paul was right. She felt bad that I hadn't maximized value.

This is a good lesson about leverage in negotiations. When someone wants something more than you want to sell it, you have more power than you realize.
