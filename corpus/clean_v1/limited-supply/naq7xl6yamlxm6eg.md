# Limited Supply: Season 6, Episode 2

When WeWork was everywhere, everyone thought it was the greatest business in the world. You're renting out tiny cubicles for a fortune. One of my friends is a real estate lawyer and she represented a building where they were trying to lease a floor to WeWork. The building owners said, "Here's our standard lease," and WeWork said, "No, we give you our standard lease. You don't give us your standard lease. We're WeWork, we don't give a shit who you are." Now WeWork is also bankrupt.

SmileDirectClub experienced similar hubris. The company went from twenty million in revenue to seven hundred fifty million in revenue in just three years. When you're growing that fast, you think you're crazy like a fox.

SmileDirectClub started in about 2016 and raised twenty million in its initial round. The guys who created Invisalign, the trademark brand for aligners, owned twenty percent of SmileDirectClub and also invested in it separately. In total, SmileDirectClub would raise one point six billion in equity and a billion dollars in debt—two and a half billion dollars total. By 2019, they were doing seven hundred fifty million in revenue and went public at a nine billion dollar valuation.

Ninety percent of their sales in their peak year came from their physical Smile shops, not from their direct-to-consumer website. People would go into their facility and the company would take five thousand photos per second to get a really good mold of someone's mouth. The average order value was two to three thousand dollars.

The relationship between SmileDirectClub and Invisalign was contentious from the start. There were lawsuits between the two. Invisalign tried to compete with SmileDirectClub by launching their own telehealth version, but SmileDirectClub sued and won, forcing Invisalign to essentially stop that initiative.

SmileDirectClub did something unusual with unhappy customers. If someone wanted a refund because they had a bad experience, SmileDirectClub would give them one, but only if they signed an NDA saying they wouldn't say anything bad about the company. It's almost like a severance agreement.

When COVID happened, their business declined significantly. They went from seven hundred fifty million in 2019 to four hundred seventy million in 2022—a thirty-five to forty percent decline. The reason was straightforward: their Smile shops closed. These physical locations were crucial to their business, but nobody wanted to sit in a dentist chair during a pandemic, open their mouth, and have someone stick something in to take photos. That's the recipe for getting COVID.

The company had signed long-term leases on these shop locations when they were doing peak business in 2019. When COVID hit, they couldn't use the spaces but still had to pay rent on them.

In 2021, SmileDirectClub raised seven hundred fifty million in debt with a fifteen percent interest rate due in 2026. They also had a one hundred fifty million dollar credit facility at fifteen percent interest due in two years. The CEO and founders loaned the business five million dollars.

Then in 2023, SmileDirectClub lost a litigation case against Invisalign and had to pay a sixty-three million dollar judgment. That had to be paid right away.

Now SmileDirectClub had nine hundred million in debt with that fifteen percent interest rate. That meant every year they had to come up with one hundred fifty million just to pay interest, not to pay down the principal. This was unsustainable.

In 2023, they tried to raise more money. They talked to sixty-one lenders—equity providers, people who could give them cash. Twenty-five of those people signed an NDA, but only one of them came back with a proposal, and SmileDirectClub determined it wasn't enough to save the business. So they filed for bankruptcy.

Looking at the bankruptcy documents, SmileDirectClub owed six million to law firms, forty-five thousand to the PR firm 5W Public Relations, one hundred seventy thousand to Billion Dollar Boy (an influencer marketing agency), one point eight million to Salesforce, one million to Horizon Media, eighteen thousand to Bizarre Voice, four point one million to Google, and only one thousand to Facebook. That Facebook number is interesting because it probably means they just stopped advertising there once they realized their cost per lead had become prohibitively expensive.

The real challenge with businesses like SmileDirectClub is the customer acquisition cost. With an AOV of two to three thousand dollars, your acquisition cost isn't going to be fifty dollars. When your cost per lead is eighty dollars and your cost per conversion is fifteen hundred dollars, you have to spend hundreds of thousands of dollars just to test whether an ad is working or not. Compare that to selling deodorant where you can spend fifty dollars and figure out if something's working.

This creates a huge moat for companies with capital, but it also boxes out other competitors who can't afford to test their funnel. If you're starting the business and you don't already have a marketer who's worked in the category, your hypothesis for creative costs maybe twenty thousand to develop. Then you run ads and spend a hundred thousand and get nothing. Then you develop another creative for twenty thousand, run it again, and continue that cycle of burning cash.

The acquisition cost problem is partly why companies like Hims and Roman survived this period while SmileDirectClub didn't. Hims and Roman didn't just expand one thing they saw exploding. Instead, they went out and found twenty-five other products, knowing that twelve might fail but thirteen would keep their business growing.

One thing that SmileDirectClub didn't do was go into retail distribution fast enough. When Native sold to P&G, there were options for how the acquisition would work. One option was a complete absorption where the business moves into the parent company's headquarters. Another option was partial integration with shadowing and oversight. SmileDirectClub chose to remain almost entirely independent. Distribution still came out of LA, marketing still came out of LA, product innovation still came out of LA. They didn't take advantage of Unilever's system of distribution and retail relationships.

This independence meant they never got the benefit that Native got when going into Target. With P&G backing Native, Target understood the relationship and the terms better. They could see the numbers of other deodorant brands at Target and understood the margins. SmileDirectClub remained blind to this information for a long time and didn't touch brick-and-mortar stores fast enough.

SmileDirectClub also never came up with another product that worked well. They were defined by their positioning around cheap razors and cheap aligners. If you position yourself as the cheap option, that becomes your identity. With Native, the conscious decision was made not to compete on price. The thinking was that if you're only competing on price, saying you're cheaper than Secret or Old Spice, you're not going to make much money at twelve dollars or whatever price point you choose.

Now SmileDirectClub has been sold and many of the early direct-to-consumer pioneers have moved on. Dollar Shave Club was sold by Unilever to Nexus Capital, a private equity firm spun out of Apollo. Nexus bought sixty-five percent of the business while Unilever kept thirty-five percent ownership. Nexus typically invests between fifty million and three hundred million per transaction, though the specific terms weren't disclosed for this deal.

The Dollar Shave Club sale was interesting because it felt like an admission that Unilever couldn't figure out how to grow the business themselves. When Direct-to-Consumer companies get acquired by large CPG companies, there are different integration models. With Dollar Shave Club, Unilever kept it almost completely independent initially. This meant Dollar Shave Club didn't take advantage of Unilever's massive distribution system the way it could have.

Dollar Shave Club also built its brand on the positioning of being cheap—"You don't need Roger Federer"—and that became limiting. Once you own the cheap position in a category, it's hard to expand beyond that. It's hard to launch adjacent products if they're expected to also be cheap.

There's also been interesting moves among other direct-to-consumer brands. Roan recently bought out their investor Al Catterton, which is unusual. Typically when things are going well, investors don't push for exits. But apparently Roan's founder wanted to buy back the equity to retain more control of the business.

Meanwhile, Alo Yoga is reportedly trying to raise money at a ten billion dollar valuation. It's interesting that Alo and Roan are going in completely different directions. One is raising money aggressively while the other is buying back investors. Alo apparently went from two hundred million in revenue in 2020 to over a billion last year, though it's hard to verify these numbers for private companies.

It's remarkable how little public information there is about revenue for major direct-to-consumer brands. You don't know Athletic Greens' revenue. You don't know Alo's revenue. You don't know Viore's revenue. People can spend all day thinking about this industry and still not know these basic numbers.

Alo hasn't made acquisitions to grow despite having such a big platform and great retail stores. They launched a skincare business, but it doesn't seem to be doing that well. It would make sense that Alo would acquire complementary brands and put them in front of their massive customer base, but they've been cautious about brand fit.

Lululemon's acquisition of Mirror for five hundred fifty million is a cautionary tale. It's remarkable that such large businesses can make such large mistakes. You'd think that with enough people in the room, someone would ask if it's really a good idea to spend five hundred fifty million on something. There's a concept from World War Z where a group of men in Israel has the specific job of disagreeing with whatever the majority believes, playing devil's advocate no matter what. That kind of thinking seems absent from some acquisition decisions.

The first half of 2024 is likely to be strong for e-commerce brands because all the expensive freight purchased in 2021 is running off now. Inventory added in 2021 and 2022 is clearing out. However, there are concerns about the second half of 2024 with the presidential election. In 2016, when Trump was elected, sales fell off a cliff for a week after the election. Everyone was affected. Growth still happened, but November 2016 wasn't easy. The first half of 2024 will probably be good for advertising as people pay attention to politics and news, but the second half could see consumer pullback due to election anxiety and uncertainty.

Student debt repayment has started again and interest rates on mortgages are at eight percent, which affects consumer spending. There's a sense that the economy is not great, and that could impact growth for consumer brands. Leading up to the election, advertising should be great because consumption of news and social media usage will be high. But after the election, social media becomes a place of grievance and the business cycle becomes less predictable.
