# Limited Supply Podcast - Season 4 Episode 1

The conversation opens with a discussion about creator brands and what makes them successful. The hosts emphasize that consumers today are sophisticated and can immediately tell whether a brand is authentic or simply has a celebrity slapped on it. They use the example of comparing two beauty brands launched with major celebrities—one that thrived and one that underperformed—demonstrating that the difference often comes down to authenticity and genuine involvement from the celebrity.

The hosts dive into Rob's Backstage Popcorn, a brand launched by the Jonas Brothers that recently raised $7 million and has secured placement in 8,000 stores nationwide. The brand cleverly leverages celebrity collaborations, including work with Kelly Clarkson and Priyanka Chopra. This strategy exemplifies a formula that works exceptionally well: massive top-of-funnel awareness from celebrity status combined with mass distribution through retailers like Walmart, Target, or GoPuff. The hosts discuss how Chris Paul's popcorn brand with GoPuff follows a similar playbook.

They trace the history of this strategy back to Brian Lee, who pioneered this approach with brands like Honest Company, Shoe Dazzle, and LegalZoom in partnerships with celebrities like Jessica Alba and Kim Kardashian. The hosts debate whether Lee was simply ahead of his time, noting that while his brands were successful, today's celebrity-backed brands have even more advantages due to the power of social media platforms like Instagram and YouTube that barely existed when those early ventures launched.

The conversation shifts to Rare Beauty, Selena Gomez's beauty brand. Unlike a simple endorsement deal, Rare Beauty represents Selena's genuine vision for a beauty company that addresses gaps she felt in the industry, particularly around inclusivity and mental health messaging. The hosts discuss several factors that contribute to Rare Beauty's success: Katie Welch, the founding team's CMO, previously worked at Honest Beauty and brought valuable expertise. The brand excels at merchandising strategy, offering minis of products that allow customers to sample and trial at low price points and low friction. This is only possible because the massive celebrity platform means customer acquisition costs are virtually zero.

The packaging and product names incorporate positive word associations that resonate with customers. Rare Beauty has generated over seven billion views on TikTok and maintains an accessible, inclusive brand image embodied by the phrase "come sit with us" rather than creating exclusionary cool-kid vibes. The brand engages directly with fans, responding to posts and reposting customer content. When compared to competitors like Glossier, which had 454 employees to generate $75 million in revenue, Rare Beauty achieved $60 million in revenue with only 80 employees, demonstrating exceptional operational efficiency.

The hosts examine Sephora's exclusive distribution of Rare Beauty and how this partnership leverages Sephora's network of influencers and ambassadors. They emphasize that the combination of massive celebrity influence and strategic retail distribution through major chains appears essential for success in this space.

A critical insight emerges about what separates successful celebrity brands from failures: the level of authentic content creation and involvement from the celebrity beyond traditional photo shoots and PR. They compare Rare Beauty's success to Give Beauty, a brand launched with Gwen Stefani that underperformed despite having comparable celebrity status and distribution channels. The difference came down to authenticity and the amount of genuine content the celebrity produced.

The hosts share an anecdote about Jeff Curl, former CEO of Stance Socks, who encountered two very different celebrity responses when seeking partnerships. One basketball player immediately asked about compensation and appearance requirements—a transactional approach. Dwayne Wade, conversely, discussed his genuine passion for quality socks and how they served his actual athletic needs. This authentic enthusiasm, regardless of financial terms, indicated Wade would be a better brand partner. The lesson is that celebrities who genuinely care about the product and brand will be more effective advocates than those viewing it purely as a transaction.

They discuss Shea Moisture and Onda tequila as examples of brands that successfully integrated their celebrity founders into the business operations, including having them on buyer calls and deeply involved in decision-making. Ryan Reynolds' involvement with MintMobile is cited as an exemplary model of a celebrity becoming truly integrated with a brand rather than simply being a face on the packaging.

The hosts reflect on challenges faced by mini-celebrity influencer founders like Mimi Ikonn of Luxie Hair, who built an eight-figure business generating seven figures in EBITDA through organic YouTube growth but faced challenges when trying to sell because potential buyers worried about the brand's dependence on the founder's personal brand. The solution involved spending the final year as owners systematically removing Mimi from content and branding to prove the business could succeed independently.

They emphasize that successful celebrity brands today are typically designed with exit strategy in mind from the beginning, separating the celebrity's personal brand from the company brand while leveraging their fame for initial growth. The founders of Feastables consciously worked to transition from "Mr. Beast Bars" to a broader product line with a brand identity that could stand on its own.

The conversation shifts to ASAP, a luxury personal care brand. In December 2012, a Brazilian private equity firm called Natura acquired a 65 percent stake in ASAP for $50 million when the company was doing approximately $50 million in annual revenue. This recent acquisition by L'Oreal for $2.5 billion—the largest acquisition in L'Oreal's history—represents a 33X return on that initial investment over the course of a decade. The revenue growth trajectory is remarkable: $210 million in 2018, $375 million in 2020, and $537 million in 2022, representing consistent 20 percent year-over-year growth.

EBITDA has similarly grown, with 2018 showing $32 million on $210 million revenue, 2020 showing $120 million on $375 million revenue (boosted by pandemic soap demand), and 2022 showing $116 million on $537 million revenue, maintaining approximately 22 percent EBITDA margins. The hosts note that this profitability is remarkable for a brand in the consumer goods space.

The hosts discuss the unusual marketing approach of ASAP—they have never seen any traditional ASAP advertising. Instead, the brand has built awareness through strategic placement in high-end restaurants, hotels, and upscale retail locations. This creates an aspirational association where customers view using ASAP products as a status symbol and statement about their taste and values. The beautiful dark-striped bottles have become so iconic that competitors now imitate the design.

ASAP has built a retail presence with approximately 400 stores and beauty counters globally, and in 2020, roughly 30 percent of their sales came directly from ASAP.com, generating approximately $10 million monthly in direct-to-consumer revenue. The hosts highlight an interesting ASAP innovation: virtual consultations with ASAP specialists who recommend products and build customized orders. They question why more skincare and beauty brands haven't adopted this model, noting that adding dermatologist credibility could significantly boost customer confidence and purchase amounts.

The hosts identify five key success factors for ASAP's growth. First, international expansion through strategically located retail stores in upscale areas and high-traffic locations. Second, the store experience itself combines luxury and aspiration with local cultural elements that make the brand feel authentically connected to each market. Third, consistent product innovation creates revenue opportunities as the customer base grows. Fourth, strategic partnerships with brands, artists, and designers produce limited edition products that generate buzz, earned media coverage, and influencer attention at minimal cost compared to traditional advertising. Fifth, exceptional customer service both in-store and online, with knowledgeable associates and accessible virtual consultations.

The hosts contrast ASAP with trendy beauty brands like Drunk Elephant, which was ubiquitous five years ago but has faded, suggesting ASAP is positioned to become a lasting legacy brand like Lancôme or Maybelline rather than a fleeting trend.

The conversation then turns to Stripe's annual letter. Stripe processed $817 billion in transactions last year, up 26 percent from the previous year, significantly outpacing the 7 percent growth rate of e-commerce generally. The letter highlights substantial opportunities for improvement in checkout processes despite e-commerce maturity. Ten percent of payments fail for preventable reasons related to poor checkout design and user experience.

Stripe shares a counterintuitive finding from analyzing billions of data points: allowing customers to type in credit card expiration dates performs better than forcing them to scroll through date wheels. This simple change improves conversion rates, though the hosts acknowledge this seems unintuitive from a pure UX design perspective.

The company discusses "adaptive acceptance," a machine learning approach that attempts to detect when a customer's card has been reissued and charge the new card without requiring manual updates. This is particularly valuable for subscription businesses where card failures can lead to subscription cancellations and customer churn.

Stripe's on-the-fly optimizations increase average revenue by 0.7 percent across their customer base, though the hosts suspect the impact is higher for subscription businesses specifically.

A significant issue emerges: Stripe recently announced they will begin charging for stored payment tokens, a service they previously provided free. Businesses received emails indicating they would be charged approximately $20,000 annually for stored tokens, at a rate of roughly 15 cents per card per month. The hosts characterize this as a bait-and-switch tactic that could increase Stripe's revenue by approximately 10 percent across their customer base. One business indicated Stripe claimed their tokens generated an additional $120,000 in incremental revenue for the business, yet Stripe now demands $20,000 to store them.

The hosts discuss the potential for disruption in this space. With advances in AI tools like ChatGPT, businesses can increasingly build their own applications for reviews, analytics, and subscriptions, potentially making many SaaS providers vulnerable to disruption. Similarly, payment processors charging based on order volume or implementing surprise price increases could face competition from more fairly-priced alternatives.

The hosts then provide practical checkout optimization advice. Small touches like displaying a lock icon and "this checkout is secure" messaging actually improve conversion rates. Ensuring proper keyboard types appear on mobile devices—numeric keypads for credit cards and zip codes rather than full alphabetic keyboards—prevents abandonment. Avoiding asking for the same information twice and using address autocomplete functionality based on zip codes reduces friction.

Email address should be the first field requested, as this allows cart recovery emails if customers abandon checkout. Zip codes should be requested before city and state, allowing systems to autofill remaining address information. The hosts recommend against requiring CVC validation, arguing that while this increases fraud, the conversion rate improvement typically outweighs fraud losses.

Shopify's upcoming single-page checkout is anticipated as a significant improvement. The hosts note that when Native operated on WooCommerce with a single-page checkout versus later moving to Shopify's multi-step checkout, conversion rates declined despite Shopify's dominance and resources. They're eager to test Shopify's new single-page checkout option.
