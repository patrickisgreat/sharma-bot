## Landing Page Structure

The first question is about the best landing page structure working right now. When it comes to landing pages, you really want to answer the same four or five questions repeatedly: What's the product? Why does it exist? How's it going to benefit me? If I order today, when does it get here? And how does it compare to the competition?

I prefer longer landing pages over short ones because if someone's going to find an answer, they'll click and finish their journey. But if they haven't found it, you can keep going. When we were selling Hint Water, we'd put a hero section with social proof first—reviews, testimonials, press logos. Then you get into what makes the product different or why the product exists in the first place. I call this the "why section."

For Hint, we had tons of angles. Most people advertise flavored water by saying it tastes good with zero sugar and zero sweetener. But we approached it differently. We'd ask: are you a mom who wants to give your kid something better than juice with less sugar? Our biggest angle was always against diet soda. People in the refrigerator aren't looking for water—they're deciding between Diet Coke and something else.

The biggest thing most landing pages miss is the angle itself. If you want the full section-by-section breakdown, check out Nick Kostlash's cookbook.

One element I always see missing but that works really well when it's there is a comparison chart. Amazon pages often have these at the bottom. A comparison chart is necessary because it gives customers a reference point. For something like Post Script, you could compare SMS versus email versus direct mail. Or you could compare direct competitors like Attentive, Klaviyo, and Send Lane. Or for CPG products, you might compare different product types rather than directly calling out competitors.

The point is that one element in the comparison chart should be something the customer knows well, which acts as a reference point to show how your product is better or worse in certain ways.

Something that's not always in landing pages but really works when it is is a four or five reasons why section about sixty percent of the way through. If someone's gotten that far, they're still looking for answers. If you reverse engineer what people talk about in their reviews into a listicle—starting with the most talked about features—you're basically reverse engineering what customers already value. Caraway pots and pans, for example, gets mentioned a lot for being nonstick, easy to clean, and easy to store. If you make that listicle with nonstick first, easy to clean second, and easy to store third, you're speaking directly to what customers care about.

There are also some interesting apps I've seen. One is Toll Story, where you get a video of the founder at the bottom right saying hello and welcoming people to the brand. I saw this recently on Wonder Mondays, which sells high-protein, low-carb cheesecake, and thought it was really interesting. There are also apps that show you live purchase notifications like "John just bought from five minutes ago from Omaha, Nebraska," or story-style reviews at the top of mobile pages.

I'm a huge fan of Toll Story, though I haven't personally implemented it. Everyone I know who's put it on has said it helped increase conversion. I think it works really well for brands that aren't super well known and have a good, publicly facing founder.

We've used an app called Livecom that does story-style content demonstration with UGC. It's helped on landing pages we've made, and it looks beautiful, renders quickly, loads quickly, and gives better analytics specific to the video than you normally get with YouTube shorts or Vimeo. But if you're not going to use Livecom, I'd recommend YouTube shorts because Vimeo gets expensive quickly. YouTube shorts are free, and embedding them is free too.

One trade-off to remember is that you can't have everything. If you add Toll Story, you might have to get rid of your live chat, or you can't have both your live chat and Klaviyo or Post Script pop-ups. I've seen landing pages with seven bubbles where you just feel attacked by bubbles. Even the simple "get 10% off" button that tricks you into an email pop-up is a terrible experience.

You have to make sure your landing page looks clean and represents your brand. There's definitely a trade-off, so when you use Toll Story and other tools, you might improve ROI but you're probably sacrificing some other element for it.

## Cash Management

When you have cash sitting at the company, that's something I've spent a bunch of time thinking about. This week I had a loan of $350,000 that matured and came back to me, so I'm now sitting on $350,000 in cash wondering what to do with it. Does it go to bonds? Does it go into Facebook ads?

The credit goes to you, but we just put it in treasuries. We call it farming—it just sits and generates returns. It goes into three month, six month, nine month, two year, and ten year treasuries. You want to make sure you're paying attention to duration though, because yields change. A year ago, ten year treasuries were yielding five percent or more, but now they're at 4.3 percent, so there's still a game to be played.

## Facebook Ads Issues

Facebook ads have been a complete shit show for the last two weeks. There was a bug that affected a handful of accounts in different ways. Some had issues where credit cards wouldn't charge and showed an internal platform error. Others had no spend for hours with no message. For others, CPM was completely inflated.

To handle this, the best approach is to find the most senior person that can help you quickly. Most people can't really do much, so you need to escalate. Some brands are still dealing with performance issues that aren't fully fixed yet, but it's slowly getting better.

I remember someone tweeted that they got off a call with their Facebook rep and it was a complete waste of time. Those Meta marketing people come from a contracted company and are generally just looking to get on the phone and see how they can get you to spend more. The real value is with the SMB reps in Austin and Mountain View who can actually help with saturation reports, CPMs, and platform issues.

The best reps are the disruptor teams in Mountain View and New York—they can make anything happen and move quickly. The disruptor reps get access to alphas and betas before anybody else, access to case studies, and various advantages that can save you anywhere from five to a hundred thousand dollars a quarter. They also have tier one support and typically manage fewer than ten clients compared to SMB reps who manage more than twenty-five.

The brands that do best on Facebook are the ones that really emphasize content and creative. They care less about the entire funnel and more about whether they have the best piece of content—something bright, colorful, flashy, and interesting.

Most of the reps that cold reach out aren't real Facebook reps—they're contracted from outside companies looking to get you to spend more. The real value reps are based in mountain view or Austin. People don't put enough effort into building relationships with Facebook reps and only hit them up when they need help, which doesn't help their situation.

## Failures That Taught Me

The biggest failure I was thinking about was trusting AI too much. We used a heatmap AI tool called Clueify for a bit, and when I dug into the report, it made no sense. But of course it doesn't make sense because it's a computer trying to act like a human analyzing a website that hasn't been run with real traffic yet.

The first business I ever tried to start was a social location mobile app to tell you where to go have fun. It was a terrible idea but a sexy idea. An investor was honest with me and said: "Don't build this. This is a terrible idea. You're never going to get distribution. You have no idea how hard this is. You're fucked." That was great advice.

The real lesson: don't try to build anything sexy. Everyone wants to build a sexy business because everyone wants to be a playboy, but they never work out. Uber might be the one exception, and even Uber wasn't sexy for a long time. Most other things fail.

For me personally, I've learned that I need motion to make things happen. I can't just sit and think about something on a sheet of paper. I have to start right away, and as it's failing, my mind is already working on the next thing. But I repeated the sexy business mistake by trying to start a liquor business selling hard liquor online. That wasn't a failure, but it had so many problems.

Before native, I wrote a Twitter thread about making Kayak for home loans. Getting a home loan is so painful, especially for entrepreneurs who don't have traditional W2s. Banks want reference letters and bank statements, but an entrepreneur's bank account might go from zero dollars to millions and then back to zero. I wanted to automate it by letting them sync my bank of America account so they could pull everything and automatically give me new rates when interest rates drop.

But that business didn't work. However, starting it taught me to just move and take action, and that motion created energy that ultimately led to starting the deal flow business, which became Native. The greatest lesson is that I need motion to make things happen. This is also the best way to learn about things like Facebook ads—you're not going to learn it from a course. You have to get in there with a thousand bucks and figure out what button does what. It's like riding a bike—you can't learn until you actually get on.

## When to Optimize for Profit

As a new direct-to-consumer brand, when should you start optimizing for profit versus product-market fit?

Most of my brands haven't revolutionized a category. Even Native didn't revolutionize deodorant—it just made it natural. Everyone already knew they needed deodorant. Generally, my brands had product-market fit but not always product-marketing fit, which is how you market in a way that makes sense and acquire customers for a reasonable amount.

At Native, I remember we were paying five dollars and fifty cents for a stick of deodorant and the math barely made sense. We had no money to spend on marketing, but I assumed that if the business worked, economies of scale would start working and we wouldn't be paying this enormous price for a stick of deodorant at the beginning.

I think that's true for a lot of categories. Brands like Shower heads, Athletic Greens, gummy vitamins, Symbiotica, and Magic Spoon all probably have cheaper wholesale costs now than when they launched. The unit costs have probably gone down by more than fifty percent. What was ten thousand units at the start might now be a hundred and fifty thousand units.

Assume that economies of scale will hit within the next six months. You won't get to P&G levels or Kellogg levels, but you'll get somewhere close. Calculate what your margin would be six months from now and assume that's your margin today. Then see if the economics work for marketing.

I do think profit is why you start a business, but you need to leave yourself room to test and figure things out before you decide to optimize purely for profit. You can't do that on day two or even month two of running a brand—there are still so many things you want to figure out.

If you're starting a new business today, think about it like this: if you're Jeff Bezos's son with forty trillion dollars, maybe you spend fifty million to figure it out. Brands like Lonia raised twenty million and went bankrupt. They went to their family for that money.

I'd think about it this way: I can spend five thousand dollars and I agree that I'm going to lose five thousand dollars no matter what. If I start at a one hundred dollar cost and can get it down to seventy-five dollars, I won't unlock another five thousand dollars in spend. But if I can get it from one hundred down to fifty, I'll unlock another ten thousand dollars. That's how I think about it.

If I've spent five thousand dollars and haven't learned anything or moved the needle, I might not unlock that other five thousand dollars. At an eighty thousand dollar annual income, that's fifty thousand post-tax, so you've just spent twenty percent of your year on Facebook ads that went nowhere. That's tough.

You want to see things going in the right direction, and only then unlock more spending. If nothing's getting better, if you went from one hundred to one hundred and twenty-three cents, move on. But if you went from one hundred to fifty, you're ready to spend another two hundred and fifty thousand.

## Finding Suppliers

There's probably no silver bullet for finding suppliers and materials. The best way is through introductions from people who've done it before or are familiar with it. I've always found warm intros from people who've had good experiences to be the best approach.

I was introduced to Mary Berry, who does packaging work. That's who we use for packaging. The alternative approach is using something like ImportYeti or Alibaba to search for suppliers, which you can definitely do to find great samples and products. But it's always better to get a warm intro.

Jeremy Kai from Atelic is another person who always helps with sourcing. He knows every factory in China and what they can do. He's made almost every product under the sun.

Warm intros work better for a couple reasons: you'll trust this person more because someone you know referred them, and they'll work harder for you because their reputation is at stake not just with you but with whoever made the introduction. If I make an introduction and it goes sideways, I feel responsible because I put my neck on the line and put my reputation on the line.

If there's no good warm intro, ImportYeti is great for seeing who's importing what from China and in what quantities. With Chinese suppliers, the hardest part is sending a wire to a bank in China you've never heard of and probably never will again. The bank name often doesn't fit in the account title field because it's a weird or different name.

Warm intros are really the only way to do this. Unfortunately, there aren't good resources out there otherwise. When working with a new 3PL or supplier, always ask for references, but try to get negative references too. Ask who's churned from that 3PL and find out what their experience was like on the way out. Did they mess up all your stuff? How was the 3PL when there was a billing issue?

I've had a lot of 3PL issues in my career. When someone talks about a problematic 3PL, I'll reach out to them on LinkedIn or Twitter and get on the phone. I'll share my bad experiences with that random person in Colorado or Oklahoma because you should not be using that person. I don't earn money from this, but I get satisfaction from making sure bad actors in the industry don't get more business.

The problem is too many people shill on the internet. This is the counter to that shilling. There's a lot of shilling on Twitter, so someone's got to make sure bad actors don't keep getting business.
