# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

The conversation began with Diana Cohen, founder of Crown Affair, discussing how the hair care industry has marketed perfection using aggressive language around taming, fixing, and managing hair. Diana explained her philosophy of shifting from a makeup approach to a skincare approach—focusing on consistent daily rituals rather than quick fixes. She outlined twelve baseline things she believed everyone should have, including investing in a high-quality hairbrush, which she noted helps physically exfoliate the scalp, moves natural oils from scalp to strand, and stimulates growth. She emphasized that most people don't think about their hair until something goes wrong, which keeps them in a disempowered, shame-based place rather than one of care and maintenance.

Diana shared her background, having grown up in South Florida and attended NYU, where she interned at Into the Gloss in 2012, before Instagram became what it is today. She worked with Ara Katz at Beach Mint and eventually met her again at a party at Acme, where Ara was starting a mobile shopping app called Jell-O Labs that would become Spring. This experience introduced her to the early days of D2C e-commerce and the Shopify ecosystem, where she met founders like Eric and Sasha from Buck Mason early in their journey.

After working at Spring, Diana was introduced to Tamara Mellon, founder of Jimmy Choo, where she learned about luxury direct-to-consumer strategy and working with high-end photographers and creative professionals. She then joined Away as the eighth employee, serving as head of partnerships. Her team from Away became her core creative team when she launched Crown Affair, including creative director Show, head of PR Ben, and head of social Kelsey.

Before Crown Affair, Diana launched a brand agency called Levitate, where she did go-to-market strategy and influencer work for brands like Outdoor Voices, Harry's, Flamingo, Buck Mason, Yumi, and the Wing. She earned Forbes 30 Under 30 recognition for this work. However, she felt she was giving away her special sauce as a consultant and wanted to become the innovator herself rather than just helping others.

Diana launched Crown Affair on January 28, 2020, six weeks before the pandemic hit. She started working on it nights and weekends, working with Show on the brand universe design and bringing in other friends like Dan, a bioengineering chemist who had worked at Supply and McKinsey, to develop products. The company is now about five years old with sixteen full-time employees, doing fifteen to eighteen million in revenue for the year.

When asked how she landed on hair care, Diana explained her deep love for hair. She was obsessed with achieving an air-dry look without relying on blowouts and dry bars. She geeked out on hair research, including reading books on hair history and even talking with a Pixar engineer who worked on hair simulators for films like The Incredibles. She wanted to create a brand with the product excellence of prestige salon brands like Orbe, Christophe Robin, or Kerastase, but that was also clean and aligned with how she actually talked about hair.

Regarding product development, Diana emphasized that making excellent products in beauty is very hard. Most beauty is mediocre, she said, using a Jamba Juice versus Erewhon smoothie analogy. Unlike makeup and skincare, which have become more democratized with added skincare benefits, hair care hasn't evolved the same way. Crown Affair works with chemists outside contract manufacturers rather than just using standard formulations with different labels. She acknowledged that many celebrity brands simply white-label products without real innovation, and while that makes business sense, it doesn't lead to lasting brands.

The brand itself is anchored around the concept of time. The trademark is "take your time," which refers both to the luxury of time and the literal benefit of their products—like a dry shampoo that allows customers to go seven or eight days between washes. Diana emphasized that clean and sustainable aren't brand pillars; they're product promises. The real differentiator is having strong brand foundation, visual literacy, and brand codes that make Crown Affair instantly recognizable.

When it comes to marketing, Diana explained that she didn't spend heavily on paid advertising. Early on, they spent about ten thousand dollars a month, and even now it's around fifty thousand. She believed that creating desirability can't be faked or bought; it requires having excellent products and building through word of mouth. She referenced working with Andrew Mitchell from Brand Foundry Ventures, her lead seed investor, who advised her not to spend a million dollars to make a million dollars, but rather to take her time and build a real business.

Diana worked with Show and copywriter Cole on the brand system, focusing on restraint and avoiding trends. She played a game of trying to identify brands that jumped on trends, finding that people couldn't even identify them. Good brands, like Chanel, have such strong codes that other brands copying them still feel like the originator. She mentioned being at Sephora where you can immediately spot Crown Affair due to the visual literacy and brand system.

The Doja Cat Met Gala moment, where Doja wore a Crown Affair towel with a pearl earring, became an iconic brand moment. The towel was inspired by Vermeer's Girl with the Pearl Earring, and the campaign simply said "take your time." The out of home agency initially questioned this approach, wondering what Crown Affair was doing acting like Bottega Veneta or Celine, but the moment proved powerful and underscored Crown Affair's luxury positioning around time and slowing down.

Regarding partnerships, Diana explained that she reached out to brands she loved, sometimes through direct messages. The MZ Wallace collaboration happened organically because she had shopped the brand for years and was eventually connected through someone at the company. She also mentioned wanting to work with brands like Goop, Rosie Huntington, and DS and Durga. She emphasized that partnerships with established brands were much more effective for customer acquisition than traditional paid advertising, as they met customers within communities they already belonged to.

On being a founder with a personal brand, Diana noted that she wasn't an influencer before launching Crown Affair and still doesn't consider herself one, though her following has grown. She loves Instagram as a direct communication tool and has made consistent TikToks, some of which have hit four to five million views. However, she acknowledged that content creation is just a sliver of her day-to-day responsibilities—she's fundraising, managing a team, running a P&L, going to Sephora for training and appearances. She's impressed by founder-influencers but acknowledged that making consistent content is harder than it looks. She prefers live experiences like podcasts, TV appearances, and panels where she can tell her story and people can fall in love with both the brand and her as the founder.

Crown Affair is primarily sold through Sephora, their main retailer, though they also work with strategic retailers like Goop and Violet Gray. Diana explained that Sephora wasn't the goal from day one—in fact, she wouldn't have designed the brand the same way if she had known about Sephora from the start. Show, as an artist, loves negative space and minimal packaging, but hair care typically needs more visual guidance with different colors and typefaces. However, Crown Affair has become a breath of fresh air in the Sephora environment.

Working with Harry's taught Diana the importance of distribution for business growth. While building D2C is best for margins, meeting customers where they are is essential. Sephora has the best loyalty program, app, and shopping experience, plus they're excellent at eventizing. Diana does personal appearances at Sephora stores, sometimes spending seven-hour days, which she finds incredibly valuable for learning from beauty advisors about customer needs and product development. She also participates in Sephora's quarterly "in the kitchen" meetings where retailers share insights about what customers are buying alongside Crown Affair products.

Initially, Crown Affair only had fifty-six doors in Sephora over two years, as hair care had less exposure than makeup and skincare. Now they have seventy-five doors and plan to expand to two hundred doors in fall. Different doors require different strategies based on local demographics and awareness levels. While some stores can't keep Crown Affair in stock, others in less established markets need different marketing approaches.

When asked about celebrity brands in hair care, Diana mentioned Rihanna's Fenty as a great example because she hired excellent teams and was intentional from day one, even though she doesn't personally know how to formulate products. She noted that Beyoncé recently launched hair around the same time, and comparing the two would be a good case study. Most celebrity brands, however, are either working with big partners like Amorous or just white-labeling products. Diana finds this frustrating because if the product isn't great, the brand won't survive five to six years. She tries new products constantly and finds most of them mediocre, though she acknowledges that not everyone wants excellent things and there's a market for mediocrity.

On underpriced attention, Diana mentioned that real-life experiences are becoming more important than ever, whether that's a cafe, pop-up, or gathering people in a room. She also predicted a shift by 2026 toward wanting people with real experience and legitimacy, especially as AI becomes more prevalent. People will likely tire of nine-year-olds and nineteen-year-olds claiming to be experts and will seek out people like Amy Poehler or Julia Louis-Dreyfus who have actual worked experience.

Diana discussed her mentorship program called Seedling, an eight-week program started after COVID hit when graduating seniors were looking for jobs. She and Elaine posted about it on Instagram hoping for ten applications and received two hundred and fifty. They personally read every application, made pairings with mentors from various industries—CMOs, stylists, fashion designers, beauty founders—and created an intimate community. Now in season eight, the program has led to roommate matches and job placements. While not easily scalable, Diana wished she could figure out how to expand it while maintaining its special, exclusive nature.
