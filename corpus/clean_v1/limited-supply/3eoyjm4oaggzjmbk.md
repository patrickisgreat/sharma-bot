# True Classic Tees: Building a Nine-Figure Brand in Two Years

Back in summer 2019, three founders—Ryan, Matt, and Nick—came up with an idea to completely redefine what a true classic t-shirt would look like on a guy. They wanted to find or create the best fitting shirt that was super comfortable and offer it for a really accessible price point. The purpose was simple: make everybody look good and feel good.

The initial R&D was just $3,000, focused on developing that first t-shirt. Ryan started with the idea of creating the most thoughtful t-shirt possible—a crew neck in black and white, but the best one in the world. The team asked themselves what guys actually want: comfort from soft fabrication and fit that's tight around the arms and chest but looser around the torso. They worked with an apparel manufacturer on favorable payment terms, typically net 60 or net 45 from when they received the product, which helped them float cash flow forward.

The first few months generated only tens of thousands of dollars, but around the third month, they hit $50,000 and realized they had genuine product-market fit. People raved about the brand, with customers saying it changed their lives and made them more confident. The reviews and low return rates proved they were onto something real.

## The Three-Dimension Expansion Strategy

Since that initial success, True Classic expanded across three key dimensions. First, on the product side, they moved from just t-shirts to about fifteen different categories including jeans, activewear, chinos, and jackets. The belief was simple: apply the same mentality that worked for t-shirts across the entire wardrobe.

Second, they expanded globally. Recognizing that the sizing and fit problems they solved for American customers weren't local issues but global ones, they took the True Classic magic worldwide. International markets now represent about twenty-five to thirty percent of their business, with presence in 190 markets.

Third, they pursued an omnichannel strategy, launching on multiple platforms including their app, Facebook and Instagram shops, Amazon, and opening five retail locations. They aggressively advertise across Meta, TikTok, and Google, continuously casting a wider net to find customers wherever they may be.

By year one they did $15 million in revenue, year two they did $90 million, and year three they did $150 million—all while bootstrapping and remaining profitable. They became one of the fastest growing direct-to-consumer brands ever, all in a commoditized space.

## The Meta Foundation

The backbone of their marketing spend has always been Meta, though it varies by season. During certain periods like Cyber 5, Meta can represent eighty percent of spend, while other months it might be fifty percent. The Disruptors program at Meta was crucial to their early success. This program identifies about eighty to ninety high-potential companies out of millions of advertisers and provides white-glove service including creative guidance, measurement support, technology access, and partnership opportunities.

Being in the Disruptors program allowed Ben to understand the insane potential of scaling companies quickly through the platform. While many brands blamed iOS 14 and privacy changes for Meta's decline, True Classic focused on solving real problems with real product-market fit. They leveraged Meta's scale by advertising very broadly since their product could serve both direct customers and gift buyers like girlfriends, wives, and daughters buying for their significant others.

## The Profitability Approach

Rather than optimizing for revenue at all costs, True Classic was born as a sustainable, profitable business from day one. They focused on simple, fundamental metrics: how many dollars do you make back for every dollar spent? They developed a deep understanding of their P&L, accounting for payment processing fees, fulfillment costs, expected returns and exchanges, product costs, and outbound shipping.

They don't think in terms of a traditional marketing budget. Instead, they work under two constraints: performance efficiency and inventory. Subject to those constraints, they try to maximize scale. When it comes to customer acquisition, they look at profit per new customer, not just CAC or ROAS. They calculate everything including expected returns, expected exchanges, expected customer service tickets, and all variable costs. Their ideal scenario is to break even on first customer acquisition, knowing the repeat behavior and cohort analysis will yield profitability.

For last year's $150 million in revenue, approximately two-thirds came from new customers and one-third from repeat customers. Their new customer acquisition spend was estimated between $50 and $60 million. This means they were willing to break even or even lose a few dollars per customer upfront, understanding that cohort analysis and repeat purchases would create long-term profitability.

## The Math Behind Efficiency

The approach is fundamentally about finding breakthroughs that incrementally improve efficiency. These breakthroughs can be creative, technology-based, landing page improvements, or any optimization that bends the curve. By constantly improving efficiency, they can acquire more customers while maintaining profitability constraints.

They don't use traditional remarketing because it becomes too cost prohibitive. However, they recognize that even with exclude lists, there's leakage and indirect repeat behavior being driven by advertising. Advertising keeps the brand relevant and top-of-mind, so it contributes to repeat purchases even if that connection isn't always directly attributable.

For email and SMS, they don't rely on what platforms like Klaviyo tell them about attribution. Instead, they segment customers into four groups: those without email or SMS, those with email only, those with SMS only, and those with both. The delta in average customer value between these groups represents the attributed value of each channel. This methodology avoids attribution debates about last-click versus first-touch.

## YouTube and Creative Strategy

YouTube is one of their more challenging channels to crack, largely because the creative bar is much higher than other platforms. Most brands aren't good enough at creative to make YouTube work, but True Classic has creative excellence as a core competency. Their founder Ryan is a creative genius, which is a huge reason for their fast growth and momentum.

While conventional wisdom says attention span is at an all-time low and everything needs to be short-form, True Classic has found that well-crafted longer-form content can work and sometimes even convert better. The key is making content worthy of watching and building authentic human-to-human connections with audiences. They differentiate themselves by talking about their products in ways no other apparel brand does, using funny, edgy, relatable content rather than polished, on-brand marketing.

The on-surface ROAS for YouTube is decimal—extremely low. Most operators would shut it down immediately. But when they run incrementality studies with partners like Measured, they find significant unmeasured value. Surveys show many people attribute their purchases to YouTube content they encountered weeks or months prior. When asked when they first heard about the brand versus how, a different picture emerges. They calibrate YouTube targets based on survey-derived implied ROAS, looking for over 2.1X to justify continued investment.

## Creative and Production

They handle creative through a combination of in-house teams, UGC creators, influencers with whitelisting, larger agencies like Tube Science and Narrative that operate on performance-based models, and other specialized agencies. They work with bigger creators like GregTube who've been with them since early days and create funny, edgy ads.

The approach to creative is rooted in understanding consumer psychology. Do you really understand your customer—who they are, what they want, what they feel, what problems you solve? Most marketing teams get stuck in their own heads about how an ad should look or whether it feels elevated enough. True Classic takes a human-to-human, authentic connection approach focused on being funny, edgy, and relatable while hitting key product points.

For budget allocation, the more they believe a piece of creative will work, the happier they are to spend on it. They validate before committing big budgets, starting with small testing and whitelisting to build confidence before cutting larger checks.

## The Team Structure

They maintain a very lean marketing team. Their hiring philosophy is to solve business needs through technology first, then SaaS partners, then agencies, then freelancers and consultants, and only hire full-time if nothing else works. They believe in investing heavily in two areas: analytics (which drives strategy) and creative (which drives execution). Everything else can be outsourced.

Their team includes a director of analytics, analytics partners like Measured and Triple Whale, in-house creative talent with a visual design leader and copywriting leader, supplemented by freelancers, consultants, and agencies as needed. A new VP of growth was starting the following week, hired from Meta. Their entire marketing OPEX is under one percent of revenue, intentionally lean.

## Retail Strategy

They've opened five retail locations strategically in cities where they have high customer concentration: two in Chicago, one in Tyson's Corner, and two in Southern California. Rather than chasing premium locations like New York City, they looked for places where their business math worked. They evaluate retail ROAS similar to everything else, aiming to break even within the four walls while understanding there's broader online impact including brand building, trust development, and omnichannel customer experiences.

They look for relatively short-term lease commitments to minimize risk and recently hired a new head of retail. Their approach is to prove retail works at a five-store scale, then scale significantly. They try different tactics within stores like in-store discounts, free shirts for trial, poker chips they hand out for store credit, and surprising customers with personalized touches to build word-of-mouth and brand love.

## Return Rates and Risk Management

Their return rate on core products like shirts is low single-digit, which they believe is low compared to the apparel industry average. They attribute this to taking fit seriously with fit calculators and thoughtful sizing. New categories like chinos and denim have higher return rates, primarily due to sizing challenges when selling online. They're working to minimize returns through better fit tools, potential AR implementations, and strategies to convert returns into exchanges.

## Customer Service Philosophy

Their customer service team is directed to provide a Ritz-Carlton experience with no rules. If something will make a customer happy, they do it. They've surprised pregnant customers with maternity leave packets. When someone angry reaches out with an issue, the goal is for them to leave raving about the experience. Their CSAT is north of ninety-five percent, with customers who came in frustrated often becoming evangelists.

This approach works because it builds trust in an online environment where there's an inherent trust deficit. Customers need to trust that the product is good and that if there's an issue, the brand will take care of them. By obsessing over customer experience and leading with empathy, they create advocates who tweet about their experiences and leave glowing reviews.

## Keys to Success

Their core values of creative thinking, resourcefulness, and leading with empathy inform everything they do. They obsess over understanding their customers and making them look good and feel good. The fundamentals they emphasize are simple: ads, website, landing pages, PDPs, checkout experience, email, and SMS. If you do those core things really well, you're already ahead of most brands.

They avoid distraction and maintain laser focus on the most impactful areas using the eighty-twenty rule. They also think about advertising spend as an investment in brand assets that should live on the balance sheet, not just expense items on the P&L. Email addresses, SMS subscribers, and customer relationships have residual value that gets monetized over time, not just on day one.

The post-purchase upsell strategy exemplifies their efficiency focus—adding incremental revenue with minimal additional cost while driving penetration into new categories. When someone comes for a shirt and tries the underwear through an upsell, they become a multi-category customer with higher lifetime value.

## What This Playbook Could Work For

If the entire playbook from the last few years were turned into a textbook for starting businesses, Ben would apply it to large TAM opportunities with broad appeal. Blue ocean strategy—going into large terms where there's room to win. The platforms, products, and brand messaging that work for apparel could translate to other categories where the fundamentals of great ads, great landing pages, great email, and great customer experience matter. The key is not to get caught up in niche opportunities where advertising becomes cost prohibitive due to regulatory or privacy constraints. Go big where you can scale meaningfully.
