# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript: Post-Black Friday with Ben True Classic Tees

This is the first episode we're doing after Black Friday, and we have a special guest. I reached out to Ben from True Classic Tees just after Black Friday and asked him to share what happened during their biggest sales event of the year. Ben, welcome back to the show. This is the second time you're here this year, and your first appearance was the most listened-to episode of Limited Supply. We're so grateful for your time.

I'm going to jump straight into business. First question: Did 2023 Black Friday Cyber Monday beat your expectations?

Yes. We changed strategy year over year. Last year was all about growth primarily. We're still relatively profitable as a DTC brand. We actually took a different approach this year focused on maximizing EBITDA. We spent more conservatively than we did year over year and had by far the most profitable month in the company's history by a large margin, while still generating over 30 million dollars in top-line sales for the entire month of November.

Just to be clear, that 30 million is not just for Black Friday itself, but for the entire month of November. And that was larger than your November 2022 top line?

Yes, still larger. When you say you spent more conservatively, what does that mean exactly? Does your Black Friday spending start earlier in October, or when does your spending strategy kick in?

We started this year a lot earlier, in line with other brands. It feels like every year the start date just keeps moving up. I wouldn't be surprised if next year we start in September. This year we started the sale on November 2nd. We figured if shoppers are already shopping, we might as well have our best foot forward with a great offer.

We started November spending early, but we really ramped it up Thursday night and going into Friday. When determining how much to spend, we have crazy intraday monitors. It's almost like we're trading stocks. We look at hourly pacing of what's coming in and where we stand on profitability in terms of contribution profit percentages. We also look at other indicators like last-click attribution to make sure we're not pushing money into channels that aren't driving immediate action.

Every day we meet at six in the morning, eight in the morning, ten, noon. Every two hours we talk about where we're currently trending. For us, two things matter: top-line revenue net of all discounts and returns, and bottom-line EBITDA. We keep re-projecting, so if we're thinking about pushing another hundred thousand dollars on Meta, we look at what we're learning today and what we expect will happen over the next five days.

So you come in with a pretty blank dry erase board rather than a pre-determined Black Friday budget?

Exactly. Even intraday, every two hours we're making decisions. Did you generally push up spend or pull back? What was the trajectory like at eight, ten, and noon?

What we do is optimize for what I call marginal incremental contribution profit. Marginal being the last dollar versus the average, which is very different. Incremental means truly driving it as opposed to attributions. Contribution profit accounts for all variable costs and structure.

We start the day and look at how we're trending overall. If we're looking really strong on a last-click basis, we're bullish and start pushing more. We have Triple Whale monitoring actual blended contribution profit trending hour by hour. If hourly contribution profit starts falling short of expectations and we don't see the return on a marginal basis from extra spending, we pull back. We don't see the value in that additional push on an intraday basis.

So certain channels we start aggressive and have to pull back. For us on Meta specifically, ASC takes a back seat and CBO starts performing really well. Even within channels, certain tactics we bet on didn't work out, so we shifted budget to others. We take that super tactically with no set budget other than the goal of maximizing contribution profit throughout the day.

At your ten a.m. meeting, when you're looking at ads and say it looks like we can spend more on Meta, is that the discussion you have?

Correct. And then we say, how much? We make a prediction. That's where we want to see if we're good at forecasting. We keep calibrating ourselves. We don't know what the future holds, but it's good to name the number. If I were to spend another hundred thousand dollars, I believe that's going to net us under 150 thousand back, whatever the case may be. You name it, then see what happens and learn whether you overstated or understated. It really helps you refine your forecasting skills. I think as a media buyer, if you just spend without knowing what to expect to come back, that's not great practice. We challenge ourselves to be better at forecasting.

Have you seen a difference in customers acquired during the year versus those acquired during Black Friday?

From an LTV perspective, gifters tend to be less creative and worse cohorts. The other really interesting thing I'm trying to understand is that we ask when people first heard about the brand with different time horizons: today, this week, this month, this year, three to six months, or longer.

Did you change your offer a lot during Black Friday, or was it pretty consistent? For instance, Me Undies does twelve days of Black Friday with different items discounted each day.

How do you think about bundles? Is it about what bundle gets a customer to purchase right now, or do you also consider clearing inventory? How do you balance inventory you want to get rid of versus maximizing sales?

Both are considerations, but I prioritize what's right for our customer. Inventory challenges are real but secondary. We use different creative methods for different problems. We do mystery packs, which surprise and delight customers while giving us flexibility on fulfillment. We're trying not to conflate different problems.

From a demand planning perspective, we're trying to level up our game by thinking about packs instead of individual items. We source bundles as units, which saves costs and headaches. Sometimes we still operate more chaotically, putting individual SKUs together on the fly, but we're working toward pre-planned packs as our model.

What's your finance stack? How are you financing all the ad spend and inventory while running profitably?

What we try to do is run really efficiently. Most direct-to-consumer brands, if run properly with a focus on profitability, don't require venture capital. There are two capital-intensive activities in most DTC brands: inventory and advertising.

With ads, we try to spend properly where we ideally break even on the initial order. If the contribution profit equals zero per customer initially, we don't have cash concerns because ads basically pay for themselves through immediate direct response. That's solved through smart attribution and data-driven analytics.

On the inventory side, we never raised equity. We're still bootstrap well into our nine-figure brand. What we've done is raise debt and keep refinancing. We have over 38 million in available credit at low double-digit interest rates. We're always looking to refinance and eventually get a bigger line with a big bank at cheaper rates.

Beyond those two, we're always thinking about cash conversion cycle and reducing DIO and payment terms with suppliers. We do things like hold reserves of fabrics at source to turn things around faster and hold less inventory, requiring less working capital. We're constantly focused on reducing that and getting as close to negative cash conversion cycle as possible.

If you're doing marketing and advertising well, it should pay for itself and you shouldn't need to raise money for it. If you're running into cash problems from ads, you're probably overspending.
