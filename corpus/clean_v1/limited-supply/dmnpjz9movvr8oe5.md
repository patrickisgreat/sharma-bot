# Limited Supply Podcast Transcript

When it comes to investors, YC had an interesting take. They said investors are good for one thing: money. The question is whether that advice holds up over time. I think it's actually a pretty smart overgeneralization, especially early on. There are bad investors who get in your way, slow things down, and try to micromanage. Then there are solid investors—a smaller number—who stay out of your way, give you the money, and are supportive when you ask. And finally, there's a small group who are proactively trying to create value while still staying out of your way.

The idea behind that YC advice is to orient early-stage founders to get back to work instead of spending months fundraising, because fundraising doesn't grow your business. It's an overgeneralization, but probably a wise one at that stage.

Alex is one of the co-founders and president of PostScript. We go back about five years—he invested in PostScript in March 2019, right around the time PostScript was coming out of Y Combinator. Before PostScript, Alex worked in e-commerce at StackCommerce in LA, where he met his co-founder Adam Turner. They became fast friends after initially being wary of each other, both being ambitious and maybe a little intimidated.

Alex left StackCommerce after six years when the company had grown from five people to about eighty. He went to work there straight out of high school in 2012, learning everything about traffic optimization, partnerships, and sales. Then Adam's older brother Colin reached out about an idea they were working on—a Shopify app that would let brands text their customers. This was 2018.

The inspiration came from a friend running a lifestyle business on Shopify who complained he had no way to text his customers. Adam and Colin had already started writing code, and Alex got involved talking to customers. They all stayed at their full-time jobs initially because they thought it would be a small passive side business. They didn't understand how big the Shopify market was.

Within a couple months of launching, they had two paying customers, then four, then six, then eight. Their first customer was actually a bootleg Donald Trump merchandise store selling MAGA hats. The revenue grew from $2,000 to $4,000 to $8,000 a month, and they applied to Y Combinator on a whim. Alex kept updating their application every weekend with new revenue numbers, so the YC partners saw the consistent growth.

They had no connections in San Francisco, hadn't worked at famous tech companies, but somehow they got an interview, prepared intensely, and got a slot.

When it came to dividing responsibilities early on with $8,000 in monthly revenue, it was all hands on deck. But there was a natural split. Adam and Colin were self-taught hackers who wrote code—they did the front-end and back-end work respectively. Alex didn't write code but was responsible for everything customer-facing. He had sales experience from StackCommerce. Over time, they knew they wanted Adam to be CEO since it was his creation. The roles fell into place pretty quickly.

On pricing, they faced an interesting problem. Email has virtually no cost to send, but text messages have real infrastructure costs. When they started, there were companies like ManyChat building Facebook Messenger bots, and everyone was hoping text messaging would become viable but cheaper than it is. PostScript was ahead of Klavio, Attentive, and Yotpo in offering SMS for e-commerce.

They did exactly what you'd expect for pricing: looked at their cost structure from Twilio's self-service rates and roughly doubled them. It was straightforward—here's what it costs us, let's mark it up.

On the difference between PostScript and competitors like Klavio and Attentive, there are actually multiple layers.

The first is growing your list faster. PostScript has an on-site opt-in feature that keeps users on the brand's website instead of sending them to their messages app. It beats the traditional paradigm in AB tests. One brand Alex mentions had a pacing chart for expected subscriber opt-ins by quarter, and when they launched on-site opt-in through PostScript, it "broke their graph." The improvement was that dramatic.

There's also how PostScript enables cashback incentives instead of just coupon discounts. Instead of offering 10% off, a brand can offer 20% cashback that the customer uses on their next purchase or gets as a digital visa. About fifty percent of consumers don't redeem it. Of those who do, many take store credit, which means the brand got paid in full for the first purchase and keeps that cash for working capital purposes. It's meaningfully higher margin while still being more attractive to customers than a straight discount.

On making more money from marketing tooling, PostScript has features like view-triggered price drop automations. If someone spends a lot of time looking at a specific product but never buys it, the price drops automatically and they get a triggered text. That's a low-volume, high-value automation that "prints money."

There's also SKU-level back-in-stock notifications. While competitors have back-in-stock features, PostScript's is at the SKU level with triggered automations, which matters tremendously for brands with lots of variants. If you're high-SKU, getting people the right variant is really important.

The conversational messaging feature is another differentiator. Flow builder isn't just for sending—it's two-way because real messaging is two-way. Brands can ask questions in their welcome flow to capture zero-party data, and PostScript's system handles fuzzy human replies. If someone says "I'm thinking about some vests," the system recognizes that, not just exact match replies like "vests," and routes them appropriately while tagging their profile.

On billing and pricing differences, PostScript bills you at the end of the month for what you use—not a credit-based tier system like Klavio. PostScript also doesn't charge for inbound messages or undelivered messages, which other platforms do. Messages fail for various reasons—someone's on a plane, their phone's off, they don't have service—and undelivered messages can represent a few percentage points of volume. That's a meaningful cost difference.

There's also how PostScript handles MMS billing differently than Attentive. MMS can contain 1,600 characters when you include emojis, but Attentive charges for the base MMS and then charges extra SMS fees if you exceed 160 characters. PostScript's approach is better for brands sending long MMS messages.

Finally, there's strategy and support. PostScript focuses exclusively on SMS for Shopify. Everyone on the team gets trained on SMS only, not email or other platforms. They offer a two-minute chat support SLA during business hours, and their sales team and customer success team deeply understand SMS because that's all they do. One brand, Jones Road Beauty, wanted to understand the incrementality of their SMS spend, and PostScript's data science team ran a custom program to help them understand exactly how much of their revenue was incremental from SMS. That kind of sophisticated analysis isn't something most other platforms would even know how to begin addressing.

The honest feedback on marketing is that PostScript does a poor job explaining these differences on their website. A comparison chart showing feature differences versus Klavio and Attentive—the kind of chart that exists everywhere from Amazon product pages to infomercials—should be front and center but isn't.

The response is that the space moves so fast that differences between platforms change every few months, so a chart can get stale quickly. There's also the fact that some players in the industry are highly litigious and aggressive, so maybe being careful about direct comparisons is strategic. PostScript has strong sales decks and comparison documents for people who go through the sales process, and the market results speak for themselves—all the best D2C brands are choosing PostScript.

But the point stands: for a small business evaluating SMS platforms and trying to make a decision without going through a full sales process, the differentiation should be clearer. For enterprise brands with big sales teams, they can extract the information during the sales process. For smaller brands, it's harder to figure out why PostScript is better than the alternatives just from the website.

There has been a clear shift toward working with larger enterprise brands—the ones doing $50 million to $100 million plus in revenue. That's where PostScript sees the most value, where the team can be deeply focused on one channel. Brands often ask why they shouldn't just use an all-in-one tool with email and SMS combined for convenience and unified attribution.

The answer is that if all you want to do is turn on an abandoned cart flow and never think about SMS again, an all-in-one tool might make sense. But if you're actually trying to make the most money from the channel as efficiently as possible, you want to work with a team and product deeply focused on SMS that's pushing the envelope and innovating.

Campaigns significantly outpace flows in terms of volume for PostScript customers. Campaigns are much higher volume. About a year and a half ago, PostScript created something called a campaign flow—basically a combination of both, where you drop a bunch of users into a flow-like experience that functions more like a series of campaigns.

How often brands send texts varies wildly based on their merchandising and marketing calendar. Fashion and apparel brands can send high volume because they have constant new products and promotions, just like with email. Home goods brands send much less frequently. Best practices reflect what makes sense for each brand's strategy.

The most sophisticated brands are under-sending based on what incremental data shows. There's been a big shift toward efficiency and cost-cutting, but in SMS specifically, that's created an over-correction. When PostScript runs incrementality studies comparing a control group getting no texts, a group getting prior cadence, and a group with higher sending, the higher-sending group drives the most return by far.

Most brands are segmenting their SMS messages so campaigns go to relevant audiences, not blasting everyone. PostScript's largest customer is spending over six figures annually on SMS, and yes, there are customers spending seven figures on SMS annually. These are brands with big lists who invest in growth and send a lot of messages. SMS lists are still really small compared to email lists because email collection has been the default for years and has been happening longer.

Best practices are evaluated through something called subscriber LTV—what's the value of an SMS phone number to you? PostScript surfaces benchmarks by product category on their site. What flows into subscriber LTV is the revenue per message you're seeing, messages per subscriber on a monthly basis, and the D30 unsubscribe rate—how many people unsubscribe in the first thirty days.

People are five times more likely to unsubscribe in the first month than they are afterward, so having a great first month journey keeps them on the list longer. That's the kind of insight an all-in-one platform will never think to work through with you.

PostScript launched an AI-powered conversational sales feature. Sales agents have access to products so they can make creative and take pictures and experience items live. The agents are trained extensively on brand voice with brand voice audits. It's still in closed beta and requires brands to have certain revenue levels—generally over $7 million annually—for the unit economics to work.

When someone has a conversation with a sales agent, the brand pays PostScript a commission on the sale, and PostScript pays the sales agent a commission too. The brand covers the text message costs, which are a tiny drop in the bucket compared to campaign volume—just a single-digit percentage point increase in SMS volume.

There's a sweet spot where conversational sales work best. Medium to high AOV brands see the highest incremental lift. True Classic around $100 AOV has done well with it. Caraway, selling pots and pans at higher AOV, has seen incredible success. High-consideration, high-AOV goods benefit from having conversations where objections can be handled and customers educated.

But even lower AOV brands with engaged communities or strong brand voices see good results. Dr. Squatch isn't super high AOV, but their community on Reddit dedicated to conversing with their personas is raving about the experience. More than 2% of Dr. Squatch's sales have been touched by someone having a conversational exchange through PostScript. The goal is to eventually double a brand's SMS revenue through this feature.

PostScript has grown from a tiny startup five years ago to almost 300 people across offices in Phoenix, Tel Aviv, and elsewhere. The growth came through strong product-market fit combined with grinding through community engagement. Early on, they showed up at events, worked with agency partnerships, participated in social communities, and built word of mouth.

On the value of sponsoring events like Shoptalk or GrowCon, PostScript does find value there, but it's not just hanging a booth. The team actively works events instead of sitting in a booth. They host dinners and events around major conferences.

Here's the secret: you need unit economics that make event marketing worthwhile. SMS is expensive enough that PostScript can charge significantly more than software vendors charging $250 a month, so the economics of expensive event strategies work out.

Early on, Alex did something memorable: he offered to run a brand's SMS strategy himself for the holiday season before Black Friday their second year, when they were nobody. That kind of hands-on community engagement helped refine the product based on real customer feedback and generated tremendous word of mouth.

None of this is particularly sexy. They do run some paid ads, but that's not where growth comes from. It's about driving positive customer sentiment, word of mouth, and being actively engaged in the game.
