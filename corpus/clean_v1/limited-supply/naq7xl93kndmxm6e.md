# Limited Supply - Bootstrapped Zero to One Playbook

Before you touch the website or hire a designer, you need to figure out your wedge. This is the clear, sharp reason someone will pick you over the forty-seven other options in your category. Your wedge isn't your mission statement or a claim that you use better ingredients. It's the very specific, concrete thing that makes people stop scrolling. It's often problem-solution messaging—the thing no one else is solving well and explaining how you solve it in a way that's super obvious.

If you can't say it in a single sentence, you don't have it yet. And if you don't have that, don't move forward with the idea. A wedge might be innovation in ingredients, like colostrum for Armra, or parazanthine for an energy drink that doesn't have the caffeine crash. It could be innovation in form factor, like a filter head for a shower. It could be price. It could be distribution access that your competition doesn't have. It could be cost advantages that let you spend more on marketing than anyone else.

Think about your wedge both from an internal standpoint—what's our reason as a team we can win—and from a consumer standpoint—what's the reason I'm going to buy this product over others? Most brands just don't have one. About eighty percent of brands at Expo West, the natural food and beverage trade show, are revolving in and out every couple of years. Only twenty percent stay. Why? Because they don't have a wedge.

Also think about the wedge for each channel you plan to sell on. If you're launching on TikTok Shop, Amazon, Nordstrom, Target, Vitamin Shop, and your own website, you always want to control your own website and think strategically about what your wedge is on that sales channel specifically. Why would somebody come to your website over Amazon or Target? If there's no answer to that question, you're just spending dollars inefficiently on ads.

Now let's talk about the website. Think about it like Rodeo Drive in LA or Fifth Avenue in New York—iconic streets competing for your attention. These websites need to be just as good as your Fifth Avenue storefronts from a design and development standpoint. Every piece needs to earn its spot. It needs to be fast. It needs to be clean. The design, copy, layouts, content, modules, and how everything works together needs to be intentional. You should be able to answer why every single element of the website is the way it is.

Mobile first is essential. Your homepage has one job: to get people to understand what you sell and why they should care within three seconds. The rest of the website should have good merchandising and navigation. I would definitely not settle for default Shopify navigation. You want to guide people through a story, not just product categories. Make it editorial and fun. The same goes with your cart. Don't settle for the default—add to cart, go to a separate URL with nothing else, then checkout. You want that to be much more efficient with a custom cart.

You can build this website two ways. Option one is finding a theme on the Shopify theme market or other theme marketplaces. These themes are maybe sixty-five to seventy percent of the way there. You can either work with a developer or use Claude or ChatGPT to figure out how to get that last thirty percent of customization done yourself.

Option two is to piecemeal it. Find a freelance designer who really understands web design and conversion rate optimization and consumer journey thinking. Pair that with an offshore developer from Ukraine, India, Sri Lanka, or Serbia. Either find a theme and then use a developer for customization, use one of the AI models to do it yourself, or work with a designer to do a full custom design in Figma and then have a developer do full development.

Depending on how senior your designer and developer are, this could cost anywhere from ten thousand dollars on the low end for a decent website all the way up to six figures. But you don't have to have the equivalent of a six-figure website right at launch. You could probably get that down to a third of the price if you piecemeal it yourself.

If you know design and strategy yourself, work through it with Claude. Tell Claude to make you a wireframe based on how to approach this brand, based on the product brief. Start editing from there. Once you feel good about the layout and module standpoint from a content perspective, bring that to a designer to design it up, then push it to a developer to integrate the tech stack. The developer handles setting up subscription, analytics, pixels, and all that kind of stuff.

For landing pages, you can build them the same way or use tools like listicle apps that let you spin them up quickly and put them on any publisher domain. Create a few landing page templates in Claude as wireframes, move those to a designer, get them developed, then duplicate those pages and swap all the content for different products, offers, messages, and angles.

A solid website should probably be between ten and fifteen thousand, maybe seventeen or eighteen thousand. You're getting something that feels really custom, feels clean, and is heavy on brand while being very thought-through from a performance and conversion standpoint. Make sure it's fast, intentional, and every element earns its spot.

Keep your tech stack lean. There's a lot of noise about what you should use, but you don't need everything right at the beginning. You need your Shopify platform set up and optimized. You need email. You need SMS, or at least SMS collection. You need basic analytics. You need subscription if you have that. You need reviews. Maybe a cart upsell product. That's pretty much all you need. Don't let SaaS marketing make you think you need every single app running at full capacity.

As you start generating revenue and hit new targets—five thousand, ten thousand, twenty thousand in daily revenue—then you start thinking about incorporating other tools like attribution, incrementality testing, forecasting, catalog management, or generative AI tools.

Email and SMS are your owned channels besides your website. These are more push methods where you can push messages to people, whereas the website requires them to come to you. It's much cheaper than running ads. You need to have automated flows running before you drive serious traffic. This includes welcome series, abandoned cart, abandoned checkout, abandoned site, post-purchase, and win back flows. Build, test, and launch these before you drive serious ad spend.

Most people think the growth funnel is just ads and website, but it's really ads, website, email form capture, and the actual emails you send. Your conversion rate might start at two percent, meaning ninety-eight percent bounce without buying. But if your email capture rate is three percent, that means ninety-seven percent bounce without you even getting their email. Using a tool like Klaviyo with a form optimizer like Alibi, you might capture eight to fifteen percent of traffic, which means way more shots on goal to send follow-up emails or texts and get that sale. This makes your entire blended acquisition cost go down.

Don't light money on fire by paying for ads without proper email infrastructure. You're either doing the work yourself or paying Facebook more to do it for you. You're paying higher CPMs because you're less well-known and your content gets less engagement.

Also send campaign emails regularly. Think about product drops, content, education, promotions. As a barometer, ask if this is something somebody could bring up later at a dinner table conversation. Don't just send emails to send emails. Send things of value. Those are more chances someone will learn your brand better and figure out why they should be using your product.

If you have a natural replenishment cycle—supplement, beauty, personal care, protein powder—you know when people will run out. Push subscription, which is ideal both for your life as an operator and for company valuation. Otherwise use email, SMS, and remarketing ads to remind existing buyers to purchase. The more you can push people to subscribe, the better.

Content is the best type of funnel. Look at brands like The Pause Life with Dr. Mary Claire Haver or KaramMDSkin with Dr. Amir Karam. They crush it because they have the best content in their category. What they do well is put out a ton of content—we're talking a dozen videos a week. This rapid output helps you quickly understand what people actually want to know about your brand and products.

Volume is key here. It's not about getting a handful of creators with followers to post something. It's about getting tons of creators to post all different types of things and figure out what works. Think of it like a March Madness bracket. You've got all these ideas for angles and videos, and you basically hit them against each other until you narrow down to what works best, then double down on it.

This is how Vayner approaches finding the Superbowl ad for big brands. They start with thousands of pieces of content across social, narrow down to concepts and formats that perform and do well, and bring those to larger formats and larger screens until the final winner becomes the Superbowl commercial.

Figure out how to get content efficiently and low cost. If you're food and beverage, find every possible event you can donate product to in exchange for content. Build a network of creators you can work with. Do brand partnerships at events. Figure out a content engine. If you don't do this work, you're just paying Facebook the difference as more ads and higher CPMs because you're less well-known.

Think about packaging and unboxing as an experience. The box, inserts, branded packaging tape—these are all huge. Every package you ship is an opportunity to drive a message. Use branded inserts for discount codes, notes to leave reviews, or referral offers. The box doesn't have to be super expensive but should be intentional. Make sure your product fits nicely so it doesn't feel cheap or lazy. Branded packaging tape feels premium and custom but isn't expensive if you source it right.

Understand shipping logistics. Two-day shipping is an expectation now. Work backwards from that. If you're shipping nationwide, consider getting a spot on the West Coast and East Coast. Know your shipping zones, average package weight, dimensions, carriers, and all these details yourself. Don't just leave it to a 3PL. You should be able to have conversations about their numbers and challenge them if something doesn't make sense.

For paid media and ad creative, start by learning Facebook ads yourself. Refuse to accept that any founder is too good to learn this. If you think that way, you'll get ripped off by whoever you hire because they've got a dozen clients and you're not their priority. Only you're your number one priority. Understand what's happening with your ads. Look at patterns and suggest things.

Think about how your content engine turns into actual ad creative. Test static images, video, UGC, founder to camera, testimonials. Test hooks, formats, and angles. Creative testing never stops. The biggest brands have a role called creative strategist whose only job is coming up with things to throw against the wall and see what sticks. Remember that content moves fast on shorts, reels, and TikTok. Trends and formats come and go within months.

Organic content gets respect if it's good, but ads are intruding in someone's feed with zero initial respect. You have to earn that respect through creativity. You need discipline about speed and how fast you're moving on testing things and moving away from what isn't working. Ship things daily, not weekly or monthly. Look at data constantly. Ask questions constantly.

The hardest part of any brand is zero to one. Once you get to one, it slowly gets easier. But zero to one is where the most time is usually lost because you have to figure out how to take something you think is great and turn it into something other people think is also great and want to buy. Someone might think your product is great, but if they don't buy into it with the same vision, you're stuck with one-time purchasers. It's not easy to build a massive business on that unless you're selling something like Eight Sleep mattresses, but even then, repeat purchases happen.
