Sniff, a fragrance brand, recently collaborated with Half Baked Harvest, a media company with six point three million monthly viewers and four point seven million Instagram followers. Through this partnership, Sniff did their entire 2021 revenue in just one week. The collaboration succeeded because Half Baked Harvest had an organically built, highly engaged audience developed over years. The timing was also perfect, as the partners launched a pumpkin-scented candle in October during pumpkin spice season.

The collaboration happened organically through Instagram DMs after they met and sent each other product. Rather than requiring upfront costs, they split all production costs and split the gross margin fifty-fifty, excluding COGS, discounts, shipping, and fulfillment costs. The collaboration generated mid-six figures in revenue, and they smartly used pre-orders rather than waitlists to collect cash upfront, creating a positive cash conversion cycle.

If you're building a business, there's a straightforward arbitrage opportunity: find established direct-to-consumer brands that aren't selling on Amazon, then either resell their products on Amazon or create a competing product and bid on their branded keywords. The business model is straightforward but risky. When the primary brand finally enters Amazon and optimizes their presence, your competitive advantage disappears. Attribution has become significantly more complicated since the iOS privacy changes. Facebook Ads Manager used to provide incredibly accurate data, but now relies on predictive sales metrics based on unclear methodologies. The most accurate approach today involves multiple tools working together. Server-to-server tracking through Elevar captures maximum available data, then platforms like NorthBeam, Triple Whale, Rockerbox, and Measured provide different attribution models. None will be perfectly accurate, and numbers between Google Analytics and Shopify will always vary slightly.

Rather than relying on any single attribution source, use these tools as data points to inform decisions rather than determine them outright. UTM parameters remain valuable because they can only underattribute sales, never overattribute them. If a UTM shows five sales, you conservatively know at minimum those five came from that source, though you may have gotten more from people who clicked the ad and later searched directly for the brand.

For audience analysis, look at conversion rates and bounce rates across different audience segments within and across channels. Within Facebook, compare performance between lookalike audiences, interest-based audiences, and specific interest targeting. A DIY-focused audience might have different bounce rates than a beverage-focused audience. This level of segmentation helps you understand which audiences actually care about your product and spend more efficiently going forward.

First order profitability should be your target if you don't understand your lifetime value. Calculate your selling price minus COGS, shipping, and freight. That's your available marketing budget for first order break-even. A good benchmark from earlier DTC advice suggests spending up to your lifetime value on acquisition, but if you're cash constrained or don't know your LTV, aim for first order profitability. Once you understand your economics and conversion rates, you can calculate exactly how hard you need to work to hit your targets.

When you're under two to three million dollars annually, focus exclusively on one channel rather than spreading yourself across Facebook, Google, Pinterest, Snap, and TikTok simultaneously. Nobody is genuinely excellent at both Facebook and Google simultaneously. Your budget and attention are too thin to properly test, analyze, and optimize across multiple channels. Once you reach five to ten million, you can add channel diversification. The platforms learn and optimize better when you give them substantial budget to work with, so concentrating spend is essential for their algorithms to function effectively.

Elections impact advertising significantly, though not necessarily positively for most brands. Grove Collaborative represents an interesting case study in public market challenges for direct-to-consumer brands. They IPO'd in June at a one point five billion dollar valuation but are now worth approximately two hundred fifty million dollars, having lost eighty percent of their value in five months. Their revenue growth was impressive historically, growing from thirty-two million in two thousand seventeen to three hundred eighty-four million in two thousand twenty-one, a twelve-time increase. However, they're now experiencing declining revenue, dropping from four hundred million to an anticipated three hundred million this year while losing one hundred million dollars in cash.

Their business model involves selling both third-party brands like Method and their own privately labeled Grove brands at roughly equal splits. Their gross margin is forty-nine percent, which isn't particularly strong for direct-to-consumer. Seventy-five percent of revenue comes from organic traffic rather than paid advertising, so profitability should theoretically be stronger. Yet they spend twenty-two point five percent of revenue on marketing and twenty-five percent on fulfillment, compared to an AOV of fifty-six dollars. This means their fulfillment percentage exceeds the recommended twenty percent threshold.

Despite claiming fantastic five-year retention at forty percent of customers, this metric seems disconnected from typical brand performance where even one-year retention is usually far lower. They launch one hundred sixty SKUs annually, essentially one every two days, and spend five percent of revenue on research and development, which seems high for this business model. Their site navigation is poor, focusing heavily on sustainability messaging when market testing shows sustainability ranks third or fourth in purchase drivers, not first. Low AOV items dominate their featured section at just four dollars and eighty cents.

The public markets are currently punishing direct-to-consumer brands broadly. Manscaped pulled their IPO attempt, and most publicly traded DTC companies are struggling. Large retailers like Walmart have tried entering direct-to-consumer through acquisitions like Jet and Bonobos without achieving competency in the space. Traditional CPG companies like P&G don't naturally understand DTC operations since their entire infrastructure is built around selling to Target and Walmart rather than directly fulfilling individual consumer orders. A P&G executive quoted earlier mentioned that even when P&G attempted a less-than-one-pound package direct shipment, fulfillment costs hit thirty dollars per unit, making the economics impossible. This infrastructure mismatch is why large companies struggle with direct-to-consumer despite acquiring successful brands.
