# Teams That Win

When you hire people for teams that win, they're hired in their zone of genius, meaning right in the middle of what they're passionate about, what they want to learn about, what they're interested in, and what they're good at. It doesn't mean they're the best when they get hired, but they have that drive to want to be the best.

These people have a certain level of hustle and they like to work hard. They're not leaving the office at five o'clock. They're usually there until six thirty or seven. But it's not even just about having a certain level of hustle. It's that they have this desire to want to be the best.

Teams that win understand that not everybody can get first place and some people are going to fall short. They just know that they don't want to be the ones that suck. There's so many environments where everybody just gets participation trophies and everybody should win, but that's just not how the world works.

When bad clients or bad experiences happen, teams that win don't see it as the worst thing in the world. Instead, they see it as a test of the system's readiness. How well can we react as a team? It's also an opportunity to get better or strengthen a muscle that's clearly weak.

These people are generally curious to see what can work. They're not defined by absolute thinking. It's not like, that's not going to work, we're not going to do that. It's more so like, okay, well, let's test it. Let's do it. Let's see what happens.

For more junior employees especially, winning teams have respect for the technologies being used. A new team member will go spend the weekend learning about the tools the company uses so they can better understand them in conversations and contexts rather than pushing to change systems immediately.

Communication is absolutely critical for teams that win. When you're not all communicating really well, everything falls apart. If the marketing side doesn't communicate with the customer service side or the operations side, you drop balls. You run out of inventory. You don't launch a new product on time.

Creativity is the backbone of winning teams. There's a bias towards action. If you can do something or not do something and you're not sure which one is right, go do something. There are no sacred cows. One major success came from a completely random creative idea. A girl on the team was going on a date in Napa drinking rosé on a Tuesday, and that inspired a rose-scented deodorant. That product was launched on the first day of summer, which happened to be Rose Day. Every news publication picked it up at once, and it sold out in ten minutes.

Team camaraderie is essential. When there's a problem, it's not that individual person's problem. It's the business's problem and it's everyone's problem because you're all in this together. Nobody is saying this is your problem. You drop the ball here. It's like, the business is out of stock on this scent. The business has this problem. That's my problem as much as it is yours.

The number one thing to avoid is people without enthusiasm. You're excited about business. You're excited about growing things. This is a small team. If you think your job is customer service because you're a customer service associate, that's crazy. There's very few of you. You need to be excited about the entire business.

The other thing to avoid is toxic employees. You need people who can talk directly to anybody on the team. You don't need to go to your manager if you can solve the issue directly. Go yell at them. Get in their face. Why are you telling me? Do you directly communicate with your colleagues about issues instead of bringing management into every problem?

# Amazon Prime Day and E-Commerce Trends

Prime Day in October is far smaller than the July Prime Day. It's like a regular Monday in terms of Amazon's sales. Mondays are actually Amazon's best day overall, which is surprising. The hypothesis is that people are back at work and don't want to actually be doing any work, so they're on Amazon shopping.

On average, brands see two to four times their revenue on Prime Day compared to an ordinary day. The October Prime Day was fifty percent bigger than the previous October Prime Day, which is surprising given macroeconomic conditions. The top categories were apparel, beauty, home, and toys. Apparel was number one, which is crazy since Amazon fashion has blown up out of nowhere.

Crest Whitestrips is the number one or number two product every Black Friday and Amazon Prime Day. At P&G, they give out a Nobel Prize for innovation that they've only given out eight times in their hundred-year history. The last time they gave it out was to the guy who invented Crest Whitestrips because it revolutionized teeth whitening, which is genuine innovation.

It's surprising that Target, Walmart, Kroger, Whole Foods, and Costco don't have their own annual shopping days like Prime Day. They could do it in person and online. If it was Costco, they could have food samples every aisle on that day. The hard part is that Amazon is the owner of the merchandise and can set sale prices through sellers. Walmart owns the merchandise, so they'd take the loss. But they could go to their biggest suppliers and say they're launching Walmart day and get them to discount by thirty percent, and they'll be featured in Action Alley, where products do really well in stores.

# Amazon Aggregators and the Roll-Up Graveyard

Roll-ups in Amazon were the hottest thing ever in 2020 and 2021. Everyone wanted to launch a holding company and acquire some brands. The model was buying a business at four times EBITDA and then going public at fifteen to twenty-five times EBITDA to arbitrage the valuation multiple. Smart money like Apollo and Silver Lake invested billions of dollars behind these companies.

Now it looks like it was a huge mistake. Thrasio is on the verge of going bankrupt after raising three point four billion between debt and equity. The last time they raised money, they were valued at between five and ten billion dollars. Perch raised nine hundred million and is looking for an exit. Apollo, their backer, wants someone to please buy this business because it's going nowhere. Bentiago Group raised three hundred twenty-five million and just went bankrupt.

The cardinal sin these aggregators made was buying businesses at the top of the market in 2021. They were overpaying for multiple reasons. The multiples were higher then. Interest rates were zero, so money was free. The brands already had the tailwinds of COVID behind them, and now they have the headwinds of a much harder economy.

Another cardinal sin was assuming there would be synergies between the different brands they acquired. They thought they could combine operations and have one person run Facebook and Amazon ads for all their brands. But the reality is that's not possible. Those are very different skill sets.

The founders who built these businesses created them with Excel models thinking they could acquire three brands and simplify supply chain, which is impossible. You can't have a rug company, a shoe company, and something else all with simplified supply chain. You need more bodies per brand for it to actually do well.

Why these businesses aren't working is that the number one thing founders bring to the table that these guys don't have is creativity and the ability to set themselves apart with brands. A lot of these brands are tier two or tier three type of brands. Maybe they've appeared on Shark Tank or had a Cool Pop or were good Kickstarter businesses at one point, but now they're just playing the same game of Facebook ads and a landing page and an offer. There's no excitement or creativity there.

Generally, these aggregators buy businesses that are half Amazon and half direct-to-consumer, with some primarily Amazon and trying to grow direct to consumer, while others are primarily direct to consumer and added Amazon as a channel.

# Landing Page Optimization

Most people build landing pages for three reasons. One is to educate somebody on a weird, expensive, out of the ordinary product. In that case, they use a listicle, collections page, or click-through page. The second reason is to sell somebody right away, which usually involves a hero product or a bundle. The third is to collect first-party data like email or phone number for later follow-up.

Every page should answer five main questions. What is it? How does it work? Why does it exist? How does it compare? And then what's called the how the fuck questions: How the fuck is this so expensive? How the fuck is this going to get to my door? How the fuck can you justify the price? How the fuck is this going to do what it claims?

The ten most common misses on landing pages are lack of high quality visuals, unclear pricing and offerings, poor CTAs that don't catch your eye or aren't fun enough, lacking benefit-driven content, not showing benefits in context, not highlighting positive reviews and social proof, lacking special offers with strikeouts and contrasting colors, having copy that's too complicated, wasting white space, requiring too many clicks, and not clearly differentiating from competitors.

High quality visuals are essential. Good photography, if you're selling a supplement, maybe infographics. If you're selling apparel, you have good lifestyle imagery or actual customers wearing the product. CTAs should catch your eye visually, not be a massive box with tiny text or vice versa. Fun CTAs are better than just "learn more" every two sections. Something like "pick my color," "choose my fabric," or "get fifteen percent off" is more enticing.

Benefit-driven content is crucial. When thinking about copy, imagine presenting it to a panel of judges: your mom, somebody who's three drinks in and slurring their words, and a third grader. If those three people can understand the copy, then it's good copy.

Above the fold is oceanfront real estate, and wasting space there is shooting yourself in the foot. Every time you have to click between a page, you're losing the opportunity to sell. One of the best landing pages is Flouse, a flossing brand. They made their product details page their landing page because it converted so well and it hits all these points very well.

The biggest miss is probably the benefits versus value props. When you're drinking your own Kool-Aid, you think about clinical strength and time release supplements, but that makes no sense to the end consumer. You need to think of a conveyor belt of a value prop getting filtered through somebody who doesn't care about your product and give them a benefit that actually matters. So instead of it's got this ingredient, it's your hair is actually going to grow stronger.

Social proof and reviews are another big miss. You're selling a supplement or a bed frame and you have nothing about whether it works or how it feels. Copy gets really boring because the people making these pages have been drinking the Kool-Aid for so long.

Delivery times are underestimated. Amazon has trained people that fifty percent of packages now arrive same day or within one day. A lot of brands aren't putting in when to expect the package. If you're shipping from China and the package will take fifteen days, you probably don't want to advertise that. But if it's going to arrive in two to five business days, you definitely want to add that information.

The shipping names in checkout can be really effective. Instead of just calling it standard shipping, you could call it "Easy Breezy Shipping (ships within 24 hours, delivers in 3-5 days, $7.99)" and the paid option could be "VIP Voyage" (ships within 12 hours, arrives in 1-2 days). Allbirds does something similar with "Fast," "Fast-ish," and "Fastest," which is effective.

# Premium Brands and Walmart

Walmart is the most unique of all retailers. Amazon and Costco are utilities like plumbing going to your house, and there's no brand denigration if you're in those stores. There's a famous case of Costco asking P&G for SK-2, their luxury beauty brand that sells at a countertop at Bloomingdale's. P&G said no. Costco said okay, then we're getting rid of all Gillette products. P&G said okay, you can have SK-2 and we'll deliver it tomorrow.

Walmart is really difficult for premium brands for two reasons. One is that premium brands don't turn well at Walmart from a sales perspective, not from a brand perception perspective. It's just a very different customer. Walmart customers are generally more price conscious than Target or Costco customers.

The reality though is that the channel is too large to ignore when you get to a certain size. If you're not going to do it and you sell your business at some point, there's meat on the bone for the buyer and they're going to go to Walmart. Method famously would never sell at Walmart because they thought it hurt their brand. SC Johnson bought the company and now it sells at Walmart.

At some point, the size of Walmart is just too big to not be there, even if it hurts your brand. There are two thousand Target stores and four thousand four hundred Walmarts in the United States. Walmart is just too big to ignore at a certain level. If you were a cooking brand, you'd probably get into Target and Costco before Walmart. But at some point, if you don't do it, whoever buys your company will take it to Walmart and squeeze that lemon for three years and get a lot of juice out of it.

# Direct-to-Consumer and Amazon Strategy

When launching on Amazon, you want to have innovation on your site first. New scents, new products should come to your website first before hitting Amazon. This is good for a few reasons. Let's say you're launching a face wash and your face cream is already on Amazon. You want customer feedback that isn't twelve one-star reviews on Amazon. You want direct communication with customers who can tell you they don't like it because it dries their skin or smells off.

You want to understand your repeat purchase rate and have enough consumers you can communicate with regularly before launching on Amazon. You're not trying to understand who your customer is or trying to fix problems in your business once you're on Amazon. You should have solved those issues first.

One strategy is to put your best flavors or products on Amazon and keep all the other products on your site to bring people there. You can have better competing deals for new customers only. For example, if someone wants to buy a single case of water, they'd go to Amazon because they don't have to pay for shipping. If they want to buy three cases, it's a better deal on your site. If they want to subscribe, it's a better deal on your site. You can create push and pull where there's advantages depending on what type of customer you are and what they're trying to buy.

# Buy with Prime

Buy with Prime integration on Amazon for DTC brands has been tested with some conversion lift. There are definitely limitations though. You can't subscribe through Buy with Prime on some brands. You can't get multiple items in the cart at the same time. It's a very single SKU order, which sucks if you're normally selling three items. You can now buy multiple products with Buy with Prime, but it's still early on and probably break even right now.

Two years from now when customers have gotten used to it and understand that Buy with Prime means faster shipping without putting in information, it'll be a lot easier. Just like with Shop Pay, everyone was confused at first, and now people understand it. If Amazon spends tens of millions of dollars advertising the Buy with Prime logo like Affirm did with their logo, it'll have the same effect. Early birds will get some benefit, but two years from now, everyone will have it.
