# Incrementality Testing and Modern Marketing Measurement

Think of randomized control trials in healthcare as the simplest comparison point for incrementality testing. When rolling out a new drug, you give one group of people a placebo and another statistically indistinguishable group the actual drug, then observe the difference in behavior between the two groups to validate efficacy. That's incrementality testing at its core—you have a counterfactual to understand what would have happened anyway in the absence of the intervention, whether that's search, video, or YouTube. This fundamental approach establishes causation rather than just correlation.

## The Problem with Attribution

Most ad platforms over-report conversions and get more credit than they deserve. When you add up all conversions attributed to each paid channel and realize the sum exceeds your actual conversions, you've discovered the attribution problem. Sophisticated marketers are moving away from attribution models and toward incrementality testing to maximize growth and understand true efficiency.

## Three Types of Incrementality Testing

Geo-experiments, which house primarily uses, have significant advantages. They require no user-level data or personally identifiable information—just sales and revenue by day by geography, which any e-commerce brand already has. This approach is privacy-durable and resilient to privacy changes. You can test across any channel, online or offline, using the exact same standardized methodology. While historically geo-testing was noisier than user-level testing and required more time and larger control groups, advancements in synthetic control methodology have squeezed more precision and power out of these tests, making adoption more practical.

User-level studies represent the second type, run by Facebook and Google through their conversion lift products. You can ask Meta or Google to set these up on your behalf. However, you can't run user-level tests on your own because in prospecting and acquisition, you don't know who these unknown users are. Google and Meta need their universe to allocate users cleanly to treatment versus control. Privacy changes have made this harder for them—for example, Google's conversion lift only fully works on Android and models iOS because they can't allocate users cleanly on that platform.

The third type involves natural experiments, where you shut something off or turn something on you haven't been running. You observe the business against a forecast of expected performance. This is less precise because there's no true counterfactual, making it more correlational than causal, but it's the best you can do with channels like influencers that can't be geo-segmented.

## When to Use Each Approach

If you're only spending on Google and Meta and you're small enough that organic demand versus paid demand isn't fuzzy, Meta and Google conversion lift studies might get you most of the way there initially. However, once you start expanding outside those two channels to Pinterest, Reddit, or Snap—which don't have comparable conversion lift tools—or once you need to measure impact across retail and Amazon, you need geo-testing. No matter your size, if Amazon and retail represent meaningful percentages of your business, you probably need geo-testing because you can't feed Amazon data back to Meta to power conversion lift.

## The Difference Between Correlation and Causation

Correlation is the classic brand search example: you spend on brand search, a user comes in and purchases. But was that correlational—did the ad just happen to hit someone coming anyway—or causal, actually influencing someone who wasn't going to purchase to make that purchase? When you have loyal customers like someone with ten Sonos products, if you show them an ad for new headphones they were going to buy anyway, that's a correlational conversion. The goal is spending money on causal conversions and moving away from just looking at correlational ones because there's probably significant waste there. Sometimes you can use incrementality to add a multiplier rather than just apply a discount—YouTube and OTT are obvious examples where brands are understating impact when they should actually be adding a multiplier based on true incrementality.

## Key Experiments and Insights

Brand search and affiliate tend to look like the best channels under last-click attribution but show no impact under incrementality testing. However, testing these channels is difficult—affiliate sites like Honey make it hard to test because they refresh the page immediately. With geo-testing, you can shut down a channel for a month or two to get clean insights.

At Netflix, testing broad targeting versus manual demographic and interest targeting on Meta showed that letting Meta algorithmically find users who would sign up crushed manual targeting. Rolling this out globally meant moving away from interest-based audiences into broad targeting, which also saved tremendous resources.

At Sonos, one major finding involved understanding where people actually buy. Search, even when showing no incremental lift in revenue, was share-shifting users from retail into direct-to-consumer with better margins. For profit optimization, this mattered significantly. The dynamics of channel performance changed when measuring across both retail and direct-to-consumer rather than just click-based attribution.

## The Delayed Lift Phenomenon

Looking back at Q4 experiments, delayed lift exceeded immediate lift in 73% of experiments tested. The immediate lift was measured during the test period, but when using the post-treatment window feature—where you stop the test and revert to business as usual while observing behavior of treatment markets—you often see additional lift two to three weeks later. For higher price point, higher AOV products, this makes sense. People need more time to convert.

Video campaigns drove 286% more delayed lift than search. YouTube and OTT generated the most delayed lift, while Meta reach was in the middle and search was the least. This timing matters significantly for Q4 planning. Buyers should consider taking efficiency targets down slightly right now in September to fill the funnel, with the understanding that they'll reap benefits come November when people are actually in market buying. If you can spend at slightly lower efficiency now, you'll have more users aware of your product for the peak selling season.

## Upper Funnel and Optimization Goals

There's always a question about the right optimization goal at different spend levels. Some brands think once they reach a million-dollar monthly spend on Meta, they should move to add-to-cart campaigns. However, the right goal depends on your specific business. If your goal is truly to maximize reach and fill the funnel, add-to-cart might not go high enough up the funnel. You can't just set and forget with upper funnel campaigns—you need to test the right way to buy for your specific business.

## The Future of Brand Marketing

Many people talk about brand marketing versus performance marketing as if they're completely separate, but building a brand doesn't require expensive TV or billboard campaigns anymore. The channels with the most scale—TikTok, Meta, YouTube—are where you can build a brand too. Brands like Water Boy have built eight-figure businesses almost entirely through organic content on platforms like TikTok and TikTok Shop.

For larger brands spending across distributors, the approach is changing. Companies like Pernod Ricard are working with incrementality partners rather than spending six months to a year waiting for MMM results. They upload distributor sales data and get results in weeks to months. CFOs care about bottom-line impact, not awareness metrics, so the future of brand marketing likely involves observing leading indicators like site visits in real time while running longer experiments with post-treatment windows to understand how those indicators ultimately drive sales.

## The Multiplier Effect Across Channels

A consistent finding across many brands is that for every dollar spent on online advertising, they see three dollars in incremental revenue across all other channels—Amazon, retail, marketplaces. This is particularly common with consumable products. However, consumer behavior is hard to influence; people will buy where they want to buy. A surprising insight is that view-based channels like YouTube and OTT drive more incremental lift in Amazon and retail compared to Meta or search. Day-to-day reporting makes YouTube and connected TV look like they can't work, but they might actually be driving higher proportions of Amazon sales that aren't visible in attribution.

## Influencer and Creator Content

Testing around influencer content has shown promising results, whereas affiliate testing shows very mixed results. The difficulty of testing influencer content shouldn't prevent you from trying—you can use burst methods where you go out with different types of branded content or seeding plays and run time-series tests to observe what's happening.

## Understanding Diminishing Returns

Diminishing returns is about marginality, not average CPA. The question isn't your average cost per acquisition but your marginal CPA—where your next dollar is best spent. A three-cell test with control, BAU plus 25%, and BAU plus 50% shows how incremental revenue or new customers scale with different spend levels. Fan Duel ran this type of test for YouTube going into March Madness and discovered that between medium and high spend levels, they were fully maxed out with no additional users, just additional cost. This calibration ensures you're not overspending relative to what you're getting back.

The key to all this testing is not letting perfect be the enemy of good. Even when you can't design a perfect experiment, implementing what you can measure helps you make significantly better decisions than relying on flawed attribution models alone.
